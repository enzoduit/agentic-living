# Chapter 1: On What Becomes Possible

---

Let me start with the thing that will be hardest for you to believe.

Early 2026. I had a brand new website — blank slate, no visitors, no reputation, no content, no history on the internet whatsoever. I gave an agent a single objective: get this website to the top of AI-powered search results for a specific topic.

I did not write a single word myself. I did not make a single decision about what the content should say. I did not change a single setting, pick a single keyword, or review a single draft.

I gave the agent the goal, described what a good result would look like, and let it work.

Six days later, the website ranked first on Perplexity — one of the leading AI-powered search engines, where millions of people now go to find answers — for the target topic.

While this was happening, I was in Patagonia. Running.

---

I want to be precise about what happened, because the precision is what makes it credible.

The agent chose what to write about. It analysed what questions were actually being asked in that topic area, identified where the gaps were, decided which angles would produce results. It wrote the content — not templated filler, but structured, specific pages that answered real questions. It handled the technical requirements: the formatting, the structure, the signals that AI search systems use to evaluate quality. It published everything. It monitored what was working and kept going. No brief for each piece. No approval step. No human in the loop between the goal and the outcome.

One clarification that matters here, before we go further. When I say the agent chose what to write — that is true. But it did not choose freely.

I set the topic. I defined the context: what information the agent had access to, what it could draw on as the factual basis for everything it produced. That context was real — not generated, not interpolated, not approximated. Actual knowledge, actual expertise I had accumulated about the subject. And within that context, one constraint was absolute: use only what is real. No invented claims. No manufactured statistics. No opinions I had not actually formed, presented as if they were mine.

The agent had autonomy inside a defined space. The space itself — the goal, the context, the constraints — was entirely my judgment.

This is not a minor technical footnote. It is the difference between delegation and abdication. At Level 1, you are not releasing the steering wheel to something that drives wherever it wants. You are defining the road. What travels along that road, and how fast, and with what precision — that is where the agent operates. What the road is, where it goes, and what the guardrails are: that remains yours.

This is how it has to work — not just ethically, but because the output has your name on it. The constraint is what makes the output yours. The constraint is what allows you to stand behind it.

This is not a story about a clever tool. It is a story about what happens when "delegating a task" and "doing it yourself" stop being meaningfully different things — when a system can hold a goal inside the space you have defined, make the decisions required to pursue it, and produce results that would have taken a team of specialists weeks to produce by conventional means.

If you are at Level 0 reading this, that description probably triggered one of two reactions. Either disbelief — *that can't really work like that* — or a quieter, less comfortable feeling: the sense of watching something move very fast while you are standing still. Both reactions are legitimate. Neither one changes what happened.

---

Here is what I have noticed when I describe this to people who have not yet experienced Level 1. There is always some version of the same response. It sounds like this: *"Yes, but that's a specific kind of task. It doesn't apply to what I do."*

I have heard this from lawyers, doctors, founders, artists, teachers, consultants, NGO directors, architects, journalists, and university professors. Every field has its version of the same objection. The specific task changes. The structure of the reasoning stays identical.

What the objection is actually saying — if you listen beneath the words — is this: *I can imagine AI doing that particular thing, but I cannot imagine it doing the thing that matters to me.*

That is a completely rational response. If you have not seen it work in your domain, on your kind of problem, producing your kind of output, you have no basis for believing it can. The demo videos do not count. Someone else's story does not count. The only thing that counts is watching it do something for you — something real, in a context you care about, producing a result you would have had to produce yourself.

The purpose of this book is to give you that experience.

Not by explaining it more convincingly. By building it, one step at a time, in your own context.

---

But before we get there — and I am going to ask you to do something at the end of this chapter that will take fifteen minutes and will matter more than you expect — I want to make the real claim clearly. Because the story about the website is not the point. The website is just a container. The point is what the story reveals about what has changed.

What changed is not that AI can write content. That has been true for years and mostly it produces content that nobody should read. What changed is the layer above the content — the layer of judgment. Deciding what to write. Deciding who it is for. Deciding what matters and what does not. Deciding when something is good enough and when it needs another pass.

That layer — the judgment layer — was, until recently, the part that required a human. The execution sat below it and required tools and time. Humans supplied the judgment; the tools supplied the throughput.

What is happening now, and what the Perplexity story illustrates, is that the judgment layer is also being automated. Not perfectly. Not in every domain. Not without careful goal-setting from a human. But increasingly, and in a growing number of contexts, a system can be given a direction and find its own way there — including making the choices along the way that used to require human intelligence.

That is the structural shift. Everything else — the lifestyle stories, the experiments, the tools and workflows and frameworks — is downstream of that one fact.

One measure captures the pace of this shift. METR — an independent AI research organisation — tracks what they call the “time horizon”: how long an agent can work reliably on a complex task before needing human input.

| Year | Reliable autonomous operation |
|------|-------------------------------|
| 2019 | ~1 minute |
| 2022 | ~8 minutes |
| 2025 | ~1 hour |
| 2026 | ~4–6 hours |
| Soon | Days / weeks |

*Source: METR Time Horizon 1.1, January 2026. Capability doubling approximately every 7 months.*

An agent that works reliably for six hours is qualitatively different from one that needed checking every eight minutes. What required constant supervision becomes something you can hand off completely. And what you can hand off completely is not where your attention should go.

---

And here is the implication that most people resist when they first encounter it:

If the judgment layer can increasingly be held by something else, what is left for you?

Not which jobs will be automated. Not which tasks will disappear. What is the right kind of work for a human being — given that the execution, and much of the decision-making within the execution, can now be handled?

I spent the last year and a half running an experiment to answer that question. Not in theory. In practice, across four countries, two ultramarathons, a server crash fixed from a beach, and a year's worth of professional work done from a phone.

The short answer I arrived at is this: setting the goal and taking responsibility for the outcome. Those two things — the beginning and the end of any real work — remain irreducibly human. Everything between them is, in principle, not where your attention should go.

For most people in knowledge work, "everything between those two things" describes the majority of their working day.

What gets returned to you, when that majority is handled by something else, is not just hours. It is a different quality of presence with the work that actually matters — the capacity to think more clearly, to go further on the problems that deserve depth, and if you choose it, a different kind of life running underneath.

That is what Level 1 actually looks like. Not a shortcut. A structural shift in what your attention is for.

## Your First Step

Before you read the next chapter, stop here.

Open a conversation with any AI — Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, whatever you have access to. It does not matter which one. Type this exact prompt:

> *"I want to write a press release dated 2035. It announces something I built or created — a product, a service, a system, a change — that transformed how [your field / your work / your life] operates. Don't write it yet. Interview me first. Ask me one question at a time until you have enough to write something real."*

Then answer honestly. Do not filter yourself. Do not evaluate whether what you're describing is realistic. Do not try to sound impressive. Just answer the questions as if you are describing something that already exists.

You do not need to know how to build what you describe. The point of this exercise is not the press release. The point is the questions the AI will ask you — because they will be questions you have not been asked before, and your answers will surprise you.

When you are done, read what it produced. Not to critique it. Just to feel it.

Something in you will respond. Pay attention to that.

Keep the press release. We will come back to it.

→ *If you are working within a company or organisation rather than building your own product:* your press release does not need to be about a startup. Imagine it is 2035 and you are in your current role — or the version of it you would be proud to describe. Write the announcement of what you accomplished. What changed in how your work gets done? What did you deliver that was not possible before? What became possible in your position that nobody had managed before you? Let the AI interview you the same way. Your answers will be just as surprising.
