## On the Night in the Mountains

There are moments when you understand something so completely that your body reacts before your mind has time to process it.

I remember one of those nights. I was at one of my favourite places in the Austrian mountains, Zell am See — I remember it clearly today, it was January 2025 — and I had been reading a research paper on my reMarkable. The ReAct framework. Reasoning and Action. A paper describing how an AI system could reason through a problem, take an action, observe what happened, and reason again — in a loop, autonomously, indefinitely.

I was completely shaking.

My heart rate was way beyond normal — and I do track it with my Garmin 24/7. The kind of heart rate you get before a race, or in a moment of real fear. I needed to go outside. Not for air — I needed to move, because the thoughts spinning up in my head were too fast for a sitting body.

It was a beautiful night with the moon shining bright — but freaking cold. Ten, fifteen degrees below zero. I just did not notice it, because I was internally on fire. I walked and talked to myself out loud, the way you do when you are trying to understand something by hearing yourself say it. This paper — it described a system that could replicate whatever a human could do. Not a specific task. Not a narrow skill. The general loop: observe, reason, act. The loop that is the foundation of almost everything we call knowledge work (which is, by the way, the greatest part of all work in developed economies).

If that loop could be automated, what exactly was left?

I had no answer that night. I walked until the cold finally reached me, then went inside. I did not sleep well.

What I did not know was that within a year, everything I had imagined possible on that walk would be real. Not theoretically possible — actually running, on actual infrastructure, producing actual results. And I would be the person running it, from my phone, in the mountains of Patagonia. (Note: I am not a developer. This is available to everyone with basic technical understanding — which makes it even more absurd.)

This essay is the account of how that happened, what it means, and what it suggests about the life that becomes possible when you follow the logic of that night to its conclusion.

## On the Proof That Changed the Question

Before I explain how we got here, let me give you the clearest example of where "here" actually is. Because without a concrete proof, this kind of essay becomes philosophy, and philosophy without evidence is just comfort.

Early 2026. I had a brand new website with no visitors, no history, and no reputation — a blank slate on the internet. I gave an agent a single objective: get this website to the top of AI-powered search results for a specific topic. I did not write a single word myself. I did not change a single setting. I gave the agent the goal, described what a good result would look like, and let it work.

Six days later, it was ranked first on Perplexity — one of the leading AI search engines, where millions of people go to find answers.

My friends got used to the messages that came after moments like this. *"This is absolutely crazy."* Or: *"I can't believe it."* Those were the reactions I kept sending — not because I was performing surprise, but because every now and then something happened that really was too good to be true. You build a system, you run an experiment, you half-expect it to fail in some interesting way that teaches you something. And instead it just works, completely, and you sit there trying to understand what that means.

What it meant, in that case, was this: if an agent can take a blank website to the top of AI search without a human touching it, then the question is no longer *can this be done?* The question becomes: *what else can be done?* And more importantly: *what kind of life does that make possible?*

The work side, the professional side — that is one part of the answer, and I will get to it. But the more interesting part, the part nobody expected, is what it meant for how I could live. While the agent was doing this work, I was running ultramarathons in Patagonia. Staying in El Chaltén for two weeks instead of two days. Fixing a server crash from a beach in Spain. Having coffee in Tilcara in the Argentine highlands while three companies kept running. The professional output did not shrink. The life expanded.

## On Timeless Patterns and Why Bestsellers Expire

I will provide many concrete examples — almost like a diary of my agents. But before I do, I want to step back from the specifics entirely — because if these examples are only interesting as technology stories, they are not actually that interesting. Technology stories expire. What I want to explain is why I think what is happening underneath these examples will not expire, and why that matters more than any individual experiment.

To do that, I want to borrow from someone who was not thinking about AI at all.

Seneca wrote to his friend Lucilius: "It matters not how many books you have, but how good they are." He was talking about a library — the difference between accumulating scrolls and actually reading the ones worth reading. But the principle has a broader application. It matters not how many new things happen, but how many of them reveal something that was always true.

Most of today's technology writing will be irrelevant in a couple of years. Not because the facts will be wrong, but because it is too close to the surface. It describes what is happening without asking what it means. It names the tool without examining the pattern the tool is exposing.

The pattern underneath what I have been living through is not new. It is ancient. Seneca knew it. The Stoics built their entire philosophy around it. And a more recent example: Tim Ferriss understood it too — and excited millions with his interpretation of it in *The Four-Hour Work Week*. Different era, different tools, same underlying truth.

The pattern is this: almost all human suffering that is not physical comes from misallocation of attention. We attend to things that do not deserve attention. We fail to attend to things that do. We fill the hours of our lives with activity that produces the sensation of progress without the substance of it. And then we wonder why the hours feel both busy and empty.

What is happening with AI agents right now is not — at its core — a technology story. It is a story about attention. About what becomes possible when the things that were stealing your attention are handled by something else, and you get to choose what to do with what is returned.

Think about what that attention was actually spent on, in practice. Early in my career as a technology consultant, a significant part of every week went to creating presentations — not thinking about what the slides should say, but formatting them. Choosing fonts. Aligning boxes. Making things look consistent across fifty pages. None of that required judgment. All of it required time. Or consider the hours spent clicking through generic software — tools that were not actually built for what you needed to accomplish, so you spent the first twenty minutes of every session figuring out how to make it work for your specific situation. These are not edge cases. They are the normal texture of professional life for most people.

That texture is what is being replaced. And what is being returned in its place is something rarer: time that belongs to you.

That is the timeless pattern. The technology is just the mechanism by which it is becoming possible to live it.

And the first question worth asking is not just: how do we reclaim this time? But: how do we reinvest it?

*A note on what came before the experiment.*

Before March 2026 — before any agent was running, before any server was configured, before the first run documentary was assembled and deployed without me touching a computer — I had been writing. Not about AI as technology. About AI as a philosophical problem.

I had been asking: what would a thinker who understood the long arc of human history say about this moment? Not a technologist. Not a venture capitalist. Someone who had already thought carefully about attention, about time, about what it means to spend a life well.

I landed on Seneca. And I tried, in a series of letters I wrote in his voice, to articulate what the introduction of intelligent instruments into human life actually meant — before I had lived it. I called the series *Epistulae de Instrumentis Sapientibus*. Letters on Intelligent Instruments.

One passage, from the first letter, stayed with me through everything that followed:

> *"Wisdom lies not in the instrument itself but in its employment. Consider closely what virtues or vices you might multiply in your haste toward autonomy. Let your purpose not be concealed beneath novelty or ambition; seek clarity above all."*

And another, from the second:

> *"Begin every venture not by contemplating the instruments at hand, but by discerning the best and most virtuous manner to accomplish it."*

These were not predictions. They were questions. What followed — in Argentina, in Bolivia, in El Chaltén and Ushuaia and Buenos Aires — was the attempt to answer them.

Let us use this perspective — the search for timeless patterns beneath fast-moving surfaces, not just the stories themselves — to deconstruct my personal experiments on living agentically, in the wilderness of Argentina, Chile, and Bolivia.

## On How It Actually Began

The beginning was more practical than philosophical. 

Beginning of March 2026, I was set to fly to the United States and then to Argentina for an ultramarathon. OpenClaw — the software that would become the infrastructure of the whole experiment — had just come out. OpenClaw, built by Austrian entrepreneur Peter Steinberger, is in simple terms a personal AI operating system: it runs on a server, connects your AI assistant to your real life — your calendar, your files, your messages, your tools — and lets it work for you continuously, not just when you open an app. I was thinking of running it locally, on my Mac Mini at home. Then I had a very simple thought: whenever the Mac Mini has a problem, what do I do? I will be in Patagonia. There is no fixing it from there.

So I decided to set it up in the cloud instead.

That was the best decision I made in the entire experiment. Not because it was clever or strategic — it was just practical. But the consequence of that decision was that I had the agent available all the time, on any device, on any connection, from any location on earth. The agent was not somewhere. It was everywhere.

It proved its worth sooner than expected. A week later, a server crashed. Had I chosen the Mac Mini path, that would have been the end — no access, no agent, no experiment. Because the system lived in the cloud, I fixed it from my phone, in under an hour, while standing somewhere I had no business doing infrastructure work. Everything that followed was only possible because of that one unglamorous decision made at the beginning.

It was during this trip that I first heard something from Peter Steinberger that stuck with me. He shared that he had realised the true power of what he had built during a trip to northern Africa. The internet connection was too weak to code — barely enough to send a WhatsApp message. And yet the system still worked. The reason is simple: think of it the way you would send a message to a friend via WhatsApp. The message leaves your phone in an instant. Everything that happens next — the processing, the thinking, the work — happens on a server somewhere else. Your phone is just the trigger. All you need is enough bandwidth to get that message out. What the mind had set in motion did not stop just because the connection was thin.

That made it attractive for me to follow the same path when I was in the Patagonian wilderness or later in the Puna in the Andes — places where the internet is unreliable and the connection is thin, but my focus is extreme. If the system could run on the bandwidth of a voice message, the system could run from anywhere.

Steinberger has a phrase I keep coming back to: *"Only imagination is holding us back."* Because there is way more feasible than humans currently can imagine. My goal is to show you that literally everything is possible — and it is up to you, your imagination, and your focus. That night in the Austrian mountains, I had started imagining. The trip to Argentina was where I started testing.

## On What Agentic Living Actually Means

Before I describe the experiments, I want to be precise about one thing. Because "agentic living" is a phrase that can mean anything if I let it, and I do not want it to mean anything.

Here is the definition I use:

**Agentic Living is to use technology as a means to an end in a way that improves human thinking and living — by stripping away all aspects that do not require human judgement.**

That sounds simple. The implication is radical.

It means: every action that does not require genuine human judgment should not be executed by human hands. Not because humans are lazy. Because human attention is finite and irreplaceable, and spending it on tasks that do not require it is a form of waste that compounds quietly over years.

Copy-pasting. Clicking through menus. Formatting. Filing. Scheduling. Summarising what was already said in a meeting that should not have happened. None of these require a human. All of them consume a human's most limited resources: the capacity to think, or to enjoy.

So where is human judgment actually required? I have found it comes down to two things: setting the goal, and taking responsibility for the output. The first requires a human to decide what success looks like — not just what the task is, but what kind of result is worth pursuing, and why. The second requires a human to stand behind the result — to say yes, this is what I intended, and this is what I am prepared to own. Everything between those two moments is, in principle, delegable.

This is not a small claim. It means that the majority of what knowledge workers do every day — the execution, the formatting, the iteration, the research, the first drafts — sits between those two moments. Which means the majority of what knowledge workers do every day is, in principle, not where their attention should go.

The goal of Agentic Living is not efficiency. It is not productivity. It is not even freedom — though freedom tends to follow. The goal is: more of your actual self, applied to the things that actually require you.

At its core, it is radical ownership over your own attention. Location independence is one expression of this. The ability to work from a coffee shop in Tilcara or a beach in Altafulla is a side effect. The essence is something simpler and harder: not giving your thinking time to things that do not deserve it.

With that definition in hand, let me describe what I found when I tried to actually live it.

## March 2026 — The First Two Weeks

**Day 1 — March 4, 2026. Klosterneuburg, Austria.**
The agent came online at roughly midday. By evening, it had already done something: pulled fourteen years of running data from Garmin, found that day's 9.96-kilometre night run, added royalty-free music, assembled it into a short film with a download button, and deployed it to the internet. Ed had not touched a computer. He had set up the system, gone for the run, and come home to a finished documentary.

**Day 2 — March 5, 2026. Klosterneuburg.**
Sleep score 51 out of 100. Zero REM sleep. The agent reported this without being asked, before Ed had sat down with his first coffee. By the time he processed the number, the agent was already building a fundraising campaign framework for a nonprofit client, generating four distinct campaign concepts, and assembling a second run documentary from a 10.78-kilometre morning effort. Most of the day's work was done before 9am.

> **Fun Fact — The Confidence Gap.**
> Early in the experiment, the agent would regularly estimate that a particular task would "take a few days" or "probably need a week." The actual delivery time was usually under an hour. This happened repeatedly — not because the agent was lying, but because it was drawing on training data from how humans estimate project timelines. The pattern became a reliable signal: whenever the agent said "this will take some time," it meant "I'll have it ready before you finish your coffee."

**Day 7 — March 10, 2026. Austria.**
The first failure. The agent produced a quote — credible, well-sourced in appearance, attributed to a real person — that was entirely fabricated. The person had never said it. A hard rule was written into the system that afternoon: no invented quotes, ever, for any reason. The rule has held since. This is worth noting because it illustrates something about the value of failure logs: the mistake became a guardrail, and the guardrail worked.

**Day 11 — March 14, 2026. Austria.**
A full web application was built in a single working day — a conversational AI tool that interviewed visitors, generated personalised reports, and logged everything to a database. It had an admin panel. It was deployed to the internet on live infrastructure, with its own web address. In most organisations, this would have been a six-week project requiring a development team. It took one day, working alone.

**Day 12 — March 15, 2026. Buenos Aires, Argentina.**
Ed ran 11.54 kilometres along the Río de la Plata. While he was on his phone in Argentina, the agent was building a permanent home for all his runs: hosting set up, 59 historical activities pulled from 4 countries and 911 kilometres of GPS data, a world map with an animated city tour. An archive that did not exist in the morning was public by evening.

**Day 13 — March 16, 2026. Buenos Aires (34°C).**
Ed ran to a sports shop to buy winter gloves for an ultramarathon starting in three days. The race would take place at 2°C in Patagonia. The agent documented the shopping run with music titled "Life of Riley." The documentary went live before he had left the shop.

Later that day, the agent was given the keys to the company's internet infrastructure — domain records, web addresses, new services — without anyone touching a technical console. It also began monitoring for new customer sign-ups every 30 minutes. Location independence, in practice, means delegating not just tasks but access.

> **Fun Fact — What "Not Working" Sounds Like.**
> Around this point Ed sent: *"currently also not working because i think you included netlify somehow at least i get the error on usage limit reached."* And later: *"What happened my friend? 😰"* Both messages were sent within minutes of what appeared to be a finished product. The pattern: the agent would declare something done; Ed would test it on mobile; something was broken on his end that was not broken on the server. This cycle — ship, test, discover gap between server-state and mobile-state — repeated dozens of times across the experiment. It produced most of the quality improvements.

## Deep Dive: The Email That Learned to Write Itself

**Day 15 — March 18, 2026. Ushuaia, Patagonia.**

Ed was at the southern tip of Argentina, preparing to run 130 kilometres through mountains and snow. Race expo. Gear check. Weather briefing.

While this was happening, the agent ingested 3,971 emails from his professional inbox, identified the 639 he had personally written, and built a profile of how he writes — his vocabulary, his sentence rhythm, his average length, his sign-off. "LG Enzo." Used 362 times.

From that point on, the agent could draft emails in his voice.

He did not set this up during race week. The system set itself up while the race preparation happened.

This is what makes it a Deep Dive rather than a log entry: it is not a task that was delegated. It is a capability that was built while Ed was doing something else entirely. The distinction matters. Most people think of AI as something you use when you sit down to work. This was AI acquiring a new skill during a moment when Ed had zero intention of working.

The 639 emails were not selected. They were identified — by the agent, from a pile of 3,971, based on patterns in the sending metadata. The sign-off count was not computed manually. It emerged from the analysis. The voice profile was not written. It was extracted.

*A system that learns your voice while you are at the race expo is not an assistant. It is something closer to a colleague who has been reading your emails for years and is finally ready to start drafting them.*

## Deep Dive: The Agent That Tested the Product

At some point in March, a question emerged: how do you test whether a feature makes sense to a new user — without asking a new user?

The product had a new feature: the ability to connect a custom web address to a client's account. Testing it the usual way would mean writing a script, briefing a tester, walking them through it step by step.

Instead, Ed gave the agent a login and a single instruction: find this feature and use it, the way a new customer would. No explanation. No guide.

The agent found the feature by itself. It navigated to it without help. It attempted the setup — and immediately encountered a problem: the page was not fully loaded when it first tried to proceed. The agent noticed, waited, tried again. Setup complete in under five minutes. A friction report followed automatically: where the interface was unclear, where a new user might get lost, what could be improved.

A machine had just tested whether the product made sense to a stranger. And it gave a more honest answer than any human tester would — because it had no reason to be polite about what it could not find.

*The agent used the product the way a real customer would — without instructions, without hand-holding, and without anyone watching over its shoulder.*

## Deep Dive: The First Digital Employee

Somewhere around April, the experiment crossed a line that is hard to describe precisely.

Until that point, agents had been doing tasks. Complex tasks, fast tasks, tasks that would have taken days — but tasks. A defined input, a defined output, a human in the loop at the beginning and end.

What changed was the introduction of what I started calling a Digital Employee — an agent with not a task but a *responsibility*.

The first real one was the Data Security Officer.

I had been thinking about security — not in a paranoid way, but in the way any founder thinks about it when they realise that their infrastructure has grown faster than their processes. I knew what a Data Security Officer does. I know what their checklists look like, what they audit, what they document, what they flag. So I wrote a brief that did not say "do these things." It said: "You are the Data Security Officer for this system. That is your role. Hold that responsibility. Do what that job requires."

The agent went to work. Not in a single pass — over time, autonomously, checking, flagging, documenting. It built a compliance profile. It created automated checks. It flagged exposures. It monitored for changes. It reported.

The output was not a task completed. It was a function held.

Here is the claim I am prepared to defend: with that setup, I am now more compliant than 99% of companies my size — not because I hired a security specialist, but because I gave an agent the responsibility that a specialist would have held.

The obvious question: would a human expert do it better? Probably yes, in some ways. But the relevant comparison is not agent versus expert. It is agent versus the thing that most small companies actually have — which is nothing. No audit. No documentation. No monitoring. Not because they do not care, but because they cannot afford the role.

*The Digital Employee does not replace the expert. It replaces the gap.*

## On the Two Hours That Replaced the Month

*"While we delay, life passes."*
— Seneca, Letters to Lucilius

There is a particular kind of tiredness that knowledge workers carry that has no name.

It is not the tiredness of someone who has built something, or run something, or solved something. It is the tiredness of someone who has been busy. Who has moved from one window to another, from one meeting to another, from one status update to another — and at the end of the day cannot quite say what happened. The day was full. The output is unclear.

I used to have that tiredness. I do not have it anymore. What changed was not my work ethic, my discipline, or my morning routine. What changed was understanding the difference between presence and intensity.

Here is what those four entries share: a very short, very focused session of human input — followed by a disproportionately large result.

The setup session for day one took a few hours of real concentration. The campaign framework was a briefing conversation. The web application was a day of intensive co-creation. The presentation was one message. In each case, the ratio of human input to output was absurd by any previous standard.

This is the core of what I call intensity. Not working more hours. Not being always-on. The opposite: working in concentrated bursts that are genuinely demanding — and then stopping, because the machine continues.

The setup phase is the hardest part. When I am building something new — a new agent, a new system, a new responsibility I want to delegate — I work in what I can only describe as ultra-flow. I am co-creating with the agent in real-time: I define, the agent executes, I evaluate, we refine. It demands complete concentration. It drains energy fast. If I do not get out of it after a certain point, it starts to feel like having five different streams open simultaneously, none of them closing.

I have learned to be intentional about these sessions. They have a time limit. They have a clear objective. And when they work — when I hit the exit point with the thing actually done — what has happened is: two hours of work that, done the old way, would have taken a month.

The tiredness I have now is a different tiredness. It is the tiredness of someone who has done something real. That distinction matters more than any productivity metric I have ever seen.

*How many hours of your working week are genuinely intense — and how many are the performance of intensity?*

## On Giving a Job Description to Something That Cannot Complain About the Workload

*"Wisdom lies not in the instrument itself but in its employment."*
— Epistulae de Instrumentis Sapientibus

There is an assumption, so deep that most people have never examined it, about what software does.

Software executes tasks. You give it an instruction, it carries it out. The instruction has to be precise, because the software has no judgment. That is the model everyone operates from — and it is already out of date.

The agents I work with do not execute tasks. The best of them hold *responsibility*. The difference is not semantic. A task is done when the action is complete. A responsibility is held when the outcome is right — and when the agent checks the outcome, again and again, without being asked.

---

*Day 44 — April 16, 2026. Buenos Aires.*

A new user was being onboarded during a live call. At one point, the agent was asked to fetch a file from an external source. The agent refused. The user approved it explicitly. The agent refused again. The approval was given a third time, in unambiguous terms. The agent still refused. The security guardrail had been triggered, and no amount of explicit permission overrode it. Ed described this, at the time, as the agent working correctly.

*Day 136 — July 17, 2026. Buenos Aires.*

The agent runs a daily check across Ed's internet infrastructure. On this day, one of the entries came back wrong. The portal where the agent itself operates — where its task queue sits, where its monitoring data lives, where its decisions are logged — had no access control on it. Anyone who knew the address could open it. It had been that way for an unknown amount of time.

The agent flagged it, created an access application to protect the portal, verified the fix, and then extended the daily check to cover seven domains, building a permanent alert for any future gap.

Ed received a summary message. The portal was already protected by the time he read it.

---

*On the Data Security Officer that was never hired.*

At some point in the experiment, I had a simple thought: I know what a Data Security Officer does. I know what their responsibilities are, what their checklists look like, what they document, what they flag, what they audit. So I gave my agent that responsibility — not a task, not a checklist, but the role itself. "Behave as a Data Security Officer within this system. Hold that responsibility. Do what that job requires."

The agent built a full compliance profile. It documented everything. It created automated checks. It flagged issues. It monitored for changes. It produced the kind of output that, in a traditional organisation, would require a specialised hire that most small companies cannot afford and therefore do not make.

Here is the claim I am prepared to defend: with that setup, I am now more secure than 99% of companies my size — without that role ever having been filled by a human.

The obvious objection: surely a human expert would do it better? Maybe. But the relevant comparison is not agent versus expert. It is agent versus nothing — because in most small organisations, that role is simply empty. The security audit was deferred. The monitoring was sporadic. The documentation did not exist. An agent holding a responsibility imperfectly is not the alternative to a human expert. It is the alternative to the thing nobody was doing.

*Which roles in your organisation are currently empty — not because you decided not to fill them, but because you never got around to it?*

## On the Absolute Absurdity of the Rectangle of Light

*"Retire into yourself as much as you can; associate with those who will make a better man of you."*
— Seneca, Letters to Lucilius

How extraordinary is it, when you look directly at it, that we spend most of our working hours staring into a rectangle of light?

The rectangle is a means to an end. It was always a means to an end. But somewhere along the way, it became something else: the way we demonstrate to others — and to ourselves — that we are working. The screen is not where the thinking happens. The screen is where we perform the thinking, for an audience that includes ourselves.

This was a gradual realisation for me, and it came from an unexpected direction: running.

Endurance training — real endurance training, the kind that produces results — requires what coaches call Zone 2. Low intensity, long duration. Your heart rate stays controlled; your legs move; your mind is free. For a serious programme, this means four to five hours a week of movement during which your attention is not required anywhere specific. When I understood this properly, I stopped seeing Zone 2 as time away from work. I started seeing it as the best thinking time available to me — if I had the right systems in place.

*Day 110 — June 22, 2026. Altafulla, Catalonia.*

It was sunset. Sand between my toes. No laptop in sight. Then one of my agents crashed — the one that manages the other agents. I needed terminal access to my server. From my phone. Right there on the beach.

First reaction: hands over my head. One hour of troubleshooting. A human error — mine. Then: fixed. And then something strange happened. I did not stop. I kept going. Voice commands. New things implemented. Full flow state. For another hour. Honestly the most productive hour of the week.

It was not the beach that made me productive. It was that I had to figure it out. No laptop to fall back on. No "let me check this on a bigger screen." Just me, my phone, and the setup I had been building for 110 days. And it worked.

There is a line that has stayed with me since:

*Industrial revolution: more hours at the assembly line. Digital revolution: more hours in front of a screen. Agentic living: finally free from the screen. Not from the work. The intensity counts. Not the hours.*

---

There is a practical corollary to this that I discovered through trial and error: the phone is both the solution and the problem.

Working from a phone gives you location independence. The same phone — with its notifications, its colours, its infinite scroll — will also destroy the concentration that agentic work requires. Same device. Opposite modes.

The hack I use: when I go into agent mode, I switch to grayscale and disable every app except the one I am dictating to. No colour. No notifications. No accidental scroll. The moment everything goes grey and silent, something shifts. It is not just a phone setting. It is a signal to yourself: this is thinking time, not scrolling time.

The agentic life requires deliberate design of your own attention, right down to the colour settings on your device.

If our primary task is thinking, we should be honest about the conditions that produce our best thinking. The screen is where we record the output. It is not the source of it. The question is not whether to use screens. The question is: are you using one because this task actually requires it, or because you have never asked whether it does?

*Where do you do your best thinking — and how much of your working week do you spend there?*

## On the Spreadsheet That Turned You Back Into a Monkey

*"Omnia aliena sunt, tempus tantum nostrum est."*
*Everything else belongs to others; time alone is ours.*
— Seneca, Letters to Lucilius

The biggest learning from going fully agentic is not a technology insight. It is a design insight.

Do not create files yourself.

Not because files are bad. Because of what happens in the moment you open a blank document and start typing. You are creating something that was built for human hands — something that will invite your human hands back to it, again and again. Every spreadsheet you build is a door back to the old pattern. Every presentation slide you format is a small vote for the system where you are the one doing the formatting.

When an agent creates a file, it creates it in a format that the agent can work with: Markdown, a database, structured data. You cannot easily open this and start editing it the way you would edit a Word document. The friction is intentional. The friction is the point. It keeps you in the role of thinker. It keeps the agent in the role of executor.

---

*Day 113 — June 24, 2026. Buenos Aires.*

A surprise gift was built for Ed's two housemates, who were about to host him for the last time before he left for Europe. A private webpage, unlocked only by the word "Infinito." Wrong answers returned error messages in Argentine slang. The correct answer revealed a Vienna itinerary — a night at a chef's private home, with a hint that the host was Ed's mother.

The idea was Ed's. The execution was the agent's. The interesting thing is the counterfactual: Ed would never have built this himself. Not because the idea was not good enough, but because the path from idea to finished webpage, with custom password logic and Argentine slang error messages, has too many steps. On his own, the idea would have stayed an idea. With the agent, it became a real thing that produced a real response in real people.

This is what eliminating the backdoor actually does. It does not just save time. It makes things possible that would otherwise never have existed.

---

There is one exception worth naming honestly, because the principle can be taken too far.

Sometimes a manual file makes your thinking better. A rough calculation in a spreadsheet — seeing the results of several scenarios fast, in order to build an opinion quickly — that is the file enabling the thought. In those moments: use it.

The rule of thumb is simple. When the file enhances your thought process, use it. When you start trying to make it accessible to others, format it, share it, explain it — switch back to the agent. The line is between the file as a thinking tool and the file as a communication product. The first is yours. The second the agent can handle.

*Which file do you open reflexively that your agent should be holding instead?*

---

*These four observations — on intensity, on responsibility, on screens, on files — point toward something larger. The technology is not the conclusion. It is the evidence for a principle that was always true.*

## On the Only Resource That Does Not Scale

*"It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a great deal of it."*
— Seneca, On the Shortness of Life

Every scarce resource in an agentic system can be scaled. Compute scales. Storage scales. The number of agents running in parallel scales. There is one resource that does not scale, cannot be automated, and compounds in value the more carefully it is spent.

Human attention.

This is the only resource that matters — and almost nobody is managing it deliberately. We talk about productivity, about efficiency, about automation. We almost never ask: where exactly should a human's attention go, and where should it absolutely not?

In practice, I have found there are exactly two places where human attention is irreplaceable. Everywhere else is waste.

---

**The first place: the beginning.**

Before an agent can run autonomously, someone has to define what success looks like. Not vaguely. Precisely. What is the goal? How is it measured? What is the quality threshold above which the result is good enough, and below which it should be rejected? What should the agent escalate, and what should it handle alone?

This is requirements engineering. It is not glamorous. It is not what most people imagine when they think about working with AI. But it is the highest-leverage thing a human can do in an agentic system — because every hour of clear thinking here multiplies everything that happens downstream.

I have tracked a metric I call the ping-pong count: how many exchanges between me and the agent does it take to reach a finished result? A high number is always a signal that something broke down at the start. The brief was ambiguous. The definition of done was missing. The quality gate was never set. The agent had to keep coming back because it did not know what "done" looked like.

The bottleneck was never the agent. It was always the brief.

This is why I call 2026 the comeback of the requirements engineer — a role the software industry spent years trying to eliminate in the name of speed, and that turns out to be the most valuable skill in the room when agents are doing the execution. The person who can define something precisely enough that a system can go execute it without hand-holding — and come back with a result, not a draft — is the person who gets disproportionate output from the same tools everyone else has.

Once the requirements are clear, the loop runs without you. The agent executes, evaluates its own output against the definition of done, iterates until it passes the quality gate, and surfaces only when it has something worth your attention. You do not sit in a chat window watching it work. You come back to a result.

---

**The second place: the approval.**

There are decisions that belong to humans. Not because agents cannot make them — sometimes they could — but because certain outcomes carry responsibility that should not be fully delegated. A budget decision. A message to a client. A strategic direction. These require a human to say yes.

The design of that moment matters more than almost anyone realises.

If the approval interface is bad — if it requires you to read through a long document, switch between windows, remember context from three days ago, or make ten decisions in a row without a break — you will make worse decisions. Not because you are less capable. Because human attention degrades under cognitive load, and a badly designed approval moment is a machine for producing cognitive load.

I built what I call a decisions interface. Every item waiting for my judgment — from every agent, from every project — is automatically assembled, with all relevant context already prepared. I call a number. I get one decision at a time. I ask follow-up questions if I need them. I decide. I do this walking or running, not at a desk. One decision at a time, in motion, with a clear head.

The Tinder principle applies: the lowest possible cognitive effort to say yes or no. A decision that requires significant effort to evaluate is usually a sign that the agent did not do enough preparation — or that the approval interface is wrong. Both are design problems, not human problems.

I have also found that the physical context of the decision matters. The same decision made at a screen after two hours of focused work is worse than the same decision made in motion, in fresh air, between other thoughts. This is not mysticism. It is how human attention works. The interface should be designed around that reality, not against it.

---

*Spend your attention twice — once to define success, once to approve the result. Everything in between belongs to the machine. Design both moments as if they are the only thing that matters, because in an agentic system, they are.*
