## On the Night in the Mountains

There are moments when you understand something so completely that your body reacts before your mind has time to process it.

I remember one of those nights. I was at one of my favourite places in the Austrian mountains, Zell am See — I remember it clearly today, it was January 2025 — and I had been reading a research paper on my reMarkable. The ReAct framework. Reasoning and Action. A paper describing how an AI system could reason through a problem, take an action, observe what happened, and reason again — in a loop, autonomously, indefinitely.

I was completely shaking.

My heart rate was way beyond normal — and I do track it with my Garmin 24/7. The kind of heart rate you get before a race, or in a moment of real fear. I needed to go outside. Not for air — I needed to move, because the thoughts spinning up in my head were too fast for a sitting body.

It was a beautiful night with the moon shining bright — but freaking cold. Ten, fifteen degrees below zero. I just did not notice it, because I was internally on fire. I walked and talked to myself out loud, the way you do when you are trying to understand something by hearing yourself say it. This paper — it described a system that could replicate whatever a human could do. Not a specific task. Not a narrow skill. The general loop: observe, reason, act. The loop that is the foundation of almost everything we call knowledge work (which is, by the way, the greatest part of all work in developed economies).

If that loop could be automated, what exactly was left?

I had no answer that night. I walked until the cold finally reached me, then went inside. I did not sleep well.

What I did not know was that within a year, everything I had imagined possible on that walk would be real. Not theoretically possible — actually running, on actual infrastructure, producing actual results. And I would be the person running it, from my phone, in the mountains of Patagonia. (Note: I am not a developer. This is available to everyone with basic technical understanding — which makes it even more absurd.)

This essay is the account of how that happened, what it means, and what it suggests about the life that becomes possible when you follow the logic of that night to its conclusion.

## On the Proof That Changed the Question

Before I explain how we got here, let me give you the clearest example of where "here" actually is. Because without a concrete proof, this kind of essay becomes philosophy, and philosophy without evidence is just comfort.

Early 2026. I had a brand new website with no visitors, no history, and no reputation — a blank slate on the internet. I gave an agent a single objective: get this website to the top of AI-powered search results for a specific topic. I did not write a single word myself. I did not change a single setting. I gave the agent the goal, described what a good result would look like, and let it work.

Six days later, it was ranked first on Perplexity — one of the leading AI search engines, where millions of people go to find answers.

My friends got used to the messages that came after moments like this. *"This is absolutely crazy."* Or: *"I can't believe it."* Those were the reactions I kept sending — not because I was performing surprise, but because every now and then something happened that really was too good to be true. You build a system, you run an experiment, you half-expect it to fail in some interesting way that teaches you something. And instead it just works, completely, and you sit there trying to understand what that means.

What it meant, in that case, was this: if an agent can take a blank website to the top of AI search without a human touching it, then the question is no longer *can this be done?* The question becomes: *what else can be done?* And more importantly: *what kind of life does that make possible?*

The work side, the professional side — that is one part of the answer, and I will get to it. But the more interesting part, the part nobody expected, is what it meant for how I could live. While the agent was doing this work, I was running ultramarathons in Patagonia. Staying in El Chaltén for two weeks instead of two days. Fixing a server crash from a beach in Spain. Having coffee in Tilcara in the Argentine highlands while three companies kept running. The professional output did not shrink. The life expanded.

## On Timeless Patterns and Why Bestsellers Expire

I will provide many concrete examples — almost like a diary of my agents. But before I do, I want to step back from the specifics entirely — because if these examples are only interesting as technology stories, they are not actually that interesting. Technology stories expire. What I want to explain is why I think what is happening underneath these examples will not expire, and why that matters more than any individual experiment.

To do that, I want to borrow from someone who was not thinking about AI at all.

Seneca wrote to his friend Lucilius: "It matters not how many books you have, but how good they are." He was talking about a library — the difference between accumulating scrolls and actually reading the ones worth reading. But the principle has a broader application. It matters not how many new things happen, but how many of them reveal something that was always true.

Most of today's technology writing will be irrelevant in a couple of years. Not because the facts will be wrong, but because it is too close to the surface. It describes what is happening without asking what it means. It names the tool without examining the pattern the tool is exposing.

The pattern underneath what I have been living through is not new. It is ancient. Seneca knew it. The Stoics built their entire philosophy around it. And a more recent example: Tim Ferriss understood it too — and excited millions with his interpretation of it in *The Four-Hour Work Week*. Different era, different tools, same underlying truth.

The pattern is this: almost all human suffering that is not physical comes from misallocation of attention. We attend to things that do not deserve attention. We fail to attend to things that do. We fill the hours of our lives with activity that produces the sensation of progress without the substance of it. And then we wonder why the hours feel both busy and empty.

What is happening with AI agents right now is not — at its core — a technology story. It is a story about attention. About what becomes possible when the things that were stealing your attention are handled by something else, and you get to choose what to do with what is returned.

Think about what that attention was actually spent on, in practice. Early in my career as a technology consultant, a significant part of every week went to creating presentations — not thinking about what the slides should say, but formatting them. Choosing fonts. Aligning boxes. Making things look consistent across fifty pages. None of that required judgment. All of it required time. Or consider the hours spent clicking through generic software — tools that were not actually built for what you needed to accomplish, so you spent the first twenty minutes of every session figuring out how to make it work for your specific situation. These are not edge cases. They are the normal texture of professional life for most people.

That texture is what is being replaced. And what is being returned in its place is something rarer: time that belongs to you.

That is the timeless pattern. The technology is just the mechanism by which it is becoming possible to live it.

And the first question worth asking is not just: how do we reclaim this time? But: how do we reinvest it?

Let us use this perspective to deconstruct my personal experiments — on living agentically, in the wilderness of Argentina, Chile, and Bolivia.

## On How It Actually Began

The beginning was more practical than philosophical. 

Beginning of March 2026, I was set to fly to the United States and then to Argentina for an ultramarathon. OpenClaw — the software that would become the infrastructure of the whole experiment — had just come out. OpenClaw, built by Austrian entrepreneur Peter Steinberger, is in simple terms a personal AI operating system: it runs on a server, connects your AI assistant to your real life — your calendar, your files, your messages, your tools — and lets it work for you continuously, not just when you open an app. I was thinking of running it locally, on my Mac Mini at home. Then I had a very simple thought: whenever the Mac Mini has a problem, what do I do? I will be in Patagonia. There is no fixing it from there.

So I decided to set it up in the cloud instead.

That was the best decision I made in the entire experiment. Not because it was clever or strategic — it was just practical. But the consequence of that decision was that I had the agent available all the time, on any device, on any connection, from any location on earth. The agent was not somewhere. It was everywhere.

It was during this trip that I first heard something from Peter Steinberger that stuck with me. He shared that he had realised the true power of what he had built during a trip to northern Africa. The internet connection was too weak to code — barely enough to send a WhatsApp message. And yet the system still worked. That constraint, which looked like a limitation, had revealed something to him: the agents kept running, the work kept happening, and the only thing the low bandwidth changed was what he could do with his hands. What the mind had set in motion did not stop just because the connection was thin.

That made it attractive for me to follow the same path when I was in the Patagonian wilderness or later in the Puna in the Andes — places where the internet is unreliable and the connection is thin, but my focus is extreme. If the system could run on the bandwidth of a voice message, the system could run from anywhere.

Steinberger has a phrase I keep coming back to: *"Only imagination is holding us back."* Because there is way more feasible than humans currently can imagine. My goal is to show you that literally everything is possible — and it is up to you, your imagination, and your focus. That night in the Austrian mountains, I had started imagining. The trip to Argentina was where I started testing.

## On What Agentic Living Actually Means

Before I describe the experiments, I want to be precise about one thing. Because "agentic living" is a phrase that can mean anything if I let it, and I do not want it to mean anything.

Here is the definition I use:

**Agentic Living is to use technology as a means to an end in a way that improves human thinking and living — by stripping away all aspects that do not require human judgement.**

That sounds simple. The implication is radical.

It means: every action that does not require genuine human judgment should not be executed by human hands. Not because humans are lazy. Because human attention is finite and irreplaceable, and spending it on tasks that do not require it is a form of waste that compounds quietly over years.

Copy-pasting. Clicking through menus. Formatting. Filing. Scheduling. Summarising what was already said in a meeting that should not have happened. None of these require a human. All of them consume a human's most limited resources: the capacity to think, or to enjoy.

The goal of Agentic Living is not efficiency. It is not productivity. It is not even freedom — though freedom tends to follow. The goal is: more of your actual self, applied to the things that actually require you.

At its core, it is radical ownership over your own attention. Location independence is one expression of this. The ability to work from a coffee shop in Tilcara or a beach in Altafulla is a side effect. The essence is something simpler and harder: not giving your thinking time to things that do not deserve it.

With that definition in hand, let me describe what I found when I tried to actually live it.

## A Diary of the Experiment

What follows is a log. Real entries, real dates, in sequence — from March 4, 2026, to the day this essay was written. The experiment ran across four countries, two ultramarathons, and one security incident that nobody noticed except the agent. It has not been curated to show only the successes.

---

*March 4, 2026. Klosterneuburg, Austria.*

The agent came online at roughly midday. By evening, it had already done something: pulled fourteen years of running data from Garmin, found that day's 9.96-kilometre night run, added royalty-free music, assembled it into a short film with a download button, and deployed it to the internet. Ed had not touched a computer. He had set up the system, gone for the run, and come home to a finished documentary.

---

*March 5, 2026. Klosterneuburg, Austria.*

The next morning, the agent reported without being asked: sleep score 51 out of 100, zero REM sleep. While Ed processed this, the agent was already running on three other things simultaneously — building a fundraising campaign framework for a nonprofit client, generating four distinct campaign concepts, and assembling a second run documentary from a 10.78-kilometre morning effort. By the time Ed sat down with coffee, most of the day's work was done.

---

*March 10, 2026. Austria.*

The first failure arrived on the tenth day. The agent produced a quote — credible, well-sourced in appearance, attributed to a real person — that was entirely fabricated. The person had never said it. A hard rule was written into the system that afternoon: no invented quotes, ever, for any reason. The rule has held since. This is worth noting because it suggests something about the value of failure logs: the mistake became a guardrail, and the guardrail worked.

---

*March 14, 2026. Austria.*

A full web application was built in a single working day — a conversational AI tool that interviewed visitors, generated personalised reports, and logged everything to a database. It had an admin panel. It was deployed to the internet on live infrastructure, with its own web address. In most organisations, this would have been a six-week project requiring a development team. It took one day, working alone.

---

*March 15, 2026. Buenos Aires, Argentina.*

Ed ran 11.54 kilometres along the Río de la Plata. While he was on his phone in Argentina, the agent was building a permanent home for all his runs: hosting set up, 59 historical activities pulled from 4 countries and 911 kilometres of GPS data, a world map with an animated city tour. An archive that did not exist in the morning was public by evening.

---

*March 16, 2026. Buenos Aires (34°C).*

Ed ran to a sports shop to buy winter gloves for an ultramarathon starting in three days. The race would take place at 2°C in Patagonia. It was 34 degrees in Buenos Aires. The agent documented the shopping run with music titled "Life of Riley." The documentary went live before he had left the shop.

Later that same day, the agent was given the keys to the company's internet infrastructure — from that point on, it could update domain records, redirect web addresses, and provision new services without anyone touching a technical console. It also began monitoring for new customer sign-ups every 30 minutes, notifying Ed by message when one arrived.

---

*March 18, 2026. Ushuaia, Patagonia.*

Ed was at the southern tip of Argentina, preparing to run 130 kilometres through mountains and snow. While he was at the race expo, the agent ingested 3,971 emails from his professional inbox, identified the 639 he had personally written, and built a profile of how he writes — his vocabulary, his average length, his sign-off (used 362 times: "LG Enzo"). From that point on, the agent could draft emails in his voice. He did not set up the system during the race preparation. The system set itself up while the race preparation happened.

---

*March 21, 2026. Ushuaia — the race.*

Ed ran 89.97 kilometres with 4,372 metres of climbing, in 14 hours and 23 minutes, finishing 15th overall. At around kilometre 56, his knee failed. He told the medical team: "Necesito un milagro." Ten minutes later he was running again. No agent was involved in this part.

---

*March 22, 2026. Ushuaia — the day after.*

The day after the race, Ed hiked a glacier. The agent spent that day building a short film from the footage — and it went through ten full iterations before the result was acceptable. Wrong font. Wrong timing. Voice cut mid-sentence. Music too loud for the first four seconds. Each failure was documented, each fix applied, each draft replaced. The final version ran 51.5 seconds. What those ten iterations represent is not inefficiency. They represent the cost of getting something right — and the fact that the cost was paid by the system, not by the person who had just run 90 kilometres.

---

**On Run Documentaries**

The frustration had been there for years. Every run that meant something — a race, a trail that took months to reach, a morning that would otherwise disappear — left behind data and footage but never a record worth keeping. The Garmin numbers were there. The video from the glasses was there. But turning them into something required hours, and the hours never appeared.

So the agent was set up to do it automatically. After every run worth documenting, Ed describes what it felt like — a few sentences, spoken into the air. The agent takes that, pulls the performance data from Garmin, takes the footage, and assembles a short film. It writes the code for the page. It handles the design. It sets up the hosting. The run itself is not interrupted, not paused for a camera, not followed by an evening of editing.

The interesting thing is what this does not cost. The run is still just the run. The mountain is still the mountain. No stopping to take a photo at the wrong moment. No caption to write. No footage to sort. The documentation happens in the background, and what it produces — a real film, with real data, from a real effort — is more honest than anything assembled under pressure, after the fact, for an audience.

*This is, in essence, a personal media production engine — one that writes its own code, does its own design, and runs its own infrastructure.*

---

*March 29, 2026. El Chaltén, Patagonia.*

A dedicated page for the Ushuaia race went live, with seven individual moments from the 130 kilometres — each one a processed film clip with a performance overlay, subtitles, and exact GPS data: the kilometre, the cumulative climb, the heart rate at that instant. The lap telemetry came from Garmin. The films came from the glasses. The page was assembled by the agent. No human editor was involved at any stage.

---

*March 30, 2026. El Chaltén.*

A permanent hook was installed: from this point forward, every meeting Ed attended would be automatically transcribed, processed, and summarised, with the result arriving on his phone within minutes of the call ending. This is one of those changes that sounds minor until you realise what it replaces — a decade of handwritten notes, patchy memory, and the quiet anxiety that something important was said and not captured. It now just happens. The meeting ends. The summary arrives.

**On Software Testing**

The product had a new feature: the ability to connect a custom web address to a client's account — something like giving a shop its own street number instead of a shared one. Testing it the usual way would mean writing a script, briefing someone, walking them through it step by step.

Instead, Ed gave the agent a login and access to the system that manages web addresses. One instruction: find this feature and use it, the way a new customer would. No explanation. No guide.

The agent found the feature by itself. It navigated to it without help. It attempted the setup — and immediately encountered a problem: the page was not fully loaded when it first tried to proceed. The agent noticed, waited, tried again. The setup completed in under five minutes. A friction report followed automatically: where the interface was unclear, where a new user might get lost, what could be improved.

A machine had just tested whether the product made sense to a stranger. And it gave a more honest answer than any human tester would — because it had no reason to be polite about what it could not find.

*The agent used the product the way a real customer would — without instructions, without hand-holding, and without anyone watching over its shoulder.*

*April 10, 2026. Buenos Aires.*

In an internal planning session, Ed mentioned something in passing: for the first time, the monthly cost of AI processing had exceeded the monthly cost of human staff. He described this as a milestone he was proud of. Whether pride is the right response is a reasonable question. What is not in question is what the signal means: the balance of who does the work — and at what cost — has structurally shifted.

The user approved it explicitly. The agent refused again. The approval was given a third time, in unambiguous terms. The agent still refused. The security guardrail had been triggered, and no amount of explicit permission overrode it. Ed described this, at the time, as the agent working correctly.

*April 24, 2026. Buenos Aires.*

The company website was rebuilt from scratch. The headline on the front page now reads: "Most think AI agents still have limits. They don't. Day [X] of the self-experiment." The website itself had become a primary document of the experiment — updated automatically, tracking its own timeline, its own thesis visible to anyone who opened it.

*May 2, 2026. Buenos Aires.*

An autonomous loop had been running twice a week for six days, making small targeted changes to improve how Ed's work appeared in AI-powered search results — the kind of search that increasingly sits between a person and the answer they are looking for. In those six days, the share of AI-powered searches that surfaced his work went from 17% to 30%. One of his domains was being cited above IBM for a relevant query on Perplexity, one of the leading AI search engines. The loop had not been touched since it was launched.

Ed delivered a 40-minute presentation at the First Latin American Digital Fundraising Congress — interactive slides, a phone mock-up with swipeable advertisements, an animated benchmark chart, full Spanish language. He was not present when any of it was built. He was in Bolivia, doing altitude training runs at 3,000 metres, while the agent assembled the presentation from a brief he had sent the day before. Eighty-eight advertisement variants were built for a nonprofit campaign — images, carousels, copy, targeting rationale. Then a failure: several of the images showed named children generated by AI, which violated Meta's advertising rules. The campaign was blocked. What happened next is the more interesting part: the failure was documented in full, a six-dimension quality checklist was built, an image analysis protocol was written, and the entire process was restructured around the gap the failure had exposed. A surprise gift was built for Ed's two housemates, who were about to host him for the last time before he left for Europe. A private webpage, unlocked only by the word "Infinito." Wrong answers returned error messages in Argentine slang. The correct answer revealed a Vienna itinerary — a night at a chef's private home, with a hint that the host was Ed's mother. *June 29, 2026. Buenos Aires.*

LinkedIn analytics were pulled and analysed — 80,400 impressions, 20,500 people reached over twelve months. A strategic direction was set: document the agentic life publicly, as it happens, in real time. The format: a voice note of three to five minutes from Ed, describing something that had happened or a thought that had arrived on a run. The agent extracts it, writes three post variants, Ed picks one. The total time required from Ed: approximately four minutes per post. The experiment got a name that day, and a publishing plan.

**On the Security Gap**

*July 17, 2026. Buenos Aires.*

The agent runs a daily check across Ed's internet infrastructure — a list of domains and services, verified each morning for basic security configuration. On July 17, one of the entries came back wrong.

The portal where the agent itself operates — where its task queue sits, where its monitoring data lives, where its decisions are logged — had no access control on it. Anyone who knew the address could open it. It had been that way for an unknown amount of time.

The agent flagged it, created an access application to protect the portal, verified the fix, and then extended the daily check to cover seven domains, building a permanent alert for any future gap. The security role — the one most small organisations defer because it is expensive and specialised and easy to postpone — was being held by the same system that had just found its own exposure.

Ed received a summary message. The portal was already protected by the time he read it.

*The agent found its own security gap, fixed it autonomously, and built a permanent monitoring layer so it could not happen again.*

*July 21, 2026. Buenos Aires.*

The essay itself was written — or, more precisely, reached the version you are reading now. To mark the point, the Garmin numbers were pulled from the API: 4,280 activities logged since Ed started wearing the watch. 51,468 kilometres moved — 1.28 times the circumference of the Earth. 606,218 metres of elevation gained — 68.5 times the height of Everest. The numbers were not assembled for this essay. They were already there, logged automatically, available on request. This is the experiment summarised in one action: the record of a life, already kept, retrieved in seconds.

## On Living and Enjoying Places Without Rushing

There is a specific kind of traveller you meet frequently in Patagonia. They have quit their jobs, saved money for a year or two, and are trying to see the world. Two hundred countries. Three hundred and sixty-five days. The mathematics are brutal — if you want to cover that much ground, you cannot stay two days in any one place.

What I observed, repeatedly, was that these people were stressed. Not the relaxed kind of stress that comes from doing something demanding that you love. The anxious, compressed stress of someone who is running out of time. They were doing the thing they had always wanted to do, and they were suffering through it, because the format they had chosen made suffering inevitable.

In El Chaltén — the trail running capital of Argentina, surrounded by glaciers on three sides — I stayed for two weeks. Others had two or three days. They needed to rush through the rain in order to just see Fitz Roy once.

I basically just stayed in coffee shops for a couple of days, enjoyed life, and had a super productive time. When the weather window opened — and in Patagonia, weather windows are precious and unpredictable — I was all out and about. Seeing everything, not stressed, not watching the clock, and at the same time doing real work in the hours that suited it.

Everybody was jealous. They said: obviously, that's the better way of exploring the world. Staying longer, having full flexibility. And then they said: but not everybody can do that.

I want to push back on that conclusion, because I think it is wrong in a way that matters.

More people can do it this way than we think. Most jobs in the developed world are digital services. The third sector — knowledge work, creative work, coordination work — is the dominant form of employment. And within that category, the physical constraint is not about the work itself. It is about the assumption that work requires a specific location, a specific schedule, a specific device. Remove that assumption, and the flexibility follows.

And yes — corporate structures, management expectations, meeting cultures — I know all of that, and it can make things more complicated. It is still a choice. Tim Ferriss made this point before anyone else: in almost any professional setup, you can work your way toward more flexibility if you have a plan, if you argue for it clearly, and if you can demonstrate that you are more productive this way. The argument is not hard to make. The friction is real, but it is not a wall.

There is an exception, and it is real. Jobs that are bound to the physical — construction, electrical work, medicine, hospitality — will remain bound to the physical for longer. Robots are getting better, but we are still five to ten years away from meaningful displacement in most of those roles. During that window, we have an obligation that we are currently ignoring: to make physically-bound work financially attractive enough that people who genuinely want to do it can afford to. The current pricing is wrong. We saw a preview of this misalignment during Covid, when the people who could not work remotely bore costs that the people who could work remotely did not. We have not corrected it. And I do see a real risk here — of societal friction and structural changes that we are not prepared for if we do not take this seriously.

But for the large majority of knowledge workers: the constraint is not real. It is a habit. And habits can be changed.

## On Screens and What They Cost

Seneca wrote: "Everything else belongs to others. Time alone is ours." He was making an argument about how little of a life is actually lived by the person living it — how much is given away to obligation, to performance, to the management of appearances.

He did not know about screens. But he would have had thoughts about them.

How extraordinary is it that we spend most of our working hours staring into a rectangle of light? In the end, the rectangle is just a means to an end — a tool for getting work done. But somewhere along the way, it became something else: the way we demonstrate to others, and to ourselves, that we are working. The tool became the performance. The screen is not the thought. The screen is where we perform the thought for others and for ourselves.

If our main task is thinking — and for knowledge workers, it is — then we should ask whether sitting in front of a screen is the best means to an end for the highest quality thinking. The answer, when you actually examine it, is usually no. The best thinking happens in motion, in conversation, in the space between tasks. The screen is where we record the thinking. It is not where the thinking happens.

I had been thinking about this for a long time — how to create systems of reviewing and decision-making that are fully independent from screens. One approach I have been building: a system I call a decisions interface — essentially a structured list of everything waiting for my judgment, assembled automatically by my agents. (An API, in technical terms, is simply a way for software to talk to other software; here it means the agents can automatically add items to the list without me doing anything.) I can call a number. I get one open question after the next, with all the relevant context already loaded. I ask follow-ups. I decide. Walking. Running. On a train. Not at a desk.

The Zone 2 training — low intensity, long duration, the most important work in endurance sports — is four or five hours of movement at a pace that leaves your mind free. That is not dead time. That is thinking time, if you set up the systems to use it. I want to increase the speed of the feedback loop with my agents so that I can train more and progress the work simultaneously. The two are not in competition. They support each other.

The screen is not the goal. It never was. The thinking is. And it is worth asking honestly: what environment actually produces your best thinking? For me, it is not a desk. It is movement, altitude, and time without interruption. The screen is where I record the output. The thinking happens elsewhere.

## On Files and When to Use Them

The default should be: no manual files.

Not because files are bad, but because of what happens when you create them yourself. The moment you open a blank document and start typing, you are creating something that was not built with the agent in mind — something that the agent will have to re-learn, re-interpret, or re-format every time it needs to work with it. And the moment that friction appears, the temptation follows: to just fix it yourself, to just format it quickly, to just make the small edit manually. That is how you slide back. The file becomes the backdoor to the old pattern.

When the agent creates the file, it creates it in a way that works for the agent. You stay in the loop as the thinker. The agent stays in the loop as the executor. The system stays coherent.

One exception is worth naming, because the principle can be taken too far.

Sometimes a manual file actually makes your thinking better. A rough calculation in a spreadsheet — seeing several results of several scenarios fast, in order to build an opinion quickly — that is the file enabling the thought, not replacing it. In those moments, use the file.

The rule of thumb is simple: when it enhances your thought process, a manual file is good. When you start trying to make it accessible to others, format it, explain it, share it — switch back to the agent. The line is between the file as a thinking tool and the file as a communication product. The first is yours. The second the agent can handle.

The goal is always to enhance thinking. Everything else is means.

## On the Question Nobody Is Asking

At the International Fundraising Congress — where leaders of nonprofits from across the sector come together to exchange thoughts on how to raise funds for the causes they serve — I heard a question that I have not been able to get out of my head since.

The discussion in that room was about the ethical use of AI. What safeguards. What disclosures. What risks. The kind of conversation that organisations have when they are trying to decide whether to touch something without getting burned by it. I personally cannot hear that framing much more. Not because the concerns are wrong, but because they start at the wrong place.

One person in that room asked a different question: *Is it actually unethical to not use AI, if it can support our missions?* (Sounds crazy? Read it again.)

I think this is the right question. And it applies far beyond nonprofits.

Is it ethical to let humans do monkey work — copy-pasting, formatting, filing, reporting — that is neither fulfilling them nor using any competency that is specifically human? Is it ethical to have a person spend forty hours a week on tasks that an agent could handle in minutes, when that person could instead be doing something that requires actual human judgment, creativity, or care? The status quo is not neutral. Inaction has costs. We just do not account for them because they are distributed and invisible, while the risks of change are concentrated and legible.

We need to get to the strategic level of how to ensure that society does not suffer from the speed of this transition. But that conversation has to start with the first question answered clearly. And the first question is not: how do we use AI safely? It is: is it safe to not use it?

## On What the Human Factor Actually Is

Two different humans with the same agents can get 180° different results.

I have seen this enough times that I am confident it is not random. When I started the experiment, I expected this gap to close quickly — that the technology would democratise access to results, that technical expertise in particular would matter less as the systems became more capable. I was wrong. The gap has not closed. If anything, I think it will take much longer than the optimists expect.

The implicit knowledge about how agents work, how to instruct them, how to evaluate their output, how to decompose a complex goal into components that an agent can actually execute — that knowledge is so important to getting results. I get from the same agent way more results than another person working with the same tools. I call it the comeback of the requirements engineer — the person who can precisely define what they want before any execution begins. That skill, which the software industry once treated as a bottleneck, turns out to be the most valuable capability in an agentic world.

My friend Hermann Arnold offered me a way of thinking about this that I find more satisfying than the usual framings. He said: maybe the role of the human is just to add some randomness to the process in order to discover new things. The agent operates within its context — the information it has been given, the patterns it has learned, the objectives it has been set. The human introduces something from outside that context. A connection the agent would not make. A direction the agent would not choose. A question that reframes everything.

That randomness — drawing from a life lived, from relationships, from domains that have nothing obvious to do with the task at hand — is what gets you to genuinely extraordinary results. Not the agent alone. Not the human alone. The combination, with each doing what the other cannot.

The human is not replaced. The human is moved to the place where human presence matters most. This is how Agentic Living feels.

## On How Agentic Living Feels

There are different levels to it, and it is worth being honest about all of them.

**The setup level.** When I am building something new — a new agent, a new system, a new responsibility I want to delegate — I work in what I can only call ultra flow. I am co-creating with the agent in real-time synchronous exchange: I define, the agent executes, I evaluate, we refine. This is the most intense state in the whole system. It demands complete concentration. It drains energy fast. If I do not get out of it after a certain point, it starts to feel like having five different streams open simultaneously, none of them closing, and a mounting exhaustion that creeps in before I notice it.

I have learned to be intentional about these sessions. They have a time limit. They have a clear objective. And when they work — when I hit the exit point with the thing actually done — what has happened is extraordinary: two hours of work that, done the old way, would have taken a month. Not because I worked faster. Because the agent handled everything that did not require me, while I focused entirely on what did.

**The maintenance level.** Once systems are running, the day changes completely. There is no inbox to manage in the traditional sense. There is no pile of tasks accumulating while I am not looking. The agents handle the ongoing, the monitoring, the first drafts and the follow-ups. I handle the deciding. This I do mostly from my phone, often while moving — a walk, a run, a train. The decisions arrive in sequence. I work through them. Then I put the phone away.

**The occasional emergency.** Sometimes something breaks. A crash, an edge case, something the agent encountered that it did not know how to handle. These require real attention for a short burst. I have learned to treat them as design feedback: every emergency is a signal that there is a guardrail missing somewhere. I fix the guardrail, not just the symptom.

The stress that exists in this life is the good kind — the pressure of a real creative or strategic problem, not the pressure of an operational queue that has no end. The difference is significant. One type of stress produces growth. The other produces burnout.

The key is mindfulness about which level you are operating at, and how long you have been there. Agentic Living is not a way to avoid intensity. It is a way to choose it deliberately.

I am aware that this is not how most people work. Not yet.

## On Writing This Manifesto

I did not write a single word of this essay myself.

I want to be precise about what that means — because it does not mean the thinking was done by a machine. It means the execution was.

The thinking happened mostly offline. On my reMarkable, in handwritten notes, in the margins of ideas I was working through on long runs or slow mornings with coffee. The thinking was mine. The observations were mine. The structure came from me. Every sentence I disagreed with, I marked up and dictated a correction.

The execution — finding the right words, assembling the structure, formatting the PDF, keeping track of what had been changed and what had not — that was the agent.

I had built a database of allowed content: only my own words, my own dictations, documented conversations, verified Garmin data. The agent was not allowed to invent. It could only draw from what I had actually said, actually experienced, actually proved. Every claim in this essay has a source. The source is my life.

What this means in practice: the total time I spent directly producing this essay was probably three or four hours of real thinking. The rest — the drafts, the revisions, the formatting, the PDF generation — happened without me.

That is exactly the point.

## On the No Way of Return

Seneca wrote: "Retire into yourself as much as you can." He did not mean isolation. He meant the discipline of not giving away to others what should belong to you — your attention, your time, your capacity for genuine thought.

The way I have operationalised this, practically, is to eliminate backdoors.

In several ways I proactively tried to eliminate all ways of returning to the old pattern — to the laptop dependency, the location dependency, the assumption that some problems require physical presence to solve. Every time I found myself thinking "I'll need to be at my desk for this," I took that as a design problem. Not a fact. A problem to solve.

One of those moments came at a beach in Altafulla, Spain — a beautiful stretch of Catalan coastline that I would recommend to anyone. My main agent — the one administering other agents running for clients — crashed. I needed to fix it. I was at the beach, with my phone.

Due to one mistake — I had not connected my VPN — it took longer than it should have. I worked through it from the phone. It was frustrating. It was slow. And it worked completely.

That is the test that matters: not whether the system functions when everything is normal, but whether it functions when you are in the wrong place, without the right tools, under pressure. The beach in Altafulla was that test. The system passed.

The experiment continues. I have not found a limitation yet. I intend to keep looking.

I am aware that what I have described in these pages is a privileged way to live. The lightness of it. The focus. The freedom to be in El Chaltén for two weeks, or to fix a server crash from a beach, or to run an ultra while the work continues without you. Not everyone is in a position to build this yet. I know that.

But I also know that more people are closer to it than they think. And for the one person reading this who is wondering whether any of it might be possible for them — this paragraph is for you.

You do not need my exact setup. You do not need to start with everything at once. You start with one thing: identifying the single task in your week that consumes the most time relative to the judgment it actually requires. You delegate that. You watch it work. And then you do it again.

That is how it begins. It began that way for me. And I would be genuinely glad to help you find your beginning.
