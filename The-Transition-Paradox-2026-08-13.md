# The Transition Paradox

*by Enzo Duit*

---

*"While we delay, life passes."*
— Seneca

---

## Musk Is Right. Sort Of.

Start with the model that everyone is using as the endpoint, because it is actually coherent.

Elon Musk has described a future where AI drives the cost of almost everything toward zero. Robots make the goods. Agents provide the services. The economy produces abundance with minimal human labour.

In that world, a strange problem emerges: too much output, not enough money chasing it. Prices collapse into deflation. And deflation is dangerous — it causes people to defer spending, companies to stop investing, economies to contract.

To understand why printing money is the mathematically correct response, you need one equation. Economics has a fundamental identity: **Money Supply × Velocity = Price Level × Output**. In plain terms: the total amount of money moving through an economy equals the total value of goods and services produced. If AI drives output through the roof while the money supply stays fixed, prices have to crash. Dramatically. The only way to keep the equation balanced — to prevent economic collapse under the weight of its own abundance — is to expand the money supply to match.

In Musk's model, governments issue checks not to stimulate demand but to prevent deflation. You are not inflating the currency. You are keeping the ratio stable as the denominator explodes.

On paper, this is elegant. It is not reckless monetary policy. It is physics.

The problem is not the destination. It is the gap between here and there.

## The Gap Nobody Is Timing Correctly

Software displaces labour fast. Brutally fast. A team of lawyers replaced by an AI system in a single deployment cycle. A customer support department of four hundred becoming two engineers and a chatbot inside a year. This is not speculation — it is happening now, and it will accelerate.

Physical infrastructure is different. Building the energy grids, housing stock, and supply chains that a post-scarcity world requires takes decades. Nuclear plants: ten to fifteen years from approval to power. New mines for lithium and copper: similar timelines. Continental grid upgrades: generational. You cannot deploy those with a software push.

So here is what the transition actually looks like in practice:

Year 3: millions of workers displaced by software. Year 15: the robots and the energy abundance arrive. In the twelve years between those two dates, Musk's model says — print money and distribute checks.

But the checks land in an economy where housing, energy, and land are still scarce. Still owned. And being bought up faster than ever by the people who are capturing the AI productivity gains. The check arrives. The landlord raises the rent.

This is the Transition Paradox: the tool designed to bridge the gap actively widens it.

## Why Money Goes to the Wrong Places First

In 1730, an Irish-French economist named Richard Cantillon noticed something about how money actually moves through an economy. It does not arrive uniformly. It enters at specific points — whoever is closest to the source of new money — and travels outward from there.

Newly created money enters through central banks, financial institutions, large corporations, government contractors. Those closest to the source spend it before the wider market has adjusted its prices. They buy real assets — land, property, infrastructure — at yesterday's prices. By the time the money reaches ordinary people, prices have already moved to absorb it.

This is the Cantillon Effect. And it is not a historical curiosity.

In an AI transition funded by money printing, the closest nodes to the new money are not displaced factory workers or unemployed logistics drivers. They are the companies building and deploying AI, the investors funding them, and the asset owners who can move capital faster than anyone else. By the time a government check reaches a 52-year-old former warehouse worker in Leipzig, the housing market has already absorbed the money that was printed to help him.

The tool designed to solve the problem makes the problem worse. Not through malice — through mechanics.

## Not All Jobs Are the Same

Here is where the story becomes personal — and where the people reading this essay have more skin in the game than they probably realise.

Not all work faces the same risk from AI. The difference comes down to a simple economic concept: how does demand respond when costs fall?

If the cost of making bread drops dramatically, people do not eat ten times more bread. Demand is fixed by biology. The jobs disappear; the market does not expand to replace them. Economists call this inelastic demand.

If the cost of software development drops dramatically, something completely different happens. Projects that were previously impossible — too expensive, too slow, too complex — suddenly become viable. Demand was never capped by appetite. It was capped by cost. Remove the cost barrier and demand expands without a ceiling. This is elastic demand. And it follows a counterintuitive rule — sometimes called Jevons Paradox after the 19th-century economist who first described it — where making something cheaper increases total consumption rather than reducing it.

Roughly half of developed economies sit in inelastic territory: basic food, housing, energy, primary transport. When AI displaces these workers, the market does not grow to absorb them. The jobs simply end.

The other half — software, science, research, education, media, creative work — is elastic. When costs fall here, demand multiplies. The opportunity expands faster than the workforce is displaced.

If you work in knowledge work, in creative fields, in research or technology or education: you are in the elastic half. You are not safe from change — but you are positioned on the right side of the economic divide. And that comes with a specific obligation.

## The Arithmetic of the Obligation

Here is a question worth sitting with.

If the elastic half of the economy multiplied its output by two — just two times, not ten, not a hundred — what happens to aggregate economic output when the inelastic half is fully displaced?

It stays flat. The elastic half grows to fill the space. Two times fifty percent equals one hundred percent of the original total.

The arithmetic is simpler than it first appears. If the elastic half of the economy multiplies its output by two — not a hundred, not ten, just two — then two times fifty percent equals one hundred percent of the original total. The elastic half grows to fill the space the inelastic half leaves behind.

The bar is not heroic. Two times is achievable today, with current tools, for anyone willing to actually change how they work — not add AI as a convenience layer on top of existing processes, but restructure how work is done around the capability.

Hermann Arnold, a Swiss entrepreneur who reviewed an early draft of this essay, said something honest about where he personally stands: five to ten times his previous output. His teams, he estimates, at two to five times. He described this as commercially successful — but not yet sufficient as a societal contribution.

He is right on both counts. But here is the harder truth that the arithmetic alone does not show: multiplied output does not automatically reach displaced workers.

The United States ran strong productivity growth from 2010 to 2020. Total economic output was up substantially. Median real wages stagnated. The aggregate improved. The distribution did not follow. The gains went to shareholders, to executives, to the owners of capital — not to the people who needed the economy to grow.

There is a second asymmetry buried inside the elastic-inelastic split, and it deserves to be named directly: it maps almost exactly onto physical work versus digital knowledge work.

The person multiplying their output five times with an agent is, in practice, a knowledge worker — someone who can do the job from a laptop, from a beach, from a mountain trail with a phone signal. That freedom is precisely what makes the multiplier available to them: relocate, restructure the day, delegate the routine parts, and the tools compound.

The electrician rewiring a house, the plumber, the crane operator, the person pouring concrete — none of that comes with the option to work from anywhere. It requires a body in a specific location, showing up, every day, for years. No home office. No four-times output from an agent, because an agent cannot climb a scaffold or run conduit through a wall. Robotics and automation are coming for these trades too, but slower than software — a general-purpose robot that can safely rewire an old house or fit a boiler is a much harder, much later problem than a chatbot that drafts contracts. For years yet, this work stays stubbornly human, stubbornly local, stubbornly tied to one place.

And this is exactly the labour the whole system depends on. The energy grid, the housing stock, the water and gas lines, the physical infrastructure this essay's own model of abundance requires — none of it gets built by a knowledge worker with a two-times multiplier. It gets built by people who committed to a specific place, a specific trade, a body that wears out doing it, and who cannot escape into remote work the way the elastic half can.

This is where the individual obligation becomes a societal one, and not just an abstract fairness argument. The people whose labour cannot be relocated, cannot be multiplied by AI, and cannot be substituted for years yet, are the same people the entire transition physically depends on. That dependency has to be priced accordingly — meaning paid, valued, and protected more than it currently is, precisely because these workers do not have the escape routes the elastic half enjoys. A society that lets electricians' and plumbers' wages stagnate while knowledge workers multiply their output and their freedom at the same time is quietly asking the least mobile people to carry the heaviest, least optional part of the transition — for less, not more, reward.

This is where the individual obligation ends and the structural question begins.

## Three Paths — Two Dead Ends

Assume you accept the argument so far, even if you only skimmed it. Here it is again, in one paragraph, so nothing that follows depends on remembering the details above.

The gap between software displacing jobs and physical infrastructure catching up is real — it will take years, not months. Printing money to bridge that gap makes the underlying problem worse, not better, because of a simple mechanism: new money always reaches asset owners, banks, and large companies first, and they spend it on real estate and other assets before prices adjust. By the time a check reaches a displaced worker, the price of everything that check was meant to buy has already gone up. Elastic-sector workers — people in software, research, creative and knowledge work — can personally close part of the gap by multiplying their own output, doubling it or more, using the tools already available. But that individual multiplication does not automatically reach the people who were displaced. It shows up as higher profits for the companies and investors who already own the productive assets, unless something structural forces it to be shared.

With that in view: what are the structural alternatives?

**Tax the winners.** Tax AI companies heavily. Redistribute. The obvious problem: the people being taxed are the same people with the most influence over tax policy. The less obvious but more important problem: taxes on corporate profits arrive slowly, irregularly, and under constant political pressure to be reduced. Workers are displaced now. The tax revenues come later, in the wrong form, and never enough. It buys time. It does not solve the structural problem.

**Regulate the speed.** Force companies to slow AI adoption. Give workers time to retrain. This is the instinct of most European governments and every trade union. The problem: slowing adoption in one country does not slow it globally. Capital migrates to wherever the transition is moving fastest. The country that delays loses tax base, loses competitive position, and ends up with the same displaced workers and less capacity to help them. Unilateral slowdown is self-defeating.

**Build sovereign capacity before the gap opens.** This is the approach that has actually worked — not in an AI context, but in an analogous one. Norway discovered oil in the 1960s. Rather than spending the revenues directly on consumption — which would have caused Dutch Disease, inflating the Norwegian economy and hollowing out its other industries — Norway systematically converted finite oil revenues into permanent financial capital. The Government Pension Fund Global now holds $1.8 trillion, equivalent to roughly $330,000 per Norwegian citizen. The rules are strict: only three percent withdrawn per year; the principal preserved permanently; invested entirely abroad to avoid distorting the domestic economy. Norway participates in global growth without cannibalising itself.

The AI equivalent of this logic is: the companies capturing AI-driven productivity gains are the new oil wells. A state that takes equity positions in those companies early — before the full value is priced in — and deploys the returns into domestic physical capacity is doing something fundamentally different from taxing and redistributing. It is owning a share of what is being created. Not extracting. Participating.

Singapore took an adjacent path through Temasek and its Housing Development Board: not by owning foreign equities but by building domestic physical assets so thoroughly that eighty percent of Singaporeans live in state-built housing. The cost of survival is removed from private speculation. Citizens can afford to live, regardless of what the private housing market does.

The contrast is Great Britain after the industrial revolution. Productive assets privatised during the transition. Displaced workers distributed into poverty rather than capacity. A century of structural catch-up, still incomplete. The lesson was available. It was not applied.

A fair objection to all of this: Norway had oil. Britain had an empire's worth of capital already concentrated by the time the reckoning came. What does a country do that has neither — no oil, no rare earths, no colonial-era capital base — and still wants to build sovereign capacity before its own transition gap opens?

The honest answer is that the resource does not have to be physical. It has to be a revenue stream large enough, and stable enough, to convert into a permanent fund the same way Norway converted oil. Three versions of that already exist.

Singapore had no oil either. What it had was a strategic location, a functioning currency, and — crucially — a government willing to run persistent trade surpluses and channel them into Temasek and GIC rather than spend them domestically. The "resource" was disciplined fiscal policy applied for decades, starting from very little. It compounded into two of the world's largest sovereign funds.

A country with meaningful AI adoption has a version of that same lever available now, without needing to discover anything underground. A national AI-productivity levy — a modest, broad-based tax on the measurable productivity gains AI delivers across the economy, not on any single company — routed directly into a sovereign fund rather than general government spending, is structurally the same move Norway made with oil revenue. The "resource" is the aggregate productivity gain itself. It does not require picking winning AI companies or owning equity in Silicon Valley — it requires the political will to treat a genuinely new revenue source as capital to be preserved, not income to be spent.

The size of that gain is already measurable in the numbers this essay has used throughout: if elastic-sector workers are doubling or tripling their output with the same tools, that additional output is a real, current cash flow — currently landing almost entirely with company owners and shareholders. A modest levy on it, started now while the flows are still small and the political fight over them has not yet hardened, is far easier than trying to claw back concentrated wealth after the fact. Norway did not wait until oil was expensive to start the fund. The lesson is not "you need oil." It is "you need to start before the resource is fully priced in and captured."

It is available again now — to any country willing to start, regardless of what is or is not under its ground.

## On Whether AI Companies Will Have Enough to Share

At this point a reasonable question arises: does this all assume AI companies will generate the kind of profits that sovereign funds could meaningfully participate in? What if the whole sector races to the bottom?

The case for race to the bottom is real. Models are becoming more efficient. Edge AI is improving — models running on mobile devices rather than data centres. Infrastructure overbuilding creates conditions for price wars. Technology has commoditised before.

But here is what the history of technology transitions actually shows: the underlying resource commoditises. The product layer does not.

AWS generates over $100 billion in revenue with margins above thirty percent. Compute — the raw hardware, the electricity through chips — did commoditise, exactly as predicted. But the APIs, the managed services, the institutional knowledge accumulated by millions of developers who built their infrastructure on AWS, the switching costs — those did not commoditise. The moat is not in the hardware. It is in everything built on top of the hardware.

Uber was supposed to face the same fate. Any driver with a car should eventually compete on price until margins collapse. Instead the network effects — driver density, demand prediction, trust infrastructure, accumulated routing intelligence — created a product layer that proved genuinely hard to replicate. The race stalled before the bottom.

Foundation AI models are playing out the same dynamic. The underlying model weights might eventually be replicated by open source equivalents. But the products built on those models — with hundreds of millions of users, accumulated interaction data, embedded enterprise workflows, compounding memory and context layers — are not replicated by better weights. More use creates better product creates more use. The moat builds itself.

Three plausible scenarios:

**Scenario A — Race to the Bottom.** Edge AI plus efficient models plus overbuilt infrastructure → compute becomes utility-priced. Margins collapse. Nothing left to participate in.

**Scenario B — Product Layer Moat.** Raw compute commoditises. A small number of dominant product companies maintain high margins through network effects and data advantages. Wealth concentrates, but it is real wealth.

**Scenario C — Hybrid with public infrastructure layer.** Both happen simultaneously. Commodity compute at the infrastructure level. Oligopolistic positions at the application layer. Public compute infrastructure emerges as viable and necessary — governments build the roads; private companies build the cars.

Scenario A is historically unusual. Scenarios B and C are more consistent with how previous technology transitions resolved — and both still produce the concentration problem. Which is exactly why sovereign equity participation matters more, not less, if Scenarios B or C are the outcome. The option value of an early stake is highest before the moat is built and the valuation prices in the expected dominance.

Norway did not wait until oil was expensive. It started building the fund when oil was cheap.

## What This Has to Do With You

I spent 140 days running an experiment in agentic living — one person, phone only, multiple companies running, two ultramarathons, four countries. The details are in a companion piece, *Agentic Living*. The short version: no limitations found.

That experiment was the individual story. This essay is the structural one. They are connected.

The full argument now:

The transition gap is real. Cash cannot bridge it — Cantillon ensures it makes things worse. Elastic sector workers can close the gap arithmetically with a two-times multiplication of output, but that multiplication does not automatically fix the distribution. Structural responses are needed: sovereign capacity building, equity participation in AI productivity gains, public infrastructure that removes the floor of the cost of living from private speculation.

None of that structural work happens without the individual part first. You cannot build a tax base on a hollowed-out economy. You cannot fund sovereign capacity on the revenues of a workforce that never multiplied its output.

Go agentic. Use the tools. Not to do the same work with less effort — but to do ten times more with the same effort.

Dario Amodei, co-founder and CEO of Anthropic, has made this point consistently in conversations with large enterprises considering AI adoption. His argument: the instinct of finance teams is to look at AI as a cost-reduction mechanism — fewer headcount, lower overheads, same output. That is the wrong frame. The right frame is expansion of what is possible: teams of ten doing what previously required teams of a thousand, markets served that were previously too expensive to reach, products built in weeks that would have taken years. The top-line question, not the bottom-line question. That shift — from efficiency to effectiveness — is the difference between individual productivity and collective economic impact.

That is the individual obligation.

And then: demand the institutions that distribute what that produces. That is the structural one.

The individual incentive and the collective obligation point in exactly the same direction.

The gap does not close itself.

*Which side of it are you building toward?*
