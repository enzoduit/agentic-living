## SECTION INSERT: On Whether AI Companies Will Have Enough to Share

*(Insert after the Norway/Singapore/UBS section, before the closing paragraph "The state that navigates...")*

Hermann Arnold, a Swiss entrepreneur who read an early draft of this essay, raised a challenge worth taking seriously: *"Du gehst davon aus, dass die grossen AI-Modelle viel Geld verdienen werden, soviel wie der norwegische Staatsfonds. Davon gehe ich heute nicht mehr aus."*

He is right to push on this. The sovereign participation argument — the idea that states should take equity stakes in AI companies and use the returns to build public capacity — depends on there being significant profits to participate in. What if the AI transition looks more like a race to the bottom?

The case for race to the bottom is not unreasonable. AI models are becoming more efficient, requiring less compute per inference. Edge AI is improving rapidly — models that run on mobile devices rather than data centres. As the major cloud providers have built out infrastructure aggressively, overbuilding creates the conditions for price wars. Technology has commoditised before.

But technology has also produced durable, extraordinarily profitable businesses that were theoretically supposed to commoditise — and didn't. The mechanism is always the same: the underlying resource commoditises, but the product layer above it does not.

**AWS** is the clearest example. Compute was supposed to become a utility — undifferentiated, low-margin, priced like electricity. Instead, AWS generates over $100 billion in revenue with margins above thirty percent. The raw hardware commoditised. But the APIs, the managed services, the integrations, the ecosystem of tools, the institutional knowledge accumulated by millions of developers who built their infrastructure on AWS — none of that commoditised. The switching costs are not in the hardware. They are in everything built around the hardware.

**Uber** faced the same theoretical race to the bottom — a transport service where any driver with a car should be able to compete on price until margins disappear. Except the network effects — driver density, demand prediction, route optimisation, trust infrastructure — created a product layer that proved genuinely hard to replicate. The race stalled before the bottom.

Foundation AI models are playing out the same dynamic. The underlying weights of a model might eventually be replicated by open source equivalents. But ChatGPT as a product — with its 200 million users, the interaction data accumulated from those sessions, the workflows businesses have built around it, the memory and context layers on top — is not replicated by better weights. The product moat compounds over time precisely because it is built from use. More use creates better product creates more use.

This suggests three plausible scenarios for where AI profits end up:

**Scenario A — Race to the Bottom.** Edge AI + efficient models + overbuilt data centres → compute becomes utility-priced. Margins collapse across the industry. There is nothing left to tax, nothing to co-invest in. The sovereign participation strategy fails before it begins.

**Scenario B — Product Layer Moat (the AWS model).** Raw compute commoditises, but the handful of companies with dominant product positions — trained on proprietary interaction data, embedded in enterprise workflows, with compounding network effects — maintain durable high margins. The wealth concentrates in fewer hands than the optimists expect, but it is real wealth.

**Scenario C — Hybrid, with public infrastructure layer.** Both happen simultaneously in different layers. Commodity compute at the infrastructure level. Oligopolistic product positions at the application layer. Public compute infrastructure becomes viable and necessary — governments build the roads, private companies build the cars on top of them.

The honest answer is that Scenario A is historically unusual. The technology transitions that looked most like commodity races — cloud, mobile, the internet itself — produced some of the most profitable companies in history, not because the underlying resource stayed expensive but because the product built on top of it created durable value. Scenario B and C are more consistent with the pattern.

Crucially: both Scenario B and C still produce the wealth concentration problem. The profits exist. They flow to a small number of private actors. Which is precisely why the sovereign participation argument matters more, not less, under those scenarios — not as a mechanism to tax AI companies after the fact, but as a strategy to take equity positions early, before the product moats are fully established and the valuations price in the expected dominance.

Norway did not wait until oil was expensive to start building the fund. It started when oil was cheap and the infrastructure was still being built. The window for sovereign AI participation is not unlimited. It is now — not because AI companies are guaranteed to remain hugely profitable, but because the option value of an early equity stake is highest before the outcome is certain.

## SECTION REPLACE: On the Obligation of the Elastic Worker — revised

*(Replaces the existing section from "This is not idealism. It is arithmetic." onwards)*

This is not idealism. It is arithmetic — and the arithmetic is more forgiving than it first appears.

A developed economy is split roughly in half. Approximately half is inelastic: housing, food, energy, primary transport. When AI automates these sectors, the jobs disappear but demand does not expand to replace them. The efficiency gain goes to the owners as margin; it does not create new work for the displaced.

The other half is elastic. And here is the crucial question: how much does the elastic half need to multiply its output to compensate for what happens to the inelastic half?

The answer is: two times.

Think it through. If fifty percent of the economy multiplies its output by two — total economic output stays flat, even if the other fifty percent is fully displaced by automation. The elastic half grows to fill the gap left by the inelastic half. This is not a guarantee that displaced workers are better off — we will come to that — but it is the arithmetic that makes the macroeconomic transition survivable.

Hermann Arnold, a Swiss entrepreneur who reviewed an early draft of this essay, made this point directly: *"Wenn 50% der Wirtschaft Servicewirtschaft ist und die auch nur einen x2 Increase hat, dann haben wir die Wertschöpfung der gesamten Wirtschaft kompensiert, auch wenn die zweite Hälfte komplett wegfällt."*

He is right. The bar is not 100 times. It is two times. That is achievable today, with current tools, by anyone willing to actually change how they work rather than add AI as a supplementary layer on top of existing processes.

Hermann also said something honest about where he currently stands: approximately five to ten times his previous output. His teams, he estimates, are at two to five times. He described this as sufficient for commercial success — but not for societal obligation.

He is right about both assessments. Five to ten times is commercially successful. But here is the harder truth: even five times output from every elastic sector worker would not by itself solve the problem. Because the problem is not only the size of the economic pie — it is the shape of the distribution.

Productivity gains in elastic sectors flow primarily to the companies and their owners, not automatically to the workers displaced from inelastic sectors. The United States ran a decade of strong productivity growth from 2010 to 2020. Total output was up. Median real wages stagnated. The aggregate improved. The distribution did not follow.

This is the second layer of the obligation. The first layer is multiplication — produce more, use the tools, stop treating AI as a convenience and start treating it as the infrastructure of your work. The second layer is ensuring that what is produced by that multiplication reaches the people who need it — which requires not individual good intentions but institutional design.

The individual obligation and the structural obligation are not substitutes. They are complements.

You cannot build public capacity — sovereign energy, public compute, social housing — on a tax base that has collapsed because elastic sector workers did not multiply their output. The revenue has to come from somewhere, and it comes from productive work.

And you cannot rely on elastic sector workers to solve the distribution problem on their own. No individual's decision to go agentic will, by itself, redirect the gains toward displaced factory workers in Detroit or logistics workers in Düsseldorf. That requires policy: sovereign equity stakes in AI companies, public compute infrastructure, housing capacity that removes the cost of living from private speculation.

The conclusion is the same as before — go agentic — but the framing is more honest. The multiplication is necessary. It is not sufficient. The structural work has to happen alongside it, not instead of it.

*Go agentic. And demand the institutions that distribute what that produces.*
