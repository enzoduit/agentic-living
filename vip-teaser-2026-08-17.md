## Reclaiming Attention, Multiplying Output, and What Comes Next

## Prologue: The Sub-Zero Epiphany

January 2025. Zell am See, Austria.

I was inside, reading a research paper on my reMarkable. The mountain air outside was fifteen degrees below zero. I didn't notice the cold until later, because by the time I finished the paper I was already outside — walking through the snow, talking to myself, trying to slow down what was happening in my head.

The paper was about the ReAct framework. Reasoning and Action. A simple architecture: an AI system observes a situation, reasons through it, takes an action, observes the result, and loops — autonomously, indefinitely.

That loop is the engine of almost all knowledge work. A consultant reads a brief, thinks, writes a response, reads the reaction, adjusts. A developer reads a bug report, reasons, writes a fix, tests it, reads the output. Every role, every desk, every developed economy. The same loop, millions of times a day.

I looked at my Garmin. My heart rate was elevated as if I were about to start a race.

If that loop could run on a server — without a human inside it — what exactly was left for human effort?

I walked until the cold finally reached me. Then I went back inside.

I did not sleep well.

Within a year, that question had an answer. Not a theoretical one. A practical one, running on actual infrastructure, producing actual commercial results, while I managed it from my phone in the mountains of Patagonia.

This book is the field manual for how that happened — and how you can do the same.

## Introduction: The A.G.E.N.T. Framework

*"While we delay, life passes."*
— Seneca, Letters to Lucilius

Most of what most people do at work does not require them.

Not because they are replaceable as people. Because the specific tasks consuming their hours — formatting, filing, scheduling, navigating software, summarising things that were already said — require time and attention, but not judgment. Not the kind of thinking that is irreplaceable.

Tim Ferriss understood a version of this when he wrote *The 4-Hour Workweek*. The early web, offshore delegation, and automated arbitrage made it possible to decouple time from income in ways that had not existed before. The underlying idea was not new: Seneca had articulated it two thousand years earlier. *"It matters not how many books you have, but how good they are."* Substitute "books" for "hours" and you have the core principle of this book.

**Agentic Living** is the practice of using technology to improve human thinking and living by stripping away every task that does not require genuine human judgment — and building the infrastructure that handles the rest continuously, without being asked.

# Chapter 1: The Night in Zell am See
## Deconstructing Knowledge Work

There is a specific kind of moment where understanding arrives before the words do.

January 2025. I was sitting inside a mountain hut in Zell am See, reading a research paper on my reMarkable. The paper was about the ReAct framework — Reasoning and Action. An architecture for how an AI system could reason through a problem, execute an action, observe the result, and loop again. Autonomously. Without stopping.

My Garmin showed my heart rate climbing. The kind of number I see at the start of a race, or in a moment of sudden fear.

I put the tablet down and went outside.

---

## The Loop

It was moonlit. Fifteen degrees below zero. I did not feel the cold.

The paper's insight was not complicated. The Observe → Reason → Act loop is the skeleton underneath almost all knowledge work. But the paper was describing something more specific: a loop that could run on infrastructure. Continuously. Without a human triggering each cycle.

Which meant the question was not abstract. It was economic.

If an AI system can observe a situation, reason about it, take an action in the world, and loop — then the loop that has always required a human at the centre of professional life does not require one anymore. Or does not require one as continuously. The boundary is somewhere, and the boundary is moving.

What I kept returning to that night was not "what can I automate?" That question leads to productivity tools. It was sharper: *what is the correct role for a human when the reasoning loop can run without them?*

The answer I eventually arrived at, across the 140 days that followed: two things. Defining what success looks like precisely enough for the system to pursue it. And taking responsibility for what it produces. The goal-setting and the approval. Everything between those two moments — the observing, the reasoning, the executing, the checking — is, in principle, delegable.

I walked until the cold reached me. I went back inside.

I did not sleep well. But I had the question.

---

## What Changed in Twelve Months

Within a year, that question had an answer that was no longer theoretical.

Not a research paper. Actual infrastructure, running actual processes, producing actual commercial results — while I managed it from my phone in the mountains of Patagonia. While preparing for an ultramarathon. While in coffee shops in Argentina and Bolivia and Spain.

I am not a developer. I cannot write production code from scratch. Everything in this book is accessible to anyone with basic technical understanding.

Which is the part that matters most. Because it means the implications are not limited to engineers or technology specialists. They are available to anyone who is willing to actually change how they work — not add AI as a convenience on top of existing habits, but restructure the whole thing.

---

## The Right Question

Walking back to the mountain hut in Zell am See, I was not thinking about productivity.

I was thinking about a much more fundamental question: if the loop can run without me, what should I be doing instead?

That question does not have a universal answer. It depends on who you are, what you care about, and what kind of life you want to build.

But the question is unavoidable now. The people who answer it deliberately — who design their lives around it — will live very differently from those who wait for someone else to decide for them.

This book is one answer. Lived across 140 days, in four countries, across two ultramarathons and three companies and one very useful security incident.

It is not the only answer. But it is a real one.

*What the loop can now do without you is the beginning of the story. What you do with what is returned — that is the story itself.*

# Chapter 2: The Attention Trap
## Why Tech Books Expire & Seneca Was Right

Most books about technology become irrelevant within two years.

Not because the facts go wrong. Because the facts are all there is. They describe what is happening without asking what it means. They name the tool without examining the pattern the tool is revealing.

There is a different kind of writing that does not expire. It asks what is always true, and uses the current moment as evidence.

---

## What Seneca Noticed

There is a useful test for books — for any ideas, really. Do not look at what is on the bestseller list this year. Wait five years, and look back at what is still being read. The vast majority of bestsellers from any given year have disappeared. What remains are the books that found something underneath the surface — a pattern that was true before the specific technology or trend that prompted them, and will still be true when that technology is replaced by something better.

Most tech bestsellers do not survive this test. They describe the moment so precisely that the description expires with the moment.

Seneca wrote to his friend Lucilius: *"It matters not how many books you have, but how good they are."*

He was addressing a library — the difference between accumulating scrolls and actually reading the ones worth reading. But the principle applies to almost anything. It matters not how many tools you adopt, how many subscriptions you run, how many productivity apps you install. It matters how many of them reveal something that was already true before they arrived.

This essay is an attempt to be one of the ones that remains. Not because the specific tools it describes will last — they will not. The names will change. The capabilities will expand. What this essay is trying to hold onto is the pattern underneath: the question of where human attention should actually go, and what happens when you answer it deliberately. That question predates AI. It will outlast this particular moment.

The pattern underneath what AI agents are doing right now is not new. It is ancient.

Almost all non-physical human suffering comes from the same source: we attend to things that do not deserve our attention, and fail to attend to things that do. We fill our hours with activity that produces the sensation of progress — and at the end of the day, we cannot quite say what happened.

Seneca knew this. The Stoics built an entire philosophy around it. The specific mechanism changes with every era. The underlying problem does not.

Not because the specific tools it describes will last — they will not. The names will change, the capabilities will expand. What this essay is trying to hold onto is the pattern underneath: that today's models are already sufficient to enable all of this.

# Chapter 3: The Proof Point
## From Blank Page to #1 on Perplexity in 6 Days

Philosophy is useful. The argument about attention, the Seneca framing, the ReAct loop — all of it matters. But without a concrete proof, this kind of thinking stays abstract. And abstract thinking about AI is not in short supply.

What is in short supply is evidence.

---

## The Experiment

Early 2026. I launched a brand-new domain. Zero search history. Zero domain authority. No existing audience, no backlinks, no established index.

I gave an autonomous agent a single objective: get this website to the top of AI-powered search results for a specific topic.

I configured what success looked like — the target queries, the quality bar, the evaluation criteria. I established guardrails. I started the process.

Then I stepped away.

Before describing what happened, it helps to understand what tools the agent could actually use. The ReAct loop has three parts: Observe, Reason, Act. The Reasoning is handled by the language model. The key question is what the agent could do in the Act step — what actions were available to it. In plain terms:

- **Query AI search engines** — specifically Perplexity, to check whether the site was being cited when someone asked a relevant question. Not keyword rankings, but AI citation.
- **Read and analyse web pages** — visit URLs, read existing content, identify what was missing or weak.
- **Write and structure content** — generate new pages in the format that AI systems favour: specific, well-structured, entity-rich, cross-referenced.
- **Deploy to the live site** — push changes directly to the server, making updates publicly visible within minutes.
- **Evaluate its own output** — re-run the citation check after each update, compare against the original benchmark, iterate.

That is the complete Act surface for this experiment. A narrow, well-defined toolkit applied to a specific objective. The loop ran on those tools, without a human triggering each step.

The agent ran twice a week on its own schedule. It crawled the top-performing content on Perplexity and other AI search engines. It identified the gaps in what existing pages covered. It generated structured content to fill those gaps, published it directly to the site repository, then re-evaluated its own performance against the original benchmarks.

No prompt from me between runs. No check-in. No approval.

Six days later: the site ranked first on Perplexity for the targeted query set.

My domain was being cited above IBM.

---

## What Actually Happened

How people find information has changed fundamentally. For decades, the model was simple: type keywords into Google, get a list of links, click. The optimisation discipline that developed around this — Search Engine Optimisation, SEO — became an $82 billion industry, with nearly two million professionals worldwide.¹ The job was to make content rank in link lists.

Then AI-powered search arrived. A significant and growing share of queries now produce no link list at all — they produce a synthesised answer, with cited sources. Perplexity alone handles over 500 million queries per month.² The person asking either gets cited, or they do not exist.

This shift has a name: Generative Engine Optimisation, or GEO. The term was coined in a 2023 research paper from Princeton, Georgia Tech, and the Allen Institute for AI. It is what the agent was doing.

The loop ran. The gap closed.

This raises a question worth sitting with: **should this be a human job?**

Traditional SEO required human judgment — understanding audience intent, crafting language that resonated with people, making editorial decisions about what to say. GEO is structurally different. The reader is not a human with preferences and emotions. It is a language model running a retrieval query. If the reader is a machine, the case for a human writer is weaker — and the case for an agent that understands how machines read content is stronger.

The experiment tested exactly this. Not just whether the result was achievable, but whether the entire loop — from content creation to deployment to citation verification — could run without a human between steps. It could. A human still decided what the domain should stand for, what questions it should answer. That goal-setting moment belongs to a human. Everything after it ran autonomously.

*¹ Grand View Research, Global SEO Market Report, 2024.*
*² Perplexity AI, company announcements, Q1 2025.*

---

## What My Friends Heard About It

My friends had gotten used to the messages I sent after moments like this.

*"This is absolutely crazy."*

Not because I was performing surprise. Because the result was genuinely too good to be true. You build a system, you run an experiment, you half-expect it to fail in some interesting way that teaches you something. And instead it just works, completely, and you sit there trying to understand what that means.

What it meant was this: the technical question — *can an agent execute high-level knowledge work autonomously?* — is answered. Not in theory. Empirically. The agent identified a goal, designed its own approach, executed without supervision, evaluated its own outputs, iterated, and produced a result that a human team would have taken weeks to achieve.

During those six days, I was in Patagonia. I was in El Chaltén, drinking coffee, watching the mountains. I was in Tilcara, in the Argentine highlands, while three separate commercial operations continued running.

The professional output did not shrink. The life expanded.

---

## The Question That Follows

The proof changes what needs to be asked.

Before: *can this be done?*

After: *what kind of life does this make possible — and what does building it actually require?*

The agent that ranked first on Perplexity did not spontaneously know what to do. It had a clear objective. It had defined quality criteria. It had explicit guardrails. It had a feedback loop. A human designed all of that — thought carefully about what success looked like, encoded it precisely enough for a system to pursue it, and trusted the system to run.

That skill — knowing what you want before you build toward it — is the most underrated capability in the agentic era.


*The system is ready to run. The question is whether you are ready to design it.*

## The Data the Book Is Written On

This is the record on which the entire book is written. Every claim the book makes is verifiable against these numbers.

**Day 1.** 165 minutes. 35 messages. Average time between messages: 4.8 minutes. This was not autonomy. This was scaffolding.

**Five months later.** 1,171 autonomous sessions in a single month. 98% of all activity required no human trigger.

The real curve in the data:**

Here is what the verified numbers actually show, month by month, counted directly from the message logs — group chat and direct chat combined, every cron trigger and heartbeat check separated out from genuine conversation:

| Month | Messages I sent | Sessions (interactive / autonomous) | Autonomy ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| March | 853 | 63 (46 / 17) | 27% |
| April | 1,566 | 178 (74 / 104) | 58% |
| May | 714 | 153 (12 / 141) | 92% |
| June | 592 | 183 (20 / 163) | 89% |
| July | 1,277 | 1,197 (26 / 1,171) | 98% |

The ratio climbs steadily from March onward and is already above 90% by May. July is not a break from the pattern. It is the same climb continuing at much larger scale: the same trajectory, with far more individual autonomous systems now running in parallel.

What actually explains the jump in raw session count — from roughly 150-180 sessions a month in April through June to nearly 1,200 in July — is not a change in autonomy itself, but a change in how many separate autonomous systems existed to generate sessions. By July, the system had grown into what Ed calls a team of digital employees: seven agents, each with defined responsibilities, each running their own task queue, each accountable for a specific domain. One for outreach. One for infrastructure monitoring. One for GEO ranking checks. One for daily cost analysis. Each autonomous. Each with a name and a role.

That level of parallel autonomous operation required a corresponding interface for oversight. Seven agents running independently generate decisions that a Telegram thread cannot manage at volume. The solution was a dedicated decisions portal  — a structured review interface where pending decisions appeared with their evidence, reasoning, and proposed action attached. Ed could approve, reject, or send back with feedback. Every action was logged. The most efficient access point: a voice interface that read decisions aloud when called, accepted verbal responses, and applied them to the decision pipeline. Built in a single session. Operating from anywhere. Autonomy did not suddenly arrive in July. It had been the majority mode of operation since May. July is where it multiplied — and where the infrastructure caught up to the scale.

This is not a transfer of work from human to machine, either. My own message volume did not shrink as autonomy rose — it moved independently, driven by whatever I happened to be building or discussing that month. April and July, the two heaviest interactive months, both correspond to periods of intense new-system construction. May and June, with fewer messages from me, were not less busy on the machine's side — they were the months where already-built systems ran themselves while I was mostly elsewhere, in the mountains.

# Chapter 9: How It Changes You
## The Human Side of the Agentic Shift

Consider what makes a technology book valuable after the technology has moved on. The specific models will change. The specific tools will be deprecated, forked, or acquired before most readers have finished the first reading. Most books about a given technology peak in relevance at publication and decline sharply from there.

What does not change at that pace is the pattern beneath.

The previous chapter is the evidence layer — specific, dateable, verifiable. This happened, on this day, in this place, with this output. The work was real. The failures were real. The friction was real. You now have an impression of what is technically possible.

But that is not what I want to leave you with. Because a book that only proves agentic workflows can produce output at scale joins every other two-year technology book: credible in 2026, a historical document by 2028.

What I want to explore instead is the layer beneath the evidence. Not what the system can produce — but what it does to the person operating it. How the work and life change shape. How the rhythm of a day reorganises when execution is no longer the binding constraint. How thinking itself shifts when the machine holds most of the threads.

These are not technical observations. They are human ones. And human patterns — how attention works, how recovery happens, how presence and absence redistribute across a working life — do not deprecate on quarterly release cycles. The bestselling books on earlier technological transitions are still on shelves decades later, not because the technology they describe still runs, but because the human pattern they identified proved durable enough to outlast the tools that revealed it.

That is what this chapter is for.

The previous chapters describe what to build and how to run it. This one describes what happens to you — and to your life — when you do.

Not the productivity metrics. Not the autonomous session counts. What happens to the person operating the system — how the work feels different, how the days change shape, how thinking itself reorganises around a new set of constraints and freedoms.
