# The Transition Paradox

*by Enzo Duit*

---

*"While we delay, life passes."*
— Seneca

---

## Musk Is Right. Sort Of.

Let's start with the model that everyone is using as the endpoint, because it is actually coherent. Let's trace what it actually implies — as a thought experiment, following the logic wherever it leads.

Elon Musk has described a future where AI drives the cost of almost everything toward zero. Robots make the goods. Agents provide the services. The economy produces abundance with minimal human labour.

In that world, a strange problem emerges: too much output, not enough money chasing it. Prices collapse into deflation. And deflation is dangerous — it causes people to defer spending, companies to stop investing, economies to contract.

To understand why printing money is the mathematically correct response, you need one equation. Economics has a fundamental identity: **Money Supply × Velocity = Price Level × Output**. In plain terms: the total amount of money moving through an economy equals the total value of goods and services produced. If AI drives output through the roof while the money supply stays fixed, prices have to crash. Dramatically. The only way to keep the equation balanced — to prevent economic collapse under the weight of its own abundance — is to expand the money supply to match.

In Musk's model, governments issue checks not to stimulate demand but to prevent deflation. You are keeping the ratio stable as the denominator explodes — not inflating the currency, but matching the money supply to the exploding denominator.

On paper, this is elegant. It is not reckless monetary policy. It is physics.

The problem is the gap between here and there.

## The Gap Nobody Is Timing Correctly

Software displaces labour fast. Brutally fast. A team of lawyers replaced by an AI system in a single deployment cycle. A customer support department of four hundred becoming two engineers and a chatbot inside a year. This is not speculation — it is documented. In July 2023, Dukaan, an Indian e-commerce platform, eliminated ninety percent of its customer service staff after deploying an AI chatbot. Klarna's AI assistant, launched in February 2024, handled the equivalent workload of 700 full-time customer service agents within its first month. [Dukaan CEO Suumit Shah, CNN Business, July 2023; Klarna press release, February 2024] This is happening now, and it will accelerate.

Physical infrastructure is different. Building the energy grids, housing stock, and supply chains that a post-scarcity world requires takes decades. Nuclear plants: ten to fifteen years from approval to power. New mines for lithium and copper: similar timelines. Continental grid upgrades: generational. You cannot deploy those with a software push.

So here is what the transition actually looks like in practice:

Year 3: millions of workers displaced by software. Year 15: the robots and the energy abundance arrive. In the twelve years between those two dates, Musk's model says — print money and distribute checks.

But the checks land in an economy where housing, energy, and land are still scarce. Still owned. And being bought up faster than ever by the people who are capturing the AI productivity gains. The check arrives. The landlord raises the rent.

This is the Transition Paradox: the tool designed to bridge the gap actively widens it.

To make that precise: the tool most people reach for — universal basic income, or any form of direct cash transfer to displaced workers — is structurally incapable of solving the problem it is meant to address. It is not that the amounts are wrong, or the timing is off. It is that cash, in an economy where the scarce assets are already owned, reliably flows to the owners of those assets. The check arrives. The landlord raises the rent. The mechanism is not a failure of implementation. It is the mechanism. If your mental model of the solution involves governments writing checks, this essay is specifically for you.

But before the structural question, the labour market question: who exactly is affected, and how differently?

## Not All Jobs Are the Same

Here is where the story becomes personal — and where the people reading this essay have more skin in the game than they probably realise.

Not all work faces the same risk from AI. The difference comes down to a simple economic concept: how does demand respond when costs fall?

If the cost of making bread drops dramatically, people do not eat ten times more bread. Demand is fixed by biology. When automation with robots speeds up, most of these jobs just disappear; the market does not expand to replace them. Economists call this inelastic demand.

If the cost of software development drops dramatically, something completely different happens. Projects that were previously impossible — too expensive, too slow, too complex — suddenly become viable. Demand was never capped by appetite. It was capped by cost. Remove the cost barrier and demand expands without a ceiling. This is elastic demand. And it follows a counterintuitive rule — sometimes called Jevons Paradox after the 19th-century economist who first described it — where making something cheaper increases total consumption rather than reducing it.

Roughly a third to close to half of developed economies sit in inelastic territory — basic food, housing, energy, primary transport — depending on how broadly the sector boundaries are drawn across adjacent industries. [OECD Service Economy in OECD Countries; Eurostat employment by sector, 2023] When AI displaces these workers, the market does not grow to absorb them.

But "inelastic" is not one category — it splits into two very different fates, and conflating them hides one of the more important distinctions in this whole picture.

The first group is back-office and administrative work: bookkeeping, data entry, first-line customer support, claims processing, scheduling. This is inelastic *and* fully automatable. Software can already do most of it end to end. In technical terms, these jobs do not shrink gradually — they end on the timeline of a single deployment cycle, because nothing about the physical world stands between the software and the task. In practice, organisations move more slowly: change management, retraining cycles, and institutional inertia stretch the visible transition over years rather than quarters. The direction does not reverse, but the pace varies — and that gap between technical possibility and organisational reality is also where the most immediate efficiency opportunities sit. Organisations that deliberately redirect freed capacity toward functions where demand *can* grow are doing something structurally different from those that simply cut headcount. That distinction matters — both for the individuals involved, and for what the aggregate transition looks like at scale.

IBM offers a concrete illustration of what deliberate reallocation can look like. In May 2023, Arvind Krishna told Bloomberg that he expected roughly 30% of IBM’s 26,000 non-customer-facing roles — approximately 7,800 positions in HR, finance, and administrative functions — to be automated away within five years. His position on software developer hiring was the mirror image: *“I don’t intend to get rid of a single one. I’ll get more.”* He meant it. By June 2026, speaking on the Masters of Scale podcast, Krishna said IBM’s software developers were “probably 40% more productive today than they were two years ago.” That productivity gain had not shrunk the developer workforce — it had grown it, because higher output per developer expands what is commercially achievable, which creates more market demand. The inelastic back-office roles got smaller. The elastic software roles expanded. The net outcome was positive — not by accident, but because the organisation actively redirected capacity from where demand was fixed toward where demand could grow. [Arvind Krishna, Bloomberg, 1 May 2023; Masters of Scale, “IBM’s $10 billion bet on what comes after AI,” 18 June 2026]

The second group is physically-bound, hands-on trades: electricians, plumbers, HVAC installers, construction crews. This is also inelastic — demand for a rewired house or a fixed boiler does not multiply just because the price drops — but it is not automatable on the same timeline. Robots and automation are coming for these trades too, and unevenly: cleaning, for instance, is already further along, because a robot that can vacuum and mop a fixed space is a much easier problem than one that can safely run new wiring through a wall in an occupied home. But a general-purpose robot that can rewire a house, fit a boiler, or read a job site the way a plumber does is a much harder, much later problem than a chatbot that processes claims. For years yet, this work stays stubbornly human, stubbornly local, and inelastic in exactly the way the first group is — with one critical difference: it does not disappear. It persists.

That persistence is the point. These are jobs the entire physical economy depends on, that cannot be done remotely, that cannot be multiplied by an agent, and that will not be automated away on any near-term timeline - and yet, by the same inelastic-demand logic that applies to back-office work, they see no natural upward pressure on wages either. If anything, they are underpriced relative to how essential and how durable they are. A society that is honest about this should be actively working to pay these roles more, not less, precisely because the people doing them do not have the option to relocate, retrain into software, or multiply their output the way knowledge workers can. Their scarcity value is only going to increase as everything more easily automated disappears around them.

Beyond wages, there is a deeper structural question: how does a society make these careers genuinely attractive? Better pay is necessary. It is not sufficient if the pathways into the trades have been allowed to decay for decades. Germany, Switzerland, and Austria retain some of Europe's most sophisticated vocational training infrastructure — co-designed with industry, well-funded, and genuinely prestigious. Switzerland leads on prestige and youth participation (roughly two-thirds of Swiss youth choose vocational education as a first choice); Germany provides the global benchmark for scale and employer integration; Austria is strong but includes a broader transition and school-based component. [OECD, Vocational Education and Training Systems in Nine Countries, 2025] Most English-speaking economies have let the equivalent degrade. That is a policy choice, and it can be reversed. The economic case for reversing it is straightforward: these roles are structurally essential to the physical transition, increasingly scarce, and the training infrastructure that produces them is cheap compared to the social cost of not having enough of them.

This also gives the clearest answer to the question people ask most often in this era: *“What job would you recommend? What skill is worth building in a world of AI?”* If the criterion is stability over the next twenty years, the physically-bound trades are the answer. Because they are the one category that sits almost entirely outside the realistic AI and robotics displacement timeline, long enough that a career built in that direction now will be structurally sound for decades. Not because they are glamorous (unfortunately). The riskiest professional paths are those most exposed to inelastic-demand automation: routine administrative work, data processing, first-tier knowledge work. The most stable ones require a body in a specific location doing something machines cannot yet reliably replicate at scale. The people who understand that early, and build skill in that direction, will find themselves in a structurally strong position as everything around them continues to automate.

There is a structural layer beneath this that rarely surfaces in policy discussions: in most developed economies, a disproportionate share of physically-bound trade work is already performed by migrants and workers with migration backgrounds. In Germany, Destatis data from 2023 shows that 67% of employees in construction finishing and drywall work had a migration background - compared to 26% across the economy as a whole. In the United States, approximately 26-30% of the overall construction workforce is foreign-born, rising to 42% among construction laborers and around 18-22% for licensed trades such as electricians, plumbers, and HVAC technicians. [Sources: Destatis 2025; NAHB / Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, 2023-2024; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025]

This is not incidental. It reflects a structural drift: in higher-income economies, native populations have increasingly avoided entering physically demanding trades — pulled toward white-collar, digital, or credentialed careers — while migrants have filled the gap, often because the entry barriers are lower and the relative income better than what they left behind. The result is a dependency that cuts across one of the most politically charged fault lines of the current decade: the same constituencies most vocal about restricting immigration are often the ones most reliant on migrant labour to wire their homes, fit their boilers, and build the infrastructure they live on. That contradiction is not a fringe tension — it is a live structural paradox, and one that helps explain why skilled trades shortages and migration policy are more intertwined than either side of the debate typically acknowledges. Research on European populism consistently finds that economic insecurity and wage stagnation among workers in routine and manual occupations correlate with higher support for anti-establishment parties — not because of trade shortages per se, but because of the broader sense of being structurally left behind while others capture the gains.

The scarcity mechanism adds a final layer. Standard economics predicts that scarcity raises prices — and there is evidence of pressure: Germany reported 369,516 unfilled skilled positions in 2025, down from 532,000 in 2023, yet wages in the trades have not risen proportionally. ManpowerGroup's 2026 Global Talent Shortage Survey reports that 72% of employers globally cannot fill open roles, with skilled trades consistently among the hardest categories to hire for. [Sources: FMC Group / Destatis 2025–2026; ManpowerGroup 2026 Global Talent Shortage Survey] But trades scarcity does not behave like scarcity in a commodity market, because there is a breaking point where the shortage stops raising prices and simply starts meaning things do not get done. A boiler that cannot be fixed in weeks goes unfixed. A housing project that cannot source electricians falls behind schedule by months. At that point, the pricing mechanism has not failed by staying too low — it has become irrelevant. The constraint is capacity, not cost. That is a different and more serious problem than wage suppression alone, and it deserves to be named as such.

The other two-thirds of the economy — software, science, research, education, media, creative work — is elastic. When costs fall here, demand multiplies. The opportunity expands faster than the workforce is displaced.

If you work in knowledge work, in creative fields, in research or technology or education: you are in the elastic two-thirds. You are not safe from change — but you are positioned on the right side of the economic divide. And that comes with a specific obligation.

## The Arithmetic of the Obligation

Here is a question worth sitting with.

If the elastic two-thirds of the economy multiplied its output — even modestly — what happens to aggregate economic output when the inelastic third is fully displaced?

It almost fills the gap. The elastic two-thirds grows to absorb most of the space.

The arithmetic is instructive even before you get to heroic assumptions. If the elastic two-thirds of the economy gains 40% more productive output — roughly what IBM reports their software developers achieving with AI tools today — then two-thirds times one-point-four equals about ninety-three percent of the original total. Not a complete replacement of the displaced inelastic third. But close. And from a realistic, documented, already-observable productivity gain. Not speculation about ten-times transformations.

The bar is not heroic. The IBM case illustrates this directly: Arvind Krishna reported in June 2026 that IBM's software developers were 40% more productive than two years earlier. Not two times. Forty percent. Applied to the elastic two-thirds of the economy, that single productivity gain brings aggregate output to approximately 93% of the pre-displacement baseline — without requiring anyone to become ten times more productive, and assuming all inelastic jobs disappear simultaneously. The realistic case — where inelastic displacement is partial and gradual, not instant — is more optimistic still.

Hermann Arnold, a Swiss entrepreneur who reviewed an early draft of this essay, said something honest about where he personally stands: five to ten times his previous output. His teams, he estimates, at two to five times. He described this as commercially successful — but not yet sufficient as a societal contribution.

He is right on both counts. But here is the harder truth that the arithmetic alone does not show: multiplied output does not automatically reach displaced workers.

The United States ran strong productivity growth from 2010 to 2020. Total economic output was up substantially. Median real wages stagnated. The aggregate improved. The distribution did not follow. The gains went to shareholders, to executives, to the owners of capital — not to the people who needed the economy to grow.

There is a second asymmetry buried inside the elastic-inelastic split, and it deserves to be named directly: it maps almost exactly onto physical work versus digital knowledge work.

The person multiplying their output five times with an agent is, in practice, a knowledge worker — someone who can do the job from a laptop, from a beach, from a mountain trail with a phone signal. That freedom is precisely what makes the multiplier available to them: relocate, restructure the day, delegate the routine parts, and the tools compound.

The electrician rewiring a house, the plumber, the crane operator, the person pouring concrete — none of that comes with the option to work from anywhere. It requires a body in a specific location, showing up, every day, for years. No home office. No four-times output from an agent, because an agent cannot climb a scaffold or run conduit through a wall. Robotics and automation are coming for these trades too, but slower than software — a general-purpose robot that can safely rewire an old house or fit a boiler is a much harder, much later problem than a chatbot that drafts contracts. For years yet, this work stays stubbornly human, stubbornly local, stubbornly tied to one place.

And this is exactly the labour the whole system depends on. The energy grid, the housing stock, the water and gas lines, the physical infrastructure this essay's own model of abundance requires — none of it gets built by a knowledge worker with a two-times multiplier. It gets built by people who committed to a specific place, a specific trade, a body that wears out doing it, and who cannot escape into remote work the way the elastic two-thirds can.

This is where the individual obligation becomes a societal one, and not just an abstract fairness argument. The people whose labour cannot be relocated, cannot be multiplied by AI, and cannot be substituted for years yet, are the same people the entire transition physically depends on. That dependency has to be priced accordingly — meaning paid, valued, and protected more than it currently is, precisely because these workers do not have the escape routes the elastic two-thirds enjoys. A society that lets electricians' and plumbers' wages stagnate while knowledge workers multiply their output and their freedom at the same time is quietly asking the least mobile people to carry the heaviest, least optional part of the transition — for less, not more, reward.

This is where the individual obligation ends and the structural question begins.

## Three Paths — Two Dead Ends

Assume you accept the argument so far, even if you only skimmed it. Here it is again, in one paragraph, so nothing that follows depends on remembering the details above.

The gap between software displacing jobs and physical infrastructure catching up is real — it will take years, not months. Printing money to bridge that gap makes the underlying problem worse, not better, because of a simple mechanism: new money always reaches asset owners, banks, and large companies first, and they spend it on real estate and other assets before prices adjust. By the time a check reaches a displaced worker, the price of everything that check was meant to buy has already gone up. Elastic-sector workers — people in software, research, creative and knowledge work — can personally close part of the gap by multiplying their own output, doubling it or more, using the tools already available. But that individual multiplication does not automatically reach the people who were displaced. It shows up as higher profits for the companies and investors who already own the productive assets, unless something structural forces it to be shared.

With that in view: what are the structural alternatives to fund this sharing?

**Tax the winners.** Tax AI companies heavily. Redistribute. The obvious problem: the people being taxed are the same people with the most influence over tax policy. The less obvious but more important problem: taxes on corporate profits arrive slowly, irregularly, and under constant political pressure to be reduced. Workers are displaced now. The tax revenues come later, in the wrong form, and never enough. It buys time. It does not solve the structural problem.

There is a third problem, less often discussed: taxing success disincentivises the risk-taking that generates it. Innovation is not a passive flow — it is the product of concentrated investment, most of which fails. The investors who funded the AI transition expected that most bets would be losses and that the few successes had to cover all of them plus a return. Heavy winner-taxation changes that calculus: capital migrates to lower-tax jurisdictions; some investment simply does not happen. The tax base you intend to capture is also the productive capacity you are eroding.

A structurally different approach works in the opposite direction: investment incentives rather than profit taxes. The United States moved this way in 2025 — restoring full immediate deductibility for domestic AI research and development, reinstating 100% bonus depreciation for AI hardware placed in service after January 2025, and preserving the 25% investment tax credit for semiconductor and AI infrastructure under the CHIPS and Science Act. The policy logic is the reverse of winner-taxation: lower the cost of building capacity rather than extract value after it is created. That produces a broader base of actors with aligned interests, rather than a smaller base with both the gains and the resentment.

The most defensible variant of the taxation approach replaces corporate profit taxes with a broad, modest levy on the measurable aggregate productivity gains AI delivers across the economy — routed directly into a sovereign fund rather than general spending. The problem: it is extremely difficult to measure what a real productivity gain with AI actually is, and how such an AI "tax" should be structured. Any attempt runs directly into the same disincentive problem — taxing success discourages the risk-taking that produces it. We should know by now that we do not want to disincentivise AI adoption and innovation.

**Regulate the speed.** Force companies to slow AI adoption. Give workers time to retrain. This is the instinct of most European governments and every trade union. The problem: slowing adoption in one country does not slow it globally. Capital migrates to wherever the transition is moving fastest. The country that delays loses tax base, loses competitive position, and ends up with the same displaced workers and less capacity to help them. Unilateral slowdown is self-defeating.

**Build sovereign capacity before the gap opens.** This is the approach that has actually worked — not in an AI context, but in an analogous one. Norway discovered oil in the 1960s. Rather than spending the revenues directly on consumption — which would have caused Dutch Disease, inflating the Norwegian economy and hollowing out its other industries — Norway systematically converted finite oil revenues into permanent financial capital. The Government Pension Fund Global now holds $1.8 trillion, equivalent to roughly $330,000 per Norwegian citizen. The rules are strict: only three percent withdrawn per year; the principal preserved permanently; invested entirely abroad to avoid distorting the domestic economy. Norway participates in global growth without cannibalising itself.

The AI equivalent of this logic is: the companies capturing AI-driven productivity gains are the new oil wells. A state that takes equity positions in those companies early — before the full value is priced in — and deploys the returns into domestic physical capacity is doing something fundamentally different from taxing and redistributing. It is owning a share of what is being created. Not extracting. Participating.

Singapore took an adjacent path through Temasek and its Housing Development Board: not by owning foreign equities but by building domestic physical assets so thoroughly that eighty percent of Singaporeans live in state-built housing. The cost of survival is removed from private speculation. Citizens can afford to live, regardless of what the private housing market does.

The contrast is Great Britain after the industrial revolution. Productive assets privatised during the transition. Displaced workers distributed into poverty rather than capacity. A century of structural catch-up, still incomplete. The lesson was available. It was not applied.

A fair objection to all of this: Norway had oil. Britain had an empire's worth of capital already concentrated by the time the reckoning came. What does a country do that has neither — no oil, no rare earths, no colonial-era capital base — and still wants to build sovereign capacity before its own transition gap opens?

The honest answer is that the resource does not have to be physical. It has to be a revenue stream large enough, and stable enough, to convert into a permanent fund the same way Norway converted oil. Two versions of that already exist.

Singapore had no oil either. What it had was a strategic location, a functioning currency, and — crucially — a government willing to run persistent trade surpluses and channel them into Temasek and GIC rather than spend them domestically. The "resource" was disciplined fiscal policy applied for decades, starting from very little. It compounded into two of the world's largest sovereign funds.

There is a version of sovereign capacity building that requires neither discovering oil nor running trade surpluses for decades. European governments currently spend between fifty and fifty-seven percent of GDP on public services and administration — France and Austria above fifty-six percent, Germany and Sweden close to fifty, the EU average at forty-nine and a half. [Eurostat General Government Expenditure, 2024] Even a ten percent efficiency improvement in public-sector operations through AI — faster processing of benefits and tax returns, reduced administrative overhead, better targeting of transfers, fewer procurement errors — would free the equivalent of five to six percent of GDP annually, without cutting services or raising taxes. In Germany, that is over two hundred billion euros per year available for redeployment into permanent capital. At that rate, Germany could build a sovereign fund equivalent to Norway's current $1.8 trillion Government Pension Fund Global in approximately nine years — and the annual contribution of €200 billion, divided across Germany's 84 million residents, represents roughly €2,400 per person per year being invested in permanent national capacity. The barriers are political and institutional, not economic. A government that deploys AI to improve its own operations before mandating the private sector to do so has both the demonstrated credibility and the generated fiscal space to fund sovereign capacity from within. This is not the primary mechanism — it does not scale fast enough to match the pace of displacement — but it deserves naming as a genuine lever that most proposals overlook: instead of capturing the private sector’s new productivity gains through taxation, making the public sector productive enough to generate capital from its own efficiency.

## On Whether AI Companies Will Have Enough to Share

At this point a reasonable question arises — actually, two reasonable questions. The first: does all of this assume that the state has enough money to invest in and capture value in AI companies? The second: will these companies generate the kind of profits that sovereign funds could meaningfully participate in? What if the whole sector races to the bottom?

The case for race to the bottom is real. Models are becoming more efficient. Edge AI is improving — models running on mobile devices rather than data centres. Infrastructure overbuilding creates conditions for price wars. Technology has commoditised before.

But here is what the history of technology transitions actually shows: the underlying resource commoditises. The product layer does not.

AWS — Amazon Web Services, the cloud infrastructure arm of Amazon; Amazon does not earn most of its money from e-commerce but from selling computing power to the world — generates over $100 billion in revenue with margins above thirty percent. Compute — the raw hardware, the electricity through chips — did commoditise, exactly as predicted. But the APIs, the managed services, the institutional knowledge accumulated by millions of developers who built their infrastructure on AWS, the switching costs — those did not commoditise. The moat is not in the hardware. It is in everything built on top of the hardware.

Uber was supposed to face the same fate. Any driver with a car should eventually compete on price until margins collapse. Instead the network effects — driver density, demand prediction, trust infrastructure, accumulated routing intelligence — created a product layer that proved genuinely hard to replicate. The race stalled before the bottom.

Foundation AI models are playing out the same dynamic. The underlying model weights might eventually be replicated by open source equivalents. But the products built on those models — with hundreds of millions of users, accumulated interaction data, embedded enterprise workflows, compounding memory and context layers — are not replicated by better weights. More use creates better product creates more use. The moat builds itself.

Three plausible scenarios:

**Scenario A — Race to the Bottom.** Edge AI plus efficient models plus overbuilt infrastructure → compute becomes utility-priced. Margins collapse. Nothing left to participate in (besides the infrastructure and hardware required to run all of it).

**Scenario B — Product Layer Moat.** Raw compute commoditises. A small number of dominant product companies maintain high margins through network effects and data advantages. Wealth concentrates, but it is real wealth.

**Scenario C — Hybrid with public infrastructure layer.** Both happen simultaneously. Commodity compute at the infrastructure level. Oligopolistic positions at the application layer. Public compute infrastructure emerges as viable and necessary — governments at least create the capacity to build the roads and co-own them; private companies build the cars.

Scenario A is historically unusual. Scenarios B and C are more consistent with how previous technology transitions resolved — and both still produce the concentration problem. Which is exactly why sovereign equity participation matters more, not less, if Scenarios B or C are the outcome. The option value of an early stake is highest before the moat is built and the valuation prices in the expected dominance.

Norway did not wait until oil was expensive. It started building the fund when oil was cheap.

Let's assume we have the money. The next question is what to do with it — and specifically, why the most obvious answer (send cash) is the wrong one.

## Why Money Goes to the Wrong Places First

In 1730, an Irish-French economist named Richard Cantillon noticed something about how money actually moves through an economy. It does not arrive uniformly. It enters at specific points — whoever is closest to the source of new money — and travels outward from there.

Newly created money enters through central banks, financial institutions, large corporations, government contractors. Those closest to the source spend it before the wider market has adjusted its prices. They buy real assets — land, property, infrastructure — at yesterday's prices. By the time the money reaches ordinary people, prices have already moved to absorb it.

This is the Cantillon Effect. And it is not a historical curiosity.

In an AI transition funded by money printing, the closest nodes to the new money are not displaced factory workers or unemployed logistics drivers. They are the companies building and deploying AI, the investors funding them, and the asset owners who can move capital faster than anyone else. By the time a government check reaches a 52-year-old former warehouse worker in Leipzig, the housing market has already absorbed the money that was printed to help him.

The tool designed to solve the problem makes the problem worse. Not through malice — through mechanics.

## What This Has to Do With You — Where the Chain of Thought Brought Me

I spent 140 days running an experiment in agentic living — one person, phone only, multiple companies running, two ultramarathons, four countries. The details are in a companion piece, *Agentic Living*. The short version: no limitations found.

That experiment was the individual story. This essay is the structural one. They are connected.

The full argument now:

The transition gap is real. Cash cannot bridge it — Cantillon ensures it makes things worse. Elastic sector workers can close the gap arithmetically — and the arithmetic does not require heroic assumptions, only the documented productivity gains already visible today — but that multiplication does not automatically fix the distribution. Structural responses are needed: sovereign capacity building, equity participation in AI productivity gains, public infrastructure that removes the floor of the cost of living from private speculation.

None of that structural work happens without the individual part first. You cannot build a tax base on a hollowed-out economy. You cannot fund sovereign capacity on the revenues of a workforce that never multiplied its output.

At the state level: do the same work with fewer resources. A government that deploys AI to process benefits faster, reduce procurement waste, and target transfers more accurately is not cutting services — it is building the fiscal space to invest in sovereign capacity. The same logic Norway applied to oil revenue, applied to the efficiency gains that AI makes possible within public administration. The Vienna model is instructive here: for decades, Vienna invested heavily in public housing infrastructure — the Gemeindebau — with the result that over 60% of Vienna's residents live in subsidised housing. Compare this to Munich, a city of comparable economic scale, where a dysfunctional private housing market has created one of Europe's worst affordability crises. Public capacity, patiently built, removes entire categories of human suffering from the reach of market speculation. That is not a side effect. That is the point.

At a company level: the same logic points in a different direction. Not fewer people doing the same work — but the same people reaching markets that were previously too expensive to serve, building products that previously required teams of a hundred, operating at a scale that previously required institutional resources.

Dario Amodei, co-founder and CEO of Anthropic, has made this point consistently in conversations with large enterprises considering AI adoption. His argument: the instinct of finance teams is to look at AI as a cost-reduction mechanism — fewer headcount, lower overheads, same output. That is the wrong frame. The right frame is expansion of what is possible: teams of ten doing what previously required teams of a thousand, markets served that were previously too expensive to reach, products built in weeks that would have taken years. The top-line question, not the bottom-line question. That shift — from efficiency to effectiveness — is the difference between individual productivity and collective economic impact.

That is the collective company-level case.

On an individual level, and specifically for everyone whose work sits in the elastic two-thirds of the economy: Go agentic. Use the tools. Not to do the same work with less effort — but to do ten times more with the same effort. That is the individual obligation.

And then: demand the institutions that distribute what that produces. That is the structural one.

The individual incentive and the collective obligation point in exactly the same direction.

The gap does not close itself.

*Which side of it are you building toward?*
