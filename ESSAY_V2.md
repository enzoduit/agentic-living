### A Field Essay

*by Enzo Duit*

## I. The Night in the Mountains

I remember one day when I was completely shaking.

I had a heart frequency that was just way beyond normal. I was in the Austrian mountains and I actually needed to go out and talk to myself in order to get rid of all the thoughts that were spinning up in my head.

The trigger was that I read the ReAct framework — the reasoning and action framework — and understood how it works. I was flipping out because I had that impression that this will eliminate the need for all human work because it's replicating whatever a human can do, also what an agent can do. This was so crazy for me in that moment that I really had no idea how to handle it.

I went out for a long walk. It was a beautiful night with the moon but freaking cold, I think 10 or 15 degrees below zero. I just didn't notice it because I was just internally on fire, talking to myself about how crazy this will change the world.

That was 2024 or 2025.

Within a year, everything I had imagined that night had become real.

## II. The Proof Nobody Expected

Fast forward to early 2026.

An empty domain. No backlinks. No content. No reputation. My agent was given one objective: get ranked first on AI search engines for a specific query. No one touched anything. It ran the loop autonomously — what to publish, where, how to optimise.

Six days later: ranked first on Perplexity.

My friends got used to the messages. *This is absolutely crazy* or *I can't believe it.* Every now and then I had the experience of something that really was too good to be true.

That is what I want to write about. Not the technology. The underlying pattern that makes all of this possible — and what it means for how we live.

---

## III. Three Steps Back

Everybody thinks this is such a fast-moving space — and this may be true for some of the technological aspects. But the question is whether it's about technology or something underlying.

I want to deconstruct the underlying patterns that are long-term, also seeking wisdom in how Stoics view the world and letting Seneca speak a bit. I want to interpret what these super short-term changes have as an underlying long-term pattern, which is obviously more interesting to study because most of today's bestsellers are completely irrelevant in a couple of weeks, months, or years.

My bet is that we find timeless wisdom in what is happening currently by understanding what is really happening and trying to figure out the underlying patterns and the things we really should think about.

### How It Actually Started

Beginning of March 2026, I was set out to fly to the US and then Argentina for an ultra. Just about when OpenClaw came out.

I was thinking of running the agent locally — on my Mac Mini. Then I had the practical thought: whenever my Mac Mini has a problem, what do I do? I'm in Patagonia. There is no fixing it.

I decided to set it up in the cloud instead.

That was the best decision ever.

I had the agent available all the time, from anywhere, on any connection. And it turned out that connection was going to matter a lot. Peter Steinberger — who I got to know during this period — told me that he got to love it when he was in northern Africa and there was not enough internet to code properly. Just getting voice messages out on WhatsApp was possible because it's the lowest bandwidth and is completely optimised on it.

That quote stuck. That made it attractive for me to follow that path when I'm in the Patagonian wilderness or the Puna in the Andes.

And as Steinberger says: only imagination is holding us back. Because there is way more feasible than humans currently can imagine.

## IV. The Experiment

What followed was not a plan. It was a series of moments where the possible kept expanding.

**The run documentary.** One of my first experiments was to connect my Garmin data and create documentaries of all my runs that I grabbed with my Meta glasses — in a documentary style, without me needing to touch anything. After every run I just told my agent my impression on the run. It took the data and the Meta glasses content and created a mini documentary and hosted it on a page.

**The software tester.** We had built a feature in our software. Instead of writing a test script or explaining the feature to a QA tester, I gave the agent access — DNS records, login credentials, the works — and told it to try to find the feature and connect a custom domain, just like a human user would. Without any explanation. Without any hand-holding.

We could watch it doing the job. Without anyone touching anything. It found the feature. It connected the custom domain. It added the DNS record in Cloudflare. Completely on its own.

**The data security officer.** I recently wanted to move into not just single tasks or single objectives but full responsibilities for an entire job. I was thinking about what a data security officer actually does — the responsibilities, the tasks, the documentation. Why not tell the agent to put itself in that role? It went out, looked at the infrastructure, and created not just one task or automation but a full profile of a data security officer.

In my assumption, if you look at SMEs and how they are set up versus what this agent did, I think I'm now way more compliant than 99% of all companies — without needing that role of a data security officer.

But — and this is important — I think that's only possible because I'm a subject matter expert. If you're too far off from a topic, you also can't hold your agents accountable. You run into the struggle of doing potentially severe failures. The human expertise is not eliminated. It moves upstream.

## V. The Life It Creates

> *Non refert quam multos libros habeas, sed quam bonos.*
> It matters not how many books you have, but how good they are.
> — Seneca

The same principle applies to hours.

It matters not how many hours you work, but how good they are.

In El Chaltén — the trail running capital of Argentina, surrounded by glaciers on three sides — I stayed for two weeks. Others who were doing the world-travel thing had just two or three days and needed to rush through the rain in order to just see Fitz Roy once.

I basically just stayed in coffee shops for a couple of days, enjoyed life, and had a super productive time. When the weather window opened, I was all out and about — seeing everything, not stressed at all, and at the same time hyper productive.

Everybody was jealous. They said: obviously, that's the better way of exploring the world. Staying longer, having full flexibility. But they said not everybody can do that.

I think more people can do it than we think.

Most of our jobs are basically digital services. At least in the developed world, it's mostly the third sector. In the first sector, it's mostly work that can be done fully digital. The physical constraint is the exception, not the rule. And even where physical presence is required — construction, electricity, medicine — the administrative and cognitive overhead of those jobs is largely detachable.

We saw a first wave of this distinction during Covid. People who needed to be physically somewhere versus those who didn't. We still haven't priced it correctly. That adjustment is coming.

## VI. The Screen Is Not the Point

How crazy is it that we spend most of our time sitting in front of a screen?

That is, first of all, not good for our body — the sitting. Not good for our eyes. And if our main task is thinking, we should question whether sitting in front of a screen is the best means to an end for the highest quality output of thinking.

I had been thinking about that a lot — how to create systems of reviewing and decision-making that are fully independent from screens.

One approach: I spun up a decisions API that creates a list of all open decisions from my digital employees. I can call a number where I get one decision after the next with all the relevant context, ask follow-up questions, and decide. Moving. Training. Not at a desk.

My hypothesis is that Zone 2 training — low intensity, long duration, the most important for endurance — needs to be leveraged so I can use this time for progressing my agents. There are things where they have limited responsibilities and are forced to ask me. I want to increase the speed of that feedback loop so I can train more and think more simultaneously.

The screen is not the goal. The thinking is.

## VII. On Manual Files

One interesting exception: whenever a file actually makes your thinking process better, use it.

Sometimes I need to do a rough calculation in Excel or Google Sheets — see a couple of results fast in order to build my opinion and my thought chain quickly. That's fine. The rule of thumb: whenever it enables your thinking, a manual file is good. Whenever you start trying to make it accessible to others, format it, explain it — switch back to the agent.

The goal is always to enhance thinking. Everything else is means.

## VIII. What This Is Not

This essay is sometimes mistaken for an argument about productivity.

It is not.

At the International Fundraising Congress — where leaders of nonprofits exchange thoughts on how to raise funds for the causes they care about — someone asked a question I found extraordinary: *Is it actually unethical to not use AI if it can support our missions?*

I personally can't hear the phrase "ethical use of AI" anymore. But this question is different. This question asks: what are the costs of *not* using it?

Is it ethical to let humans do monkey work that is neither fulfilling them nor using their specific human competencies?

I think we should ask this question more. And then get to the strategic level of how we ensure society doesn't struggle from the speed of the transition — not as a reason to slow down, but as the next problem to solve once the first question is answered clearly.

## IX. The Human Factor

> *Omnia aliena sunt, tempus tantum nostrum est.*
> Everything else belongs to others. Time alone is ours.
> — Seneca

Two different humans with the same agents can get 180° different results.

I thought this would be eliminated already. It is not. The implicit knowledge about how AI works and how agents work is so important to get results. I get from the same agent way more results than another person.

My friend Hermann Arnold said that maybe the role of the human is just to add some randomness to the process in order to discover new things. I really love that approach. That randomness — making interesting connections with something the agent may not have in context, something from outside the normally relevant system — that's what gets you to extraordinary results.

The human is not replaced. The human moves to a different position.

Not the operator. The chairman.

Not the one doing. The one knowing what to do, and ensuring it gets done well.

---

## X. What Comes Next

I have not found a limitation yet.

That is not a boast. It is an open question that I intend to keep testing.

The self-experiment continues. In several ways I actually proactively tried to eliminate all backdoors — to fully push myself into not being dependent on a physical place or a notebook.

At the beach in Altafulla in Spain — by the way, an awesome beach, the entire Catalonian area is worth recommending — my main agent crashed. The one administering other agents for clients. I needed to fix it from my phone. Due to one silly mistake of not being connected with my VPN, it took longer than needed. But I was able to fix it completely from my phone.

That is the test. Not whether everything works when everything is normal. Whether it works when you are genuinely unavailable and something breaks.

It worked.

The experiment continues.
