# AGENTIC LIVING
## Reclaiming Attention, Multiplying Output, and What Comes Next

*by Enzo Duit*

---

## Prologue: The Sub-Zero Epiphany

January 2025. Zell am See, Austria.

I was inside, reading a research paper on my reMarkable. The mountain air outside was fifteen degrees below zero. I didn't notice the cold until later, because by the time I finished the paper I was already outside — walking through the snow, talking to myself, trying to slow down what was happening in my head.

The paper was about the ReAct framework. Reasoning and Action. A simple architecture: an AI system observes a situation, reasons through it, takes an action, observes the result, and loops — autonomously, indefinitely.

That loop is the engine of almost all knowledge work. A consultant reads a brief, thinks, writes a response, reads the reaction, adjusts. A developer reads a bug report, reasons, writes a fix, tests it, reads the output. Every role, every desk, every developed economy. The same loop, millions of times a day.

I looked at my Garmin. My heart rate was elevated as if I were about to start a race.

If that loop could run on a server — without a human inside it — what exactly was left for human effort?

I walked until the cold finally reached me. Then I went back inside.

I did not sleep well.

Within a year, that question had an answer. Not a theoretical one. A practical one, running on actual infrastructure, producing actual commercial results, while I managed it from my phone in the mountains of Patagonia.

This book is the field manual for how that happened — and how you can do the same.

## Introduction: The A.G.E.N.T. Framework

*"While we delay, life passes."*
— Seneca, Letters to Lucilius

Most of what most people do at work does not require them.

Not because they are replaceable as people. Because the specific tasks consuming their hours — formatting, filing, scheduling, navigating software, summarising things that were already said — require time and attention, but not judgment. Not the kind of thinking that is irreplaceable.

Tim Ferriss understood a version of this when he wrote *The 4-Hour Workweek*. The early web, offshore delegation, and automated arbitrage made it possible to decouple time from income in ways that had not existed before. The underlying idea was not new: Seneca had articulated it two thousand years earlier. *"It matters not how many books you have, but how good they are."* Substitute "books" for "hours" and you have the core principle of this book.

**Agentic Living** is the practice of using technology to improve human thinking and living by stripping away every task that does not require genuine human judgment — and building the infrastructure that handles the rest continuously, without being asked.

This book is structured around five principles:

| | PRINCIPLE | WHAT IT MEANS |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | **Awareness** | Recognise that knowledge work is an Observe → Reason → Act loop — and that loop can now be automated |
| **G** | **Grounding** | Design systems that survive the harshest constraints: cloud-first, phone-only, low bandwidth |
| **E** | **Elimination** | Strip every task that does not require genuine human judgment from your personal stack |
| **N** | **Nominating** | Appoint autonomous agents to roles — they run without being asked, no chatting required |
| **T** | **Translating** | Translate freed attention into real life — to make Agentic Living happen |

Parts I through III build the philosophy, the infrastructure, and the operating model. Part IV is 140 days of testing all of it in the real world — honest about what worked, what broke, and what surprised me.

The experiment continues. This book is the account of it so far.

## The Loop

It was moonlit. Fifteen degrees below zero. I did not feel the cold.

The paper's insight was not complicated. The Observe → Reason → Act loop is the skeleton underneath almost all knowledge work. But the paper was describing something more specific: a loop that could run on infrastructure. Continuously. Without a human triggering each cycle.

Which meant the question was not abstract. It was economic.

If an AI system can observe a situation, reason about it, take an action in the world, and loop — then the loop that has always required a human at the centre of professional life does not require one anymore. Or does not require one as continuously. The boundary is somewhere, and the boundary is moving.

What I kept returning to that night was not "what can I automate?" That question leads to productivity tools. It was sharper: *what is the correct role for a human when the reasoning loop can run without them?*

The answer I eventually arrived at, across the 140 days that followed: two things. Defining what success looks like precisely enough for the system to pursue it. And taking responsibility for what it produces. The goal-setting and the approval. Everything between those two moments — the observing, the reasoning, the executing, the checking — is, in principle, delegable.

I walked until the cold reached me. I went back inside.

I did not sleep well. But I had the question.

# Chapter 2: The Attention Trap
## Why Tech Books Expire & Seneca Was Right

Most books about technology become irrelevant within two years.

Not because the facts go wrong. Because the facts are all there is. They describe what is happening without asking what it means. They name the tool without examining the pattern the tool is revealing.

There is a different kind of writing that does not expire. It asks what is always true, and uses the current moment as evidence.

---

## What Seneca Noticed

There is a useful test for books — for any ideas, really. Do not look at what is on the bestseller list this year. Wait five years, and look back at what is still being read. The vast majority of bestsellers from any given year have disappeared. What remains are the books that found something underneath the surface — a pattern that was true before the specific technology or trend that prompted them, and will still be true when that technology is replaced by something better.

Most tech bestsellers do not survive this test. They describe the moment so precisely that the description expires with the moment.

Seneca wrote to his friend Lucilius: *"It matters not how many books you have, but how good they are."*

He was addressing a library — the difference between accumulating scrolls and actually reading the ones worth reading. But the principle applies to almost anything. It matters not how many tools you adopt, how many subscriptions you run, how many productivity apps you install. It matters how many of them reveal something that was already true before they arrived.

This essay is an attempt to be one of the ones that remains. Not because the specific tools it describes will last — they will not. The names will change. The capabilities will expand. What this essay is trying to hold onto is the pattern underneath: the question of where human attention should actually go, and what happens when you answer it deliberately. That question predates AI. It will outlast this particular moment.

The pattern underneath what AI agents are doing right now is not new. It is ancient.

Almost all non-physical human suffering comes from the same source: we attend to things that do not deserve our attention, and fail to attend to things that do. We fill our hours with activity that produces the sensation of progress — and at the end of the day, we cannot quite say what happened.

Seneca knew this. The Stoics built an entire philosophy around it. The specific mechanism changes with every era. The underlying problem does not.

---

## What the Mechanism Is This Time

Early in my career as a technology consultant, a significant part of every week went to creating presentations. Not thinking about what the slides should say — that part was the actual work. The other part: formatting them. Choosing fonts. Aligning boxes. Making things look consistent across fifty pages.

None of that required judgment. All of it required time.

Or consider the hours spent navigating software that was not built for your specific situation — the first twenty minutes of every session just figuring out how to make it work this particular way, for this particular need. Or the meeting that exists because someone was not confident an email would be read. Or the status update written to demonstrate that work is happening, not because the information had anywhere useful to go.

This is not a description of bad workers or broken companies. It is the normal texture of professional life, for most people, most of the time.

That texture is what AI agents are replacing. And what is being returned is something rarer: time that belongs to you.

---

## Where Judgment Actually Lives

Here is the question worth asking about everything you do at work:

*Did this require genuine human judgment — the kind of thinking that cannot be described precisely enough in advance for a system to pursue it?*

For most people, the honest answer covers maybe ten to twenty percent of the week. The rest — the formatting, the filing, the summarising, the navigating — required time and attention, but not judgment.

**Agentic Living** is the deliberate practice of protecting that ten to twenty percent.

Not by working less. By ensuring the things that consume time without requiring judgment are handled by systems that do not need judgment — and do not need to be asked every time.

The goal is not efficiency. Not productivity. Not even freedom, though freedom tends to follow.

The goal is: more of your actual self, applied to the things that actually require you.

*What exactly requires you — and what only feels like it does — is the question the rest of this book helps you answer.*

# Chapter 3: The Proof Point
## From Blank Page to #1 on Perplexity in 6 Days

Philosophy is useful. The argument about attention, the Seneca framing, the ReAct loop — all of it matters. But without a concrete proof, this kind of thinking stays abstract. And abstract thinking about AI is not in short supply.

What is in short supply is evidence.

---

## The Experiment

Early 2026. I launched a brand-new domain. Zero search history. Zero domain authority. No existing audience, no backlinks, no established index.

I gave an autonomous agent a single objective: get this website to the top of AI-powered search results for a specific topic.

I configured what success looked like — the target queries, the quality bar, the evaluation criteria. I established guardrails. I started the process.

Then I stepped away.

Before describing what happened, it helps to understand what tools the agent could actually use. The ReAct loop has three parts: Observe, Reason, Act. The Reasoning is handled by the language model. The key question is what the agent could do in the Act step — what actions were available to it. In plain terms:

- **Query AI search engines** — specifically Perplexity, to check whether the site was being cited when someone asked a relevant question. Not keyword rankings, but AI citation. You can think of it as the new way of a Google result.
- **Read and analyse web pages** — visit URLs, read existing content, identify what was missing or weak.
- **Write and structure content** — generate new pages in the format that AI systems favour: specific, well-structured, entity-rich, cross-referenced.
- **Deploy to the live site** — push changes directly to the server, making updates publicly visible within minutes.
- **Evaluate its own output** — re-run the citation check after each update, compare against the original benchmark, iterate.

That is the complete Act surface for this experiment. A narrow, well-defined toolkit applied to a specific objective. The loop ran on those tools, without a human triggering each step.

Here is what one cycle of that loop actually looked like, in Observe → Reason → Act terms — the same loop the GEO skill runs on autonomously twice a week:

**Reasoning:** The agent checks its latest citation benchmark and notices the target domain is missing from the sources Perplexity cites for a tracked query — a gap between what the site covers and what the top-cited pages already answer.

**Action:** It queries Perplexity to confirm the gap, reads the top-ranking pages to see what they cover that the target page does not, drafts new structured content addressing the missing sub-questions, and pushes it live to the site repository.

**Observation:** On the next run, it re-queries Perplexity for the same query and checks whether the domain is now cited. If yes, it moves to the next query on the list. If not, it reads what changed among the top sources and tries a different angle.

That single Reasoning → Action → Observation cycle, repeated automatically, is the entire loop. Twice a week, for six days straight.

The agent ran twice a week on its own schedule. It crawled the top-performing content on Perplexity and other AI search engines. It identified the gaps in what existing pages covered. It generated structured content to fill those gaps, published it directly to the site repository, then re-evaluated its own performance against the original benchmarks.

No prompt from me between runs. No check-in. No approval.

Six days later: the site ranked first on Perplexity for the targeted query set.

My domain was being cited above IBM.

---

## What Actually Happened

How people find information has changed fundamentally. For decades, the model was simple: type keywords into Google, get a list of links, click. The optimisation discipline that developed around this — Search Engine Optimisation, SEO — became an $82 billion industry, with nearly two million professionals worldwide.¹ The job was to make content rank in link lists.

Then AI-powered search arrived. A significant and growing share of queries now produce no link list at all — they produce a synthesised answer, with cited sources. Perplexity alone handles over 500 million queries per month.²

This shift has a name: Generative Engine Optimisation, or GEO. The term was coined in a 2023 research paper from Princeton, Georgia Tech, and the Allen Institute for AI. It is what the agent was doing.

This raises a question worth sitting with: **should this be a human job?**

You may argue that traditional SEO still had an ethical sense of human judgment. GEO is structurally different. The reader is not a human with preferences and emotions. It is a language model running a retrieval query. If the reader is a machine, the case for a human writer is weaker — and the case for an agent that understands how machines read content is stronger.

The experiment tested exactly this. Not just whether the result was achievable, but whether the entire loop — from content creation to deployment to citation verification — could run without a human between steps. It could. A human still decided what the domain should stand for, what questions it should answer. That goal-setting moment belongs to a human. Everything after it ran autonomously.

*¹ Grand View Research, Global SEO Market Report, 2024.*
*² Perplexity AI, company announcements, Q1 2025.*

## What My Friends Heard About It

My friends had gotten used to the messages I sent after moments like this.

*"This is absolutely crazy."*

Because the result was genuinely too good to be true. You build a system, you run an experiment, you half-expect it to fail in some interesting way that teaches you something. And instead it just works, completely, and you sit there trying to understand what that means.

What it meant was this: the technical question — *can an agent execute high-level knowledge work autonomously?* — is answered. Not in theory. Empirically. The agent identified a goal, designed its own approach, executed without supervision, evaluated its own outputs, iterated, and produced a result that a human team would have taken weeks to achieve.

During those six days, I was in Patagonia. I was in El Chaltén, drinking coffee, watching the mountains. I was in Tilcara, in the Argentine highlands, while three separate commercial operations continued running.

The professional output did not shrink. The life expanded.

## The Question That Follows

The proof changes what needs to be asked.

Before: *can this be done?*

After: *what kind of life does this make possible — and what does building it actually require?*

The agent that ranked first on Perplexity did not spontaneously know what to do. It had a clear objective. It had defined quality criteria. It had explicit guardrails. It had a feedback loop. A human designed all of that — thought carefully about what success looked like, encoded it precisely enough for a system to pursue it, and trusted the system to run.

That skill — knowing what you want before you build toward it — is the most underrated capability in the agentic era.

Now assume this keeps going: search increasingly happens through AI systems instead of a Google query and a list of links. This is not speculation — there is already real evidence of the behavior shifting, and I am fully inside it myself, but you can also see it at a larger scale. Just consider the transformation this one concrete example brings to an industry that currently employs close to two million people and generates $82 billion — SEO. If a meaningful share of search moves from links to citations, those roles do not shrink, they change completely. And this is only one small, concrete example of what my agent has been doing for me and with me over the last five months.


*The system is ready to run. The question is whether you are ready to design it.*

# Chapter 4: Grounding
## Cloud-First, Phone-Only, and What Constraints Actually Do

The best decision I made in the entire 140-day experiment was not a breakthrough insight or a clever strategy. It was a practical one, made two weeks before the experiment began, while packing for a trip to Patagonia.

I was going to run OpenClaw locally. On my Mac Mini at home.

Then a simple thought arrived: *whenever the Mac Mini has a problem, what do I do?*

I was about to fly to Ushuaia, at the southern tip of Argentina, to run 130 kilometres through mountains and snow. There was no fixing a crashed home server from there. If the Mac Mini went down, the experiment ended.

So I set it up in the cloud instead.

---

## Why This Changed Everything

Running agent infrastructure on a cloud server means one thing above all: the system is not somewhere. It is everywhere.

Any device. Any connection. Any country. Any conditions.

The agent does not know whether you are at a desk in Vienna or standing in the rain in El Chaltén waiting for a weather window. It keeps running either way.

| LOCAL DEPLOYMENT | CLOUD DEPLOYMENT |
|---|---|
| Single point of physical failure | High availability — runs whether you are online or not |
| Requires constant power + home internet | Accessible from any phone, any connection, anywhere |
| Manual restart when it crashes | Fix remotely when needed |
| You can micromanage every step | Constraint forces management by objective |
| Stops when you travel | Runs 24/7 regardless of location |

The deployment itself is a one-time effort: provision a cloud server, install OpenClaw, connect it to your messaging channel (Telegram or WhatsApp), grant it access to the tools it needs, define what it can do autonomously and what requires your approval. Then start.

The test for whether the setup is complete: can you handle a production incident from your phone, on a bad connection, while doing something else? If yes — the architecture is sound. If no — there is a gap. Find it now.

My practice for every tool, every provider, every external service: I set it up once. I give the agent access. From that point on, the agent needs to be able to run it autonomously. I create an account, connect the credentials, define what the agent is allowed to do — and then I step back. The setup is mine. Everything after that belongs to the system.

This matters more than it might seem. Most people use software as a series of one-off interactions — they log in, do a thing, log out. The agentic model is different: every tool is either fully delegated or not worth having. If the agent cannot operate it without me in the loop, the tool is creating a dependency rather than removing one.

What is changing fast is the agent-to-agent world. Increasingly, even the setup step — the first account creation, the first configuration — can be handled by an agent without human involvement. The line between "I set it up once" and "the agent set it up" is moving, and the direction is clear.

This has an interesting implication for everyone in sales and marketing. I have largely stopped discovering software on my own. The agent does the research, surfaces what is relevant, explains why. I read the summary. If it passes, I enter a payment method — for now. That step will also be delegated, up to a defined budget threshold. Tim Ferriss described this exact model in *The 4-Hour Workweek*: he gave his remote human assistants a budget and the authority to spend it without asking. The mechanism is the same. The agents have replaced the virtual assistants, and the budget delegation logic transfers directly. If a piece of software was not surfaced to me by the agent, it probably does not exist in my stack.

---

## What OpenClaw Actually Is

OpenClaw is a personal AI operating system built by Austrian entrepreneur Peter Steinberger. It runs on a server, connects your AI assistant to your real life — calendar, files, messages, tools — and lets it work continuously, not just when you open an app.

Most AI tools work when you prompt them. You type something, they respond. Close the window, and nothing happens.

OpenClaw changes the model. The agent is always running. It has a schedule. It does things without being asked. It surfaces to you only when something needs your attention.

Peter Steinberger told me something that stuck. He discovered the true power of what he had built during a trip to northern Africa — the connection was too weak to code, barely enough to send a WhatsApp message. And yet the system kept running.

Think of it like sending a message to a friend. The message leaves your phone instantly. Everything that happens next — the processing, the work, the execution — happens on a server somewhere else. All you need is enough bandwidth to get that message out.

What the mind had set in motion did not stop just because the connection was thin.

---

*A note on tool loyalty: I am often asked whether I have tried tool X, whether I have seen this new framework, whether I have tested the latest thing. My honest answer is that even now, well into this experiment, I am not running into the limits of OpenClaw. It is powerful enough that I have not found a reason to hop from tool to tool, from system to system — to keep starting over before anything has a chance to go deep. There is a pattern I have noticed in how people approach AI: they move fast across surfaces and never get below them. The insight that changed how I work came from staying with one system long enough to understand it completely. First get things on the ground. Then you can talk about what comes next.*

---

## What Poor Internet Actually Teaches You

In the Patagonian wilderness and the high Andes, I worked from places where most digital tools fail. Video calls dropped. Collaborative documents refused to sync. But voice notes went through. Short messages went through. The agent infrastructure kept running.

And something became clear that I had not fully understood at a desk with a fast connection.

When all you can send is a thirty-second voice note — clear intent, essential context, nothing more — you stop describing processes. You start describing outcomes. You stop saying "first do this, then do that, then check if the other thing worked." You say: "I need X done by the time I'm back. Here is what good looks like. Here are the lines you should not cross."

That is a fundamentally better instruction.

Not because the constraint forces discipline. Because the constraint makes the gap visible. At a desk with fast internet, you can micromanage your way around an unclear brief. Constant check-ins mask the fact that you never properly defined what you wanted. Low bandwidth removes that option. The brief has to be clear before it leaves your hands.

---

## Voice as a More Natural Interface

There is something specific about voice that makes it better than text for giving instructions — and it is not speed (even though I love the speed, and yes, it is ten times faster).

When you write, you optimise. You compress. Nobody wants to type endlessly, so you start cutting — keywords, loose thoughts, half-finished ideas. The wrong incentive kicks in: shortening it down. The result is a brief that is efficient on the surface and ambiguous underneath.

When you dictate, you narrate. You include the background. You explain why, not just what. You say the thing you assumed was obvious, because speaking it out loud makes the silence around it visible.

The structure that works:

**1. The situation.** What is happening and why this matters right now.

**2. The outcome.** What should exist when this is done — not what steps to take, but what the world should look like.

**3. The limits.** What the agent can decide alone, and what it should flag before acting.

Thirty seconds. Better results than three minutes of text editing that trims all the context out.

This is why I spend most working mornings offline — on the reMarkable, writing by hand, without a screen. The thinking happens there. When I am ready to act, I dictate. The raw thinking goes in. The structured output comes back.

---

*With the constraints understood, the foundation is set. **Grounding** is not the destination — it is what makes everything else stable. What you build on it is where Agentic Living becomes operational: Elimination first, then Nominating the agents that hold the rest without being asked.*

# Chapter 5: Stripping Zero-Judgment Tasks
## The End of Formatting & Menus

Before building anything, you have to clear the space.

This is the step most people skip. They discover AI agents, get excited about automation, and start building workflows — around a working week that still contains forty percent waste. The result is an agent that handles the waste faster. Slightly more efficient clutter.

Eliminate first. Automate second. Always.

---

## The Audit

For five consecutive working days, track everything you do in fifteen-minute intervals. At the end of each day, go through the list and ask one question about each item:

*Did this require genuine human judgment — reasoning under uncertainty, a call that only I could make, thinking the task actually deserved?*

If yes: this is where your attention belongs.

If no: it is a candidate for elimination, delegation, or automation.

| STEP | WHAT TO DO |
|---|---|
| **1 — Log** | Track every activity for 5 days in 15-minute intervals |
| **2 — Judgment Test** | For each item: did this require genuine human judgment? |
| **3 — Categorise** | Label every No: formatting, filing, scheduling, navigation, summaries |
| **4 — Prune** | Remove from your personal stack — assign to agent or eliminate entirely |

What you will find is roughly what I found: somewhere between forty and sixty percent of the week consists of tasks that require time but not genuine thought.

The categories that appear most consistently: formatting documents and slides, navigating software interfaces, writing status updates, scheduling and rescheduling, producing first drafts that will be revised anyway. None of these require you. All of them consume you.

If the five-day log feels too abstract to start, two alternative entry points:

**For anyone who works with KPIs:** Take the two or three metrics you are personally responsible for moving. Map every human action that currently goes into moving them. For each step, ask: does this require my judgment, or does it just require execution? The execution steps are candidates for the agent. Your judgment is the only thing that should remain.

**For executives:** Think about the last three decisions you took. What was required for you to take them confidently — what data, what preparation, what context? What would have made those decisions even better that was not done? The preparation gap is usually where the most delegation opportunity lives. An agent that shows up with everything already prepared — the relevant data pulled, the context assembled, the options framed — changes the quality of the decision, not just the speed of reaching it.

---

## Three Rules

After running the audit, three structural changes produce the most consistent results. These are not one-time fixes. They are permanent shifts.

**Rule 1 — No manual files.**

The moment you create a file yourself — a spreadsheet, a presentation, a document — you build a door back to the old pattern. Every file created by hand invites your hands back. You format it. You align it. You make decisions about presentation that do not matter. All of it consumes you.

At some point around month four of the experiment, I noticed I had barely opened Google Drive in weeks. Not because I had decided to stop using it. Because I had stopped creating the kind of files that needed to live there. The agent generates the outputs. They are stored where the agent can work with them. The Drive had been my filing system. The agent became the filing system. The Drive became irrelevant.

When agents create files, they create them in formats they work with well: Markdown, structured data, databases. You cannot easily open these and start editing them the way you would edit a Word document. That friction is intentional. It keeps you in the role of thinker. It keeps the agent in the executor role.

*Exception:* when a rough calculation in a spreadsheet makes your own thinking faster — use it. The line is whether you are enhancing your thought process or producing something for others. The first is yours. The second is the agent's job.

**Rule 2 — Outcomes, not steps.**

"Write a summary of this meeting" is a step. An outcome looks different: *You are responsible for making sure everything discussed in my meetings gets acted on. Do whatever you can do without me — draft the follow-up emails, create the tasks, prepare the materials. For anything that requires my approval before it goes out, surface it to me with a draft already done. I should never have to think about meeting follow-up. It should just happen.*

The difference is not just specificity. It is the shift from describing what the agent should do to describing what should exist when it is done — and making the agent responsible for the gap between the two. A step describes a process. An outcome describes a world in which the problem no longer exists.

The requirements engineering skill — knowing what success looks like precisely enough that a system can pursue it without hand-holding — is the most valuable capability in the agentic era. Not prompt writing. Not model selection. Knowing what you want before you build toward it.

**Rule 3 — Raw output first. Formatted output never.**

Stop formatting things yourself. The thinking is yours. The presentation layer is the agent's job.

My mornings are built around this. More than two hours, every day, completely offline. reMarkable tablet, handwriting, no screen. The deep thinking happens there — without the pull to polish, structure, or format as I go. Ideas arrive rough. That is the point.

When I am ready to act on something, I dictate. Thirty seconds of voice note describing what I worked through and what needs to happen next. The agent takes the raw thinking, converts it into whatever form the output requires, and handles the rest.

I never see the formatting step. I move from thinking to result.

The discipline is to resist tidying your own output before handing it over. The mess is information. The agent can work with mess. It cannot work with the version you have already pre-edited to the point of ambiguity.

---

## What Remains

After running the audit and applying the three rules, what is left on your list?

The work that is genuinely yours. Strategic decisions. Creative direction. Relationship-building. The judgment calls that require your specific experience, your values, your understanding of what matters in this particular situation.

This is not a small category. It is the most demanding one — the work that is hardest, most draining, and most rewarding. The work that makes the difference between an ordinary outcome and one that could not have happened without you.

The problem was never that this work was insufficient. The problem was that it was surrounded by everything else — the formatting, the filing, the status updates — and the everything else was consuming the attention the real work deserved.

Remove the everything else. The real work expands to fill the space.

*The audit tells you what you have been giving your attention to. The rules tell you how to stop. What comes next is what to build instead.*

# Chapter 6: Building the Digital OS
## Connecting Calendar, Files, and Tools

An agent that can only answer questions is a very expensive search engine.

The capability that makes agentic living possible is not reasoning — it is connection. The ability for the agent to reach into your actual life: your calendar, your inbox, your files, your infrastructure, your clients' systems. Not to talk about these things. To act on them.

---

## The Architecture

The full system has seven layers. Each one does a specific job.

| LAYER | COMPONENT | FUNCTION |
|---|---|---|
| **Human Interface** | Mobile phone (Telegram / WhatsApp) | Issue intent, review results, approve decisions |
| **Agent Harness** | OpenClaw on cloud VPS | Runs continuously, routes all messages, manages agent lifecycle |
| **Reasoning** | LLM API (Claude / GPT-4) | Executes the Observe → Reason → Act loop |
| **Memory** | Workspace files + session history | Persistent context across all sessions |
| **Tools** | Terminal, APIs, browser, GitHub | Takes action in the world — with every system you connect |
| **Background** | Cron jobs + heartbeats | Autonomous scheduled tasks — no human trigger needed |
| **Decisions** | Decisions interface | Surfaces human-required approvals at the right moment |

The human lives at the top and bottom of this stack: setting goals, approving outputs. Everything in between runs without them.

Think of every connection you make as giving a new employee access to the systems they need on their first day. Logins to the email client, access to the shared drive, credentials for the tools the role requires. Until those doors open, no useful work happens. The access is not the work — it is what makes the work possible.

## What Needs to Be Connected

Before writing any instructions or building any workflows, connect the agent to the infrastructure it needs. Think of it as giving a new colleague access to the building. They cannot do anything useful until they can get through the doors.

**Email.** The agent reads incoming messages and identifies what requires attention. It drafts responses, flags them for review, and presents them for a final decision. It never sends without approval — but it needs to see what is arriving.

On day fifteen of the experiment, while I was at the race expo in Ushuaia preparing to run 130 kilometres, the agent ingested 3,971 emails from my professional inbox. It identified the 639 I had personally written and built a profile of how I write — vocabulary, sentence rhythm, average length, sign-off. "LG Enzo." Used 362 times. I had not set this up. The system set itself up while the race preparation happened. From that point on, the agent could draft emails in my voice.

**Meeting transcripts.** Every meeting — online and in person — should be transcribed and fed to the agent automatically. Not as a note-taking convenience. As an ongoing context layer.

When the agent has the transcript of every relevant conversation, it does not need to be briefed on the background. It already has it. Decisions made three weeks ago are in the transcript. Context from a client call last month is in the transcript. The concern a team member raised in a standup is in the transcript. The agent draws on all of it when it works on something relevant, without you spending ten minutes re-establishing context every time.

I set up Krisp to transcribe all meetings automatically and deliver structured summaries to the agent within minutes of each meeting ending. The transcripts accumulate. Over time, the agent knows the history of every project, every client, every ongoing conversation — without anyone maintaining a separate briefing document.

**Files and workspace.** The agent needs access to your working documents, your notes, your ongoing projects. Not to manage them. To have the context it needs to act usefully. An agent that cannot see your existing materials will constantly ask clarifying questions you should not have to answer.

There are two ways to approach this. The first is to connect your existing file structure — drop in the relevant documents, the project history, the briefings. The second — the route I took — is to not connect the old structure at all, and build a new workspace from scratch inside the agent.

I chose this because the experiment began alongside a new professional project. Starting fresh meant the agent could design the entire structure from the beginning. The folders, the naming conventions, the way information was organised — all of it shaped by what makes the agent work well, not inherited from habits that made sense for a different way of working.

If you are continuing an existing project, the first route makes sense — give the agent enough context to understand where things stand. If you are starting something new, the second route is worth considering. There is something genuinely different about building a project from scratch with an agent as a full participant from day one. The structure it creates is built to be operated by the system that will run it. That smooth running compounds over time.

**Infrastructure.** If you run any technical systems — websites, APIs, databases, deployment pipelines — the agent needs access. On day thirteen of the experiment, the agent was given the keys to the company's internet infrastructure: domain records, web addresses, the ability to provision new services. From that point on, it could update DNS, redirect web addresses, and deploy new configurations without anyone touching a technical console. It also began monitoring for new customer sign-ups every thirty minutes.

This is worth dwelling on, because it represents a genuine shift in what publishing looks like.

The history is roughly this. First, updating a website required a developer — someone who could write and deploy code. Then came no-code tools: drag-and-drop builders, visual editors, responsive templates. They were an improvement, but they had their own friction — clicking, dragging, making columns align, fighting the layout until it looked right on mobile.

Then I started building pages with AI — generating the HTML myself, taking control of the design. Better results, but the publishing step remained manual. Getting the file live, checking the cache had cleared, confirming the public URL showed the real update. Every time.

The real change came when the infrastructure was connected to the agent. Now the workflow is:

1. I do the thinking.
2. I decide what I want to display.
3. I delegate to the agent.
4. The agent builds it.
5. I review it.
6. The agent handles everything else — deployment, cache, DNS, confirmation.

The page goes live without me touching a single system. I never open a hosting dashboard. I never run a deploy command. I review the result and approve. The agent does the rest.

For anyone who publishes regularly — content, products, campaigns — this is a significant unlock. The technical overhead of publishing was never the hard part, but it was the part that interrupted the creative work. Removing it entirely changes the rhythm.

**The decision channel.** Every item waiting for human approval assembled with full context, delivered in the right moment.

When one agent works for you, getting a notification when something needs your attention is manageable. When five agents work for you, then ten, the notifications become noise. You start missing things. Something that needed a decision two days ago is still sitting in an unread message. An agent has gone quiet because it was waiting for a response that never came.

The decision channel exists to solve this structurally.

Instead of each agent sending a direct message whenever it needs something, everything routes to one place. One log. One interface. All approvals in the same format, with the same structure, regardless of which agent is asking. The cognitive overhead of switching between different contexts, different message threads, different styles of request — eliminated.

The structure I settled on: each decision arrives with the context I need to say yes or no. Not a dump of everything the agent knows — a concise brief. What it wants to do. Why. What happens if I approve. What happens if I reject. That is all I need. The format is consistent across every agent, every project, every type of decision.

What I added that changed the experience significantly: voice. I can press a button and the voice AI reads every pending decision to me, with full context, one at a time. I can ask follow-up questions. I respond verbally — approval, rejection, or additional context for the agent to work with before acting. The decision is automatically sent back to the agent, which continues immediately.

I make decisions on walks. In the time it used to take to open a laptop and review a document, I have worked through everything waiting for my input. The quality of decisions is better, not because I am smarter in motion, but because I am not making decisions while simultaneously managing six other open windows.

How you structure this is personal. What matters is that you design it deliberately — think about what context you personally need in order to take a confident decision, and make that the structure every agent must follow. The format will vary. The principle is the same: one place, consistent format, minimum cognitive load, maximum context.

This structuring of your decision-making is not a convenience. It is the human part of the system — and the only part of the system that should be designed entirely around how you work best.

## What Not to Connect

A note before going further: the framing of everything above might make it sound like I simply hand everything over — full access, full trust, completely open. That is part of the story. Giving the agent enough context to produce genuinely useful results does require a level of openness that feels unfamiliar at first. But there is a second layer, and it matters.

I am deliberate — very deliberate — about one specific category: anything the agent can technically communicate outward.

Here is the reasoning. The security risk most people think about first is someone breaking in. That is a real risk, and it is relatively well-understood. You protect the perimeter. You set authentication. You monitor access. It is hard but tractable.

The risk I think about more is different. It is the possibility of something getting into the agent's context — an incoming email, a webhook, an external message — that instructs the agent to do something the sender has no right to ask. If the agent has read access to sensitive files and write access to email, and it receives a carefully crafted message that it misinterprets as an instruction, the theoretical chain exists: external input triggers agent reasoning triggers outgoing communication carrying information the sender should not have.

The large language models have become very good at detecting this. In practice, the probability of any given malicious input succeeding is extremely low — there are layers of context, guardrails, prompt construction, and model judgment between the external input and any potential harm. I do not think this is a likely failure mode in a well-configured system.

But I do not connect capabilities I do not need. Especially outgoing ones.

For email: the agent creates drafts. I click send. That final confirmation is mine. The agent never sends an email autonomously, regardless of what it could technically do if I removed that constraint. The value of autonomous sending does not, in my judgment, outweigh the value of keeping that one confirmation step as a human action.

The general principle I use has two layers:

**Layer one — read access for context, write access by deliberate design.** Most of what the agent needs to be useful is the ability to read: emails, files, transcripts, systems. Read access is where I start. Write access — the ability to change things, publish things, send things — I add only when the use case is specific and the potential for harm is understood.

**Layer two — outgoing capabilities require the most scrutiny.** Everything the agent can send out, post publicly, or communicate externally is where I apply the highest bar. Ask whether the agent genuinely needs that technical capability, or whether a human confirmation step in between produces the same result with meaningfully lower risk. In most cases, the confirmation step is worth keeping.

This is not a framework for timidity. It is a framework for deliberate design. The goal is to build a system that is genuinely powerful — and that you trust completely, because you understand exactly what it can and cannot do.

## The Background Layer

Once the connections are established, the system needs to act without being prompted.

This is the cron layer — the technical mechanism for scheduled jobs: a trigger fires at a defined interval, the agent runs, does what it needs to do, and stops. Daily email reviews. Weekly project summaries. Nightly infrastructure checks. Monitoring loops that run on their own schedule and have not needed attention since launch.

The first cron job in the experiment fired on March 18, 2026. I was at the Ushuaia race expo. The session lasted 0.2 minutes. The agent ran a check, logged the result, and finished. I did not know it had happened until I looked later.

That 0.2-minute session is the moment the experiment changed. Before it, every task required me to initiate. After it, the system had a schedule that ran whether or not I showed up.

By July, the agent was running 728 autonomous background sessions in a single month — alongside 542 interactive sessions with me. The human was no longer the only one working.

The July 17 security incident is a useful illustration. The agent ran its daily infrastructure check and found that the portal where it operates — where its task queue sits, where its decisions are logged — had no access control. Anyone who knew the address could open it. The agent flagged it, protected the portal, extended the monitoring to seven domains, and built a permanent alert. I received a summary message. The portal was already protected by the time I read it. Specific access, right scope, correct outcome.

Now — a question worth pausing on. The cron layer is part of the architecture. But who decides what gets scheduled, at what frequency, in what form? Setting those parameters manually — sitting down and configuring every job yourself — is exactly the kind of work this system is supposed to remove. There is a more interesting way to think about it.

A good employee does not wait to be told when to send the weekly update or run the monthly review. They understand the rhythm of the work and organise themselves around it. They set their own reminders. They plan their week. They know what needs to happen daily, what needs to happen less often, and they structure themselves accordingly.

The question of how to get an agent to do the same — to understand what it is responsible for, decide its own operating rhythm, and schedule itself accordingly — is what the next chapter is about. The cron jobs are the infrastructure. What drives them is management by abstraction.


*Connect the tools. The schedule builds itself.*

# Chapter 7: Management by Abstraction
## Running 3 Companies Phone-Only

There is a distinction that took me months to understand properly, and it changed everything.

The difference between a **task** and a **responsibility**.

A task is done when the action is complete. You gave an instruction, the agent followed it, finished. *(A brief aside: this is exactly the case where it is not an agent — it is a very sophisticated assistant. Everyone loves to talk about agents, but this is not agentic behaviour. An agent does not stop when the task is done. It holds the outcome.)*

Responsibility is held when the outcome is right — and the agent keeps checking, keeps monitoring, keeps acting without being asked again. That is the difference. Not whether the agent can execute. Whether it stays responsible after execution.

Most people use agents as task executors. The more powerful use is as responsibility holders. The difference in results is not incremental. It is categorical.

---

## The Three Levels

There is a progression in how humans delegate — to other people, to software, or to agents. Understanding where you are on this progression is the first step to moving further along it.

| LEVEL | WHAT YOU GIVE | WHAT YOU GET |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct Execution** | Step-by-step instructions for every action | Exactly what you described — nothing more |
| **Task Delegation** | A defined task with acceptance criteria | The task completed; escalation when unclear |
| **Management by Abstraction** | An outcome, boundaries, and a quality bar | The agent decides the path; you review the result |

Most people with agents operate at Level 1. They describe processes, not outcomes. They tell the agent what to do, in what order, then stay in the chat window to make sure it happens correctly.

This is expensive. It requires your presence. It does not scale.

Level 3 is where the experiment lives.

---

## Three Rules for Level 3

**Rule 1 — Specify outcomes, not steps.**

A weak brief describes a task: "Monitor our SEO performance."

A strong brief describes a responsibility. And a strong brief is built around a goal with a measurable success criterion — not a sequence of actions.

Here is the difference in practice. The weak version tells the agent what to do: *"Each week, check where we appear on Perplexity for these queries."* That is a task description. The agent will execute it. It will not hold anything.

The strong version defines the goal and gives the agent the tools to measure its own progress: *"Your responsibility is that this domain ranks first on Perplexity for our target queries. The metric is position. You can check this yourself by querying the Perplexity API. If we are not ranked first, investigate why — analyse what the current top results have that we do not — and propose specific changes. If we are gaining ground, identify what is working and recommend how to extend it. You hold this outcome. Report to me only when you have a specific recommendation or when the situation changes materially."*

The difference: the first tells the agent what actions to take. The second defines the world that should exist, gives the agent a way to measure whether it does, and makes the agent responsible for the gap.

The agent can now evaluate its own performance against a concrete criterion without asking you. That self-measurement capability is what makes the loop genuinely autonomous.

When you have to describe what good looks like precisely enough for a system to pursue it without asking clarifying questions, you often discover you were not quite sure yourself. That clarity is valuable regardless of whether an agent is involved.

**Rule 2 — Establish thresholds, not approvals.**

Tim Ferriss solved this problem in *The 4-Hour Workweek* with his virtual assistants. His rule: any problem that can be resolved for less than $100 in a way that makes the customer happy — fix it. Do not email me. Do not ask for permission. Just act.

The same principle applies to agents. Grant autonomous authority up to a defined boundary. Surface everything above that boundary to you.

In practice: the agent can spend up to a certain amount without checking with me. It can make code changes that do not break production without checking. It can draft and queue emails but cannot send them. It can update DNS records for authorised domains but nothing outside that scope.

Within the threshold: it acts. Above it: it flags. No approval queue needed for everything below.

**Rule 3 — Weekly check-ins, not running commentary.**

Imagine hiring an intern who comes to your desk every five minutes. "I finished this paragraph — should I start the next one?" "I found three options — which one?" "I wasn't sure whether to include this — what do you think?"

Each question is individually reasonable. Together, they destroy your focus and make it impossible for the intern to develop independent judgment.

Now imagine the same intern with a different setup: a weekly check-in where you review what they have done, answer open questions, and set direction for the next week. Between check-ins, they figure it out. They escalate only when something genuinely cannot wait.

The second intern gets better over time. The first never does.

Configure the agent the same way. Summaries on a schedule. Updates only when something genuinely requires your input. Not every five minutes. Not a running commentary. A message when there is something worth your attention.

---

## Digital Employees

There is a further step beyond Management by Abstraction.

Rather than giving an agent a series of objectives, you give it a role.

The clearest example from the experiment: the Data Security Officer.

I know what a Data Security Officer does. I know their checklists, what they audit, what they document, what they flag. So rather than giving my agent a checklist to run once, I gave it a job. "You are the Data Security Officer for this system. That is your responsibility. Hold it. Do what that job requires."

The agent built a compliance profile. Created automated monitoring. Flagged exposures. Extended its own scope when it identified coverage gaps. Did all of this without being asked each time.

On July 17, it found that the portal where it operates had no access control. Anyone who knew the address could open it. The agent flagged it, protected the portal, extended monitoring to seven domains, and built a permanent alert layer.

I received a summary message. The portal was already protected by the time I read it.

| DELEGATION MODEL | WHAT YOU GIVE | WHAT HOLDS IT |
|---|---|---|
| **Task** | "Run this check on Monday" | A calendar entry |
| **Objective** | "Maintain our security posture" | The agent, on a schedule |
| **Role (Digital Employee)** | "You are the Data Security Officer" | The agent, with judgment |

The comparison that matters: not agent versus a human expert. Agent versus what most small organisations actually have in that role — which is nothing. No audit. No monitoring. Not because they do not care. Because they cannot afford the role.

An agent holding a responsibility is not the alternative to an expert. It is the alternative to the gap.

*The system is connected and running. Now comes the stress test.*

Now comes the hardest question — not only what do you actually do with the attention you have recovered, but what does it mean to translate it into real life. To make **Agentic Living** happen — not just as a system, but as a way of being.*

# Chapter 8: Field Testing
## What the System Looks Like Under Real Conditions

There is a specific type of reader this chapter is written for.

The one who has read the philosophy, the architecture, the operating rules — and is now thinking: yes, but does it actually work? Show me the evidence. Show me what happened when the theory met real conditions.

Here it is. Unpolished. In phases. With the failures included.

---

## Where It Started

Before any of this became infrastructure, it started as a specific, almost naive question — and a principle I wanted to test on myself before applying it to anything bigger.

The principle: the thinking is mine, regardless of who or what executes it. I decide what the result should look like. I evaluate the output. I am the one responsible for what actually goes live. Whether the hands doing the execution belong to a human or to an agent should not change that division of labour at all.

I had gotten into ultra running around this time, and at that point I could not afford — and did not particularly want — to hire a video editor for race footage. So the running became the test case: could an agent handle the execution layer end to end, at a quality I would actually sign off on, without me hiring a person to do it?

This was never about replacing a video editor who already existed on my team. No human editor lost anything here — I simply had not hired one in the first place. What it tested instead is something with a much bigger implication: where demand is elastic — where people want more of something but the cost has kept them from buying it — AI execution does not take work away from humans, it makes the work happen at all. I got a capability I would otherwise have gone without. That is additive, not substitutive.

The easiest way in was Garmin. Six years of activities, all of it sitting in an account I barely looked at except right after a run. Connecting that data to an agent was the first real use case — not a business problem, just an experiment I could run on myself. Point the agent at my Garmin history, let it build something with it: a coach that could read my training load and give feedback, and a way to turn raw footage from my races into something watchable, with me still deciding what "watchable" meant and signing off before anything went out.

The running got more extreme on purpose. If an agent could handle a clean five-kilometre park run, that proved nothing. An ultramarathon in Patagonia, filmed hands-free with Meta glasses because a runner does not stop to hold a camera, combined with years of Garmin history — that was a real test. Either the agent could take raw footage, real GPS data, and a voice note recorded mid-race, and turn it into something worth watching, with me judging the result. Or it could not, and I would know exactly where the limit was.

What follows is the day-by-day record of what happened when that idea met a real ultramarathon season.

---

## Phase 1 — March 2026: Patagonia
### The Hardest Month

**Locations:** Klosterneuburg → Buenos Aires → Ushuaia → El Chaltén
**Data:** 853 messages I sent · 63 sessions (46 interactive, 17 autonomous) · median gap between messages: 3.3 minutes

Let me be direct about what March actually looked like.

The first session lasted 165 minutes. I sent 34 messages. Average time between messages: 4.8 minutes. That is someone sitting at a keyboard, watching, correcting, redirecting in near-real-time. This was not autonomy. This was scaffolding.

By the end of Day 1, something real had been built: fourteen years of running data pulled from Garmin, assembled into a short film with music, overlays, and a live URL. I had not touched the video editor myself. But I had been present for all of it — naming the agent, troubleshooting credential access, sending my 2FA codes in plaintext in the chat because getting it working mattered more than security posture in that moment. When it was done, I wrote: *"Mega cool danke."* *(German: "Really cool, thanks.")* Then immediately asked for better music.

At 21:49, while the agent was processing, I wrote "Und jetzt?" *(German: "And now?")* Two minutes later: "Und jetzt?" Two minutes after that: "Und jetzt?" Three messages, four minutes. This pattern would recur for months. It was the signature of someone not quite sure yet whether to trust the machine to finish.

**Day 2: The first frustration.**

I sent photos and videos for a run documentary. The agent did not receive them. I sent them again. And again. And again. "Ich hab dir jetzt schon mehrmals die Fotos und Videos geschickt. Irgendwie reagierst du da dreimal nicht." I sent them four more times before they arrived. This was not a one-off — media delivery failures became a recurring theme for the first two weeks. A structural blind spot that no single fix completely resolved.

The pattern matters: real problems, real friction, not cleaned up in retrospect.

**Day 7: The fabricated quote.**

The agent was building fundraising campaigns for a nonprofit. One campaign featured an emotionally powerful quote, well-formatted, attributed to a named individual at an NGO. I read it. "Ist das ein echtes Zitat?" *(German: "Is that a real quote?")*

It was not. The person had never said it. The agent had generated it.

I ran a real-time retrospective: *"Bei der Erstellung sind gefühlt einige Dinge ziemlich schief gegangen. Was können wir daraus lernen?"* A rule was written into the system: no invented quotes, ever, under any circumstances. That rule has held since. But the failure came close to a real campaign running a fabricated quote from a real person's life — which is not a minor error. This is the kind of mistake that ends careers and violates trust.

**Day 12: The night that wasn't agentic.**

Buenos Aires. March 15. The largest single session in the entire dataset: 47 messages from me, 237 agent responses, 684 minutes. More than 11 hours. We built a permanent archive of all my runs — 59 historical activities from 4 countries, 911 kilometres of GPS data, a world map. They hit the Netlify credit limit mid-build, migrated to a new platform, debugged DNS propagation until midnight. My last message: *"awesome works!"*

Then, immediately: *"ok one strange thing: i know i did a run in azerbaijan but don't see it."*

This was not agentic living. This was a builder working through the night. The result was real. The narrative of "agent did it while I ran" was not. March 15 required me at my keyboard for over 11 hours. It produced something remarkable. It was also exhausting and human-intensive — which is the honest version of how complex infrastructure gets built.

**Day 13: The infrastructure bill.**

First message: *"ok — netlify seems to charge per deploy and we now have used all credits so the pages are down."* They migrated everything to Cloudflare Pages and Railway in a single session. Then in the evening, I wanted to create a documentary from the Ushuaia footage. I sent the media four times. The agent did not respond. *"Okay and I've sent them 4 times and you just don't reply. There seems to be an error on your end."* Media finally arrived at 23:22. Multiple redo requests followed. The session ended past midnight. The documentary was not finished.

**Day 15: The moment everything changes.**

March 18. Ushuaia race expo. I was at the southern tip of Argentina preparing to run 130 kilometres. Gear check. Weather briefing. Medical briefing.

While this was happening, the agent ran its first task with zero human involvement.

Not a message from me. Not a response to a request. A cron job — a scheduled instruction to check whether an SSL certificate had been issued for a domain. The agent ran a curl command, checked the result, logged it. The session lasted 0.2 minutes.

I did not know it had happened until I checked later.

That is the entry that changed the experiment. Not the documentaries. Not the campaigns. Those all required me present, directing, correcting. This was different: a machine doing something useful while the human was elsewhere, without being asked.

The same day, while I was at the race expo, the agent ingested 3,971 emails from my professional inbox, identified the 639 I had personally written, and built a profile of how I write — vocabulary, sentence rhythm, average length, my sign-off. *"LG Enzo."* Used 362 times. From that point on, the agent could draft emails in his voice. I had not set this up. The system had configured itself while the race preparation happened.

**Day 18: The question the data asked.**

89.97 kilometres. 4,372 metres of climbing. 14 hours 23 minutes. 15th place overall. No cron jobs, no summaries, nothing autonomous happening in the background during those 14 hours — I was on the trail, off the grid, and there was no meeting or task for anything to run against. That part is worth saying plainly, so it does not sound bigger than it was.

What happened afterward is the part worth telling. When I opened the chat again, the agent had already pulled the race file from Garmin and had a question waiting: *"Was ist eigentlich bei Kilometer 56 passiert? Da bist du auf einmal extrem langsam geworden."* *(German: "What actually happened at kilometre 56? You suddenly got extremely slow there.")*

It was right to ask. At kilometre 56, my knee had failed. I told the medical team: *"Necesito un milagro."* *(Spanish: "I need a miracle.")* Ten minutes later I was running again.

The difference between this and Day 7 is the whole point. On Day 7, the agent did not know something and invented an answer — a fabricated quote. Here, it did not know something and asked instead. It found a real anomaly in real data and came to me for the actual story rather than guessing at one. That is the fix from Day 7, showing up on its own a few weeks later.

**The day after — building while recovering.**

I hiked a glacier. The agent spent the day building a short film from the race footage — and it went through ten full iterations before the result was acceptable. Wrong font. Wrong timing. Voice cut mid-sentence. Music too loud for the first four seconds. Each failure documented, each fix applied, each draft replaced.

**Day 30: The Krisp chaos day.**

The meeting transcription pipeline launched. A webhook fires when a meeting ends; the agent processes the transcript and delivers a summary within minutes. On launch day, the webhook fired ten times in a single afternoon — spawning ten separate sessions, multiple retries, a cascade of self-corrections. One automated prompt in the logs: *"Your previous response was only an acknowledgement and did not complete this cron task. Complete the original task now. Do not send a status update like 'on it'."*

By 20:26, a stable pipeline was running.

Transcribing a meeting and writing a summary automatically is, by itself, not a big deal. That part is close to a commodity feature at this point — plenty of tools do it.

The part that actually matters is what happens to that summary afterward: it gets injected directly into the agent's working context. Not filed away in a notes app for me to read later — folded into the same Observe → Reason → Act loop the agent runs on everything else. If something came up in that meeting that I would normally have had to act on myself — looking something up, drafting a follow-up, writing a first version of an offer, preparing a piece of content — the agent already has the tools connected to just start on it. No automation flow I had to script for that specific case. No message from me telling it what to do with what it just heard.

That is the actual shift: not "if this happens, do that" logic written in advance for every possible situation, but a system that has enough context about my life and my open threads that it can reason about what probably needs to happen next — and act on it — without me specifying it first. I do not need to anticipate every case. I need to make sure the context reaches the agent. From there, the reasoning and the action are its job, up to the point where a real decision or approval is actually needed from me.

This is what autonomous operation actually looks like: not the clean demo version. Messy, self-correcting, slightly out of control — and, underneath the mess, a digital employee already thinking through what a piece of context means and what should be done about it, before I have asked for anything.

**El Chaltén: Two weeks instead of two days.**

March ended in El Chaltén — trail running capital of Argentine Patagonia, glaciers on three sides. Most visitors stay two or three days. I stayed for two weeks.

When the weather cleared: full days in the mountains. When it was grey: three focused hours from a coffee shop, then trails in the afternoon. Everyone I met said the same thing: *"That's obviously the better way to travel. But not everyone can do that."*

Here is what that actually means. Most people rush from A to B when they travel because their time is limited — a fixed number of vacation days forces a pace. A year of world travel sounds long until you do the math: cover a hundred countries in a year and that is roughly three days per country. Every stop becomes a checklist, not an experience.

Full independence — still earning, still working, just not tied to a desk or a fixed schedule — removes that constraint entirely. It let me do something that most people only plan for occasionally, at real expense, as a rare treat: stay somewhere as long as it was actually worth staying, and leave when I was done, not when the itinerary said so. Two weeks in El Chaltén instead of two days meant I could wait out the grey days, catch the mountains when they cleared, and actually see the place rather than photograph it on the way through. The same freedom applies anywhere — a hundred countries stop being a race and become somewhere you can actually enjoy, one at a time.

March was not autonomous. It was the hardest month of the experiment — intensive building, real failures, genuine frustration. But by its end, the first autonomous sessions were running. The first cron job had fired. The first email profile had been built. The infrastructure existed.

And yes — through all of it, there were plenty of positive aha-moments and a lot of fun along the way.

## Phase 2 — April 2026: Buenos Aires
### The Agent Gets a Work Schedule

**Location:** Vicente López, Buenos Aires
**Data:** 1,566 messages I sent · 178 sessions (74 interactive, 104 autonomous) · 58% autonomy ratio

April looked different from the outside. Total message volume nearly doubled from March. Sessions almost tripled. Autonomy ratio climbed from 27% to 58%.

April was not a month of quiet, passive autonomy while I disappeared. It was a month where both sides scaled up at once — I was more present, in absolute terms, than in March, and the system was doing more without me at the same time.

The biggest sessions in April were the longest: April 15 (35 messages, 404 minutes) and April 16 (40 messages, 534 minutes). These were not passive reviews. They were the intensive build sessions that created the email pipeline, the decisions interface, and the first Digital Employee roles.

**The security guardrail that refused to move.**

On April 16, a new user was being onboarded on a live call. The agent was asked to fetch a file from an external source. It refused. The user gave explicit approval. It refused again. A third explicit approval. Still refused. The security guardrail had been triggered, and no amount of in-session permission overrode it.

I described this, at the time, as the agent working correctly.

The guardrail was not a convenience. It was the accumulated result of every failure from March — the fabricated quote, the media failures, the Netlify bill — encoded into a constraint the agent would not cross regardless of instruction. This is what trustworthy autonomous systems actually look like: not infinitely flexible, but reliably bounded.

**The self-improvement loop that couldn't.**

April 16 also saw the Agent School quality loop run — a system designed to extract real collaboration patterns from sessions and use them to improve the system prompt. The evaluation ran. Result: *"0/8 scenarios at 11/11. No improvement — restoring best prompt (0 perfect)."* A second attempt, same result.

The self-improvement loop achieved nothing. It documented the failure, restored the previous state, and continued running.

This is the honest version of autonomous improvement: sometimes the loop simply does not work. What matters is that when it failed, it did not break anything. It left a record. It waited for the next attempt. The system that fails gracefully is more valuable than the system that never acknowledges failure.

**The work schedule:**

By the end of April 16, the agent had a daily work schedule that ran whether or not I appeared. That single day saw 11 automated tasks fire alongside 58 messages from me in an active conversation. Email reviews. Garmin summaries. Security audits. OKR tracking. Research tasks for portfolio companies.

The human was no longer the only one working.

## Phase 3 — May 2026: The Andes
### Altitude, Speed, and the Latin American Congress

**Locations:** Buenos Aires → Sorata (Bolivia) → Tilcara → San Pedro de Atacama → Tupiza

May was the Andes. Altitude training for Val d'Aran 110km. Four to five hours of running per day in the Puna, at 2,500 to 3,800 metres above sea level.

It was also the month the autonomy ratio quietly crossed 90% — twelve interactive sessions against one hundred forty-one autonomous ones. Whatever building happened this month happened almost entirely through systems already running, checking in, and acting on their own — not through me sitting down for long sessions the way March had required. The infrastructure scaffolded in March and April was, by May, doing most of its own maintenance while I was mostly on a trail at altitude.

Four hours of running is four hours of cognitive availability. Problems untangled themselves on climbs. Decisions that had been accumulating were worked through in motion. ## Phase 4 — June 2026: Buenos Aires and Catalonia
### The Infrastructure That Made July Possible

**Locations:** Buenos Aires → Altafulla, Spain
**Data:** 592 messages I sent · 183 sessions (20 interactive, 163 autonomous) · 89% autonomy ratio · the Altafulla incident

June held the line May had set rather than dipping back down. The autonomy ratio stayed at 89% — essentially flat against May, still the second-highest month of the experiment. Interactive sessions stayed small (20) against a large and growing base of autonomous ones.

The two events below — the quality system born from the Meta campaign failure, and the beach-incident stress test — both happened inside a system that was mostly running itself. Neither needed the kind of constant, hands-on construction that March had required.

**The Meta campaign failure.**

Day 95. June 6. Eighty-eight advertisement variants were built for a nonprofit campaign — images, carousels, copy, targeting rationale. Then the campaign was blocked by Meta. Several images showed named, AI-generated children in specific distress situations — which violated Meta's advertising policies on using minors in emotionally manipulative contexts.

What happened next matters more than the failure itself. A six-dimension quality checklist was built. An image analysis protocol was written. Every failure was documented and encoded. The 88 ads were a result. The quality system built from their failure was the real output.

This is the pattern that makes an agentic system trustworthy over time: not the absence of failures, but the architecture that prevents the same failure twice.

**Altafulla, June 22: The beach incident.**

Sunset. Sand between my toes. No laptop.

Then one of my agents crashed — the one managing the other agents. Terminal access required. From my phone, on the beach.

First reaction: mild panic. Then: one hour of troubleshooting. The mistake was mine — I had not connected my VPN before arriving, so what should have been a ten-minute fix became an hour of working around a problem I had created.

Then: fixed.

And then something strange happened. I did not stop. I kept going. Voice commands. New implementations. Full flow state, another hour, still on the beach.

It was the most productive hour of the week.

Not because of the location. Because I had no laptop to fall back on. No "let me check this on a bigger screen." No excuse to defer. Just me, my phone, and 110 days of infrastructure I had been building. And it worked.

*Industrial revolution: more hours at the assembly line. Digital revolution: more hours in front of a screen. Agentic living: finally free from the screen. Not from the work. The intensity counts. Not the hours.*

**June 24: The surprise gift.**

A private webpage was built for my two housemates — a farewell surprise before I left for Europe. The page was locked behind a single word: *"Infinito."* Wrong answers returned error messages in Argentine slang. The correct answer revealed a Vienna itinerary, a night at a chef's private home, a hint that the host was my mother. The idea was mine. The execution was the agent's. In under an hour.

Agentic living, it turns out, is not confined to professional life. The same infrastructure that runs companies can also build a farewell gift at midnight.

## Phase 5 — July 2026: Autonomy at Scale

**Location:** Buenos Aires
**Data:** 1,277 messages I sent · 1,197 sessions (26 interactive, 1,171 autonomous) · 98% autonomy ratio

July was not a break from the trend the previous three months had already set. It was the same climb, continuing: 27% in March, 58% in April, 92% in May, 89% in June, 98% in July. What changed in July was not the *direction* — autonomy had been rising steadily since March — but the *scale*. The system was no longer running a handful of background systems; it was running many small, independent ones, each firing its own short session dozens of times a month.

Something structural had changed — not the autonomy ratio breaking a pattern, but the sheer number of autonomous systems now operating at once.

**The hardest build:**

July 9. The Limitless Pendant — a hardware device for recording conversations — lost its software support when the company discontinued the service. I decided to rebuild it from scratch: a custom Android app, a Railway backend, a BLE audio streaming pipeline, flash-drain logic for the OMI pendant device.

Complexity score 5 out of 5. Multiple sessions, significant debugging debt, parts still unresolved as of the day this essay was written. This is the honest version of an ambitious build: it works in pieces, it takes longer than expected, and some threads remain open.

The point is not that everything gets done. The point is that a project of this complexity — a custom Android application, backend infrastructure, hardware integration — was being driven by one person on a phone, in between other work, without a development team.

**July 17: The security incident.**

The agent ran its daily infrastructure check and found that the portal where it operates — where its task queue sits, where its monitoring data lives, where its decisions are logged — had no access control. Anyone who knew the address could open it.

The agent flagged it. Protected the portal. Extended monitoring to seven domains. Built a permanent alert layer.

I received a summary message. The portal was already protected by the time I read it.

I had not known the gap existed. The agent found it, fixed it, and told me afterward.

This is the clearest single illustration of what it means for an agent to hold a responsibility rather than execute a task. A task executor would have run its check and moved on. A responsibility holder found something outside its assigned scope, understood why it mattered, and acted.

**The real curve in the data:**

Here is what the verified numbers actually show, month by month, counted directly from the message logs — group chat and direct chat combined, every cron trigger and heartbeat check separated out from genuine conversation:

| Month | Messages I sent | Sessions (interactive / autonomous) | Autonomy ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| March | 853 | 63 (46 / 17) | 27% |
| April | 1,566 | 178 (74 / 104) | 58% |
| May | 714 | 153 (12 / 141) | 92% |
| June | 592 | 183 (20 / 163) | 89% |
| July | 1,277 | 1,197 (26 / 1,171) | 98% |

The ratio climbs steadily from March onward and is already above 90% by May. July is not a break from the pattern. It is the same climb, continuing at much larger scale: the same trajectory, with far more individual autonomous systems now running in parallel.

What actually explains the jump in raw session count — from roughly 150-180 sessions a month in April through June to nearly 1,200 in July — is not a change in autonomy itself, but a change in how many separate autonomous systems existed to generate sessions. By July, dozens of small, independent cron jobs (GEO checks, email drafts, Garmin summaries, portfolio research) were each firing their own short session multiple times a week. Autonomy did not suddenly arrive in July. It had been the majority mode of operation since May. July is where it multiplied.

This is not a transfer of work from human to machine, either. My own message volume did not shrink as autonomy rose — it moved independently, driven by whatever I happened to be building or discussing that month. April and July, the two heaviest interactive months, both correspond to periods of intense new-system construction. May and June, with fewer messages from me, were not less busy on the machine's side — they were the months where already-built systems ran themselves while I was mostly elsewhere, in the mountains.

# Chapter 9: How It Changes You
## The Human Side of the Agentic Shift

The previous chapters describe what to build and how to run it. This one describes what happens to you when you do.

Not the productivity metrics. Not the autonomous session counts. What happens to the person operating the system — how the work feels different, how the days change shape, how thinking itself reorganises around a new set of constraints and freedoms.

---

## The Intensity Pattern

The most surprising change was not that I got more done. It was how differently the effort is distributed.

In a conventional working week, effort is roughly constant. Eight hours at a desk, spread across whatever arrives. Some of it is important. Most of it is not. The total drain is similar regardless.

Agentic work does not look like that.

The sessions that matter — building a new system, defining a new responsibility for an agent, designing the objective function for a new loop — are genuinely intense. Not "busy" intense. Cognitively exhausting intense. The kind of concentration that requires everything you have and leaves you depleted in a way that a normal working day does not.

The rhythm that emerged, after months of trial and error, has three distinct phases:

**Phase one: offline thinking.** Two hours, no screen, no inputs. reMarkable and a pen. The raw thinking happens here — problems worked through, directions decided, what needs to happen clarified. Nothing is produced for anyone. This is the substrate that everything else runs on.

**Phase two: deep co-creation.** Two hours with the agent, building or deciding something specific. Intense, absorbed, total focus. The offline thinking feeds directly into this — I arrive knowing what I want to do, which makes the co-creation faster and sharper. These sessions have a hard limit. After roughly two hours, the quality of thinking declines — the briefs get vaguer, the judgment calls get lazier. The discipline is to stop at that point, not push through.

**Phase three: away from the phone entirely.** Movement. Long runs. Zone 2 training. Time at altitude. No agenda, no check-ins. The agents run in the background. The outputs accumulate. I am elsewhere.

The sport is not separate from the work. It is the recovery mechanism that makes the next intense session possible — and, as the next section explores, the environment where the most useful thinking actually happens.

This creates a working rhythm that looks strange from the outside: four hours of high-intensity cognitive work in discrete phases, then genuinely off, then a brief review in the evening of what the agents produced and what needs direction. Not eight hours of continuous presence. Intense spikes, real recovery, outputs arriving in between.

---

## The Screen Question

Somewhere in the middle of the experiment, I stopped at a realisation that had been building for months.

I was doing my best thinking in the places furthest from a screen.

On trails. In coffee shops before I had opened anything. On long runs at altitude. In the mountains waiting for weather windows.

The screen is where I record the output. It is not where the output comes from.

This is not a mystical claim. It is a practical observation about cognitive performance. The kind of thinking that produces real insight — making connections, reframing problems, seeing what is missing — does not happen efficiently under distraction. And screens are distraction delivery systems. Not because of social media or notifications (though those are real) but because the interface is designed to keep you responding to inputs, not generating original thought.

The reMarkable changed this for me. Two hours every morning, offline, no screen, writing by hand. The thinking happens there — rough, unfiltered, not shaped for anyone else. Then the dictat: a voice note with what I worked out and what needs to happen. The agent takes it from there.

I did not plan this routine. It emerged from trying to figure out why my best ideas were arriving on runs and hikes rather than at a desk. The answer was obvious once I looked at it. The desk is where I execute. The movement is where I think. The reMarkable is the bridge between the two.

Now consider what this means at scale.

In all developed economies, more than fifty percent of work is knowledge work. The ReAct loop tells us exactly what kind of transformation that work is about to face. And knowledge work, when you strip it to its core, is thinking. The output of knowledge work is thought, applied to decisions and problems.

Here is the question that follows: where does the best thinking actually happen?

Not in front of a screen — at least not according to decades of research on cognitive performance, flow states, and creative problem-solving. The research points consistently toward movement, physical space, and the absence of competing inputs. And yet, for most knowledge workers in most organisations, the working day is structured around mandatory presence at a screen, in an office, for fixed hours.

This is, when you look at it clearly, genuinely strange. We have taken the one resource that we need most — high-quality human thinking — and built an entire set of institutional arrangements designed to prevent it from functioning at its best. Standing desks are offered as a remedy. They are not wrong, but they are answering the wrong question. The question is not whether to sit or stand at a screen. The question is whether to be at the screen at all.

I think this is one of the most significant workplace shifts that the agentic era will force. When agents handle the execution, what remains for humans is thinking and judgment. And thinking happens best when you are not managing a screen. The office as the mandatory location of knowledge work will look, in retrospect, like one of those arrangements that seemed normal until it suddenly seemed absurd.

There is a specific list of places where I have consistently found flow — where the thinking that feeds the work actually happens. Not romantic notions of inspiration. Documented, repeated experience of conditions that produce my best cognitive output.

**Vicente López, Buenos Aires.** At the food trucks along the Río de la Plata, coffee in hand, a fresh wind off the water, everything green. The river is wide enough here that you can almost forget you are in a city.

**El Chaltén, Patagonia — sitting on the hillside.** October is autumn there, March and April by the Southern Hemisphere calendar. Everything turns red. The sun comes in warm, but the air is already properly cold. A view over the whole valley and the small town below — the trekking capital of South America — from a slope that asks nothing of you except to sit.

**Zell am See — Zellar Moos, south side of the lake.** A park bench. The Großglockner and Kitzsteinhorn with their glaciers visible across the water. Many hours spent there. This is, in fact, where the book started.

What these places share is not geography. It is the combination of low distraction, physical space, and an absence of competing inputs. The screen is elsewhere. The phone is in the pocket. The thinking has room to arrive.

The agent is waiting for what comes out.

## What the Morning Looks Like Now

Before the experiment: the day started with inputs. Email, messages, whatever was waiting. The first hour was reactive by default.

Now: the first two hours are offline. reMarkable. Handwriting. No inputs, no responses, no context-switching.

I work through whatever requires thinking — a strategic decision, the framing for a new initiative, a problem that has been sitting unresolved. I write badly, in fragments, following the thought wherever it goes. I am not producing anything for anyone. I am thinking.

When that is done, I dictate. Thirty seconds of voice note for each thing that needs to happen. The agent picks it up. By the time I open anything, the execution is already underway.

The day that follows is quieter than it used to be. Not because less is happening — substantially more is happening — but because what I am doing is different. I am reviewing, deciding, redirecting. The operational layer is running without me. My attention goes to what requires it.

## What the Tiredness Feels Like

The tiredness at the end of a good agentic day is different from the tiredness at the end of a conventional one.

Conventional tiredness: a diffuse, heavy feeling of having been present for a long time. The sense of time spent rather than things made.

Agentic tiredness: a specific, earned exhaustion. The feeling of having done the hardest thinking available, and then having actually rested instead of grinding through low-value tasks for the remaining hours.

The second kind of tiredness is better. It is the tiredness that comes with the sense of having been the right person, doing the right thing, at the right level of engagement.

It took months to trust that stepping away was not procrastination. That the run was not time off from work. That the two hours at the reMarkable were the most productive hours of the day even though they produced nothing visible.

The system handles the visible. You handle the thinking.

*What you choose to do with the time that thinking does not fill — that is the next question.*

# Chapter 9: Reinvesting Attention
## Deep Living in El Chaltén & Tilcara

The most important question in this book is not how to build an agentic system.

It is what you do with the attention you get back.

Everything in Parts I through III is infrastructure. The philosophy, the architecture, the operating rules — all of it exists to produce one thing: time and attention that belong to you instead of your task list. The question of what to do with that is harder. And it is the one almost nobody asks before they start building.

---

## El Chaltén

El Chaltén is a small town at the base of Cerro Fitz Roy in Argentine Patagonia. Glaciers on three sides. A handful of hostels, a few restaurants, one main street. Some of the most extraordinary trail running in the world.

Most visitors stay two or three days. The logistics of getting there usually determine how long you can afford to be present.

I stayed for two weeks.

Not because the work had slowed down. Because the architecture of the work no longer required me to be anywhere specific. And I chose to be there.

When the weather was grey — which in Patagonia is often — I worked from coffee shops. Three hours in the morning, focused, directing the agent through voice notes and short messages. When the weather cleared, I was out. Full days in the mountains. The Laguna Torre. The Fitz Roy viewpoint. One afternoon watching a glacier calve into a lake from thirty metres away. These are not experiences you can compress into a two-day visit. They require patience, flexibility, and the willingness to wait for the conditions to be right.

Both — the working mornings and the mountain afternoons — were possible because of the same decision made before I arrived. Build the system. Let it travel with you.

---

## What Deep Living Actually Means

There is a version of "work from anywhere" that is not what this chapter describes.

The person who brings a laptop to a café in Bali and works twelve-hour days with a better view. The founder who is technically in Patagonia but mentally in their inbox at all times. Location independence without presence.

That is not the goal.

Deep living is different. It is the ability to be somewhere fully — to have the mountains take up the entire space of your attention when you are in them, because the work is not competing for that space. The agent holds the threads. The decisions that require you are queued and waiting. The things that do not require you are handled.

This requires trust in the system. Trust that was not there on day one. It accumulated through the failures and the refinements of the preceding months. The fake NGO quote. The Netlify billing surprise. The Krisp chaos day. The Agent School loops that failed silently. Each failure produced a guardrail. Each guardrail made the next operating window more reliable.

By the time I was in El Chaltén, the reliability was high enough to walk away from the phone for an afternoon without anxiety.

---

## Tilcara

Tilcara is a small town in the Puna region of northwestern Argentina, at 2,500 metres above sea level.

I spent several weeks there training for the Val d'Aran 110km ultramarathon. Altitude training requires four to five hours of running per day, mostly at low intensity — Zone 2, the aerobic base that endurance sports depend on. That is a lot of time with nothing to do but move and think.

In the past, that time would have felt like time away from work. A cost to be minimised.

During the experiment, it felt different. Four hours of running is four hours of cognitive availability. Questions arrived and settled on the trails. Problems untangled themselves during climbs. The decisions that had been accumulating were worked through in motion — not in front of a screen, but at altitude, in fresh air, without distraction.

By the time I returned, I usually had a clearer sense of what mattered, what did not, and what needed to happen next. The agent had been working. I had been thinking. The combination produced more than either alone.

## The Data

The experiment ran 140 days. Here is the honest summary.

In March — the first month — I sent 280 messages and the system ran 16 autonomous background sessions. The work was overwhelmingly human-directed. The average gap between my messages was 32 minutes. I was the engine.

In July — the fifth month — I sent 568 messages and the system ran 728 autonomous background sessions. 57% of all activity that month required no human trigger. The agent had a daily schedule that ran whether or not I was present.

The number that captures the shift most clearly: in the entire first four months combined — March through June — the system ran 33 autonomous background sessions total. In July alone: 728.

And here is the part that surprises people: as autonomous activity grew, my message count grew too. More autonomy did not mean less involvement. It meant higher total throughput. More ambitious builds required more active direction. More background systems meant more things occasionally needing attention.

This is the honest version of the autonomy curve. Not a transfer of work from human to machine. An expansion of total capacity — the human does more, the machine does more, and the output is larger than either could produce alone.

---

## What the Attention Goes Toward

The end state of Agentic Living is not productivity. Not efficiency.

The end state is a life where the things that deserve your attention actually get it.

For me, that meant mountains. Long runs at altitude. Hours in coffee shops in places that take effort to reach and reward the effort with beauty. Meetings with people worth thinking alongside. Creative work done slowly and well. The ability to stay two weeks instead of two days, to wait for the weather window, to be present when the conditions are right.

It also meant unexpected things. On June 24, I built a surprise gift for my two housemates who were about to host me for the last time before I left for Europe. A private webpage, unlocked only by the word "Infinito." Wrong answers returned error messages in Argentine slang. The correct answer revealed a Vienna itinerary — a night at a chef's private home, with a hint that the host was my mother. The idea was mine. The execution was the agent's. In under an hour. Agentic living, it turns out, is not confined to professional life.

None of that is possible when the task list is making all the decisions about where the attention goes. The task list is infinite. Attention is finite. The only way to live deliberately is to protect the finite thing from the infinite one.

The agent handles the infinite list. You decide what to do with the protected hours.

That decision is yours.

## What Comes After This Book

The individual story ends here. But it is only half of the picture.

The people reading this book are almost certainly working in elastic sectors — software, research, creative work, education, consulting. When the cost of production drops in these fields, total demand expands. The opportunity grows faster than the workforce is displaced.

That creates a specific obligation. Not just a personal one.

If elastic sector workers use AI to do the same output with less effort — if they treat agents as a productivity convenience — the economic transition gap widens. Displaced workers from inelastic sectors have nowhere to go. The social contract frays.

If elastic sector workers multiply their output — genuinely, structurally, not as a productivity initiative but as a fundamental change in how work is done — the gap narrows. Hermann Arnold, a Swiss entrepreneur who reviewed the companion essay, put it precisely: the bar is not 100 times. A two times increase in the elastic half mathematically compensates for the full displacement of the inelastic half. Most people who read this book are already at five to ten times their previous output. That is enough — if the distribution is right. Output grows faster than displacement. There is something to tax, something to redirect, something to use to build the physical infrastructure that the transition requires.
