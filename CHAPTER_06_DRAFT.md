# Chapter 6: On Translation — Making the Freed Hours Real

---

Here is the hardest question in this entire book. Not how to build the system — by now you have the tools to do that. Not how to nominate the first responsibility — you have the framework.

The hardest question is: once the system is running and the hours start coming back to you, what do you actually do with them?

This is not a small question. Most people who arrive at Level 1 expecting freedom discover something unexpected: the time that was previously consumed by execution does not automatically fill with better work. It fills with whatever is nearby. More email. More meetings. More responding to inputs. The same kind of low-quality busyness they had delegated, now replicated by choice rather than necessity.

Translation is the practice that prevents this. It is the answer to the question of what to do with what the system returns — and it is the reason this framework has a T at the end.

---

## Where Your Best Thinking Actually Happens

Let me ask you something before we go further.

Think of the last time you had a genuinely original idea. A real one — something you had not read somewhere, something that came from connecting things in a new way. Where were you?

Almost certainly not at your desk.

Over the course of the experiment, I noticed a pattern I could not ignore. My best thinking was arriving in specific places: on trails, in coffee shops before I had opened anything, on long runs at altitude, in the mountains waiting for weather windows. Almost never in front of a screen.

This is not a mystical claim. It is a practical observation about cognitive performance. The kind of thinking that produces real insight — making connections, reframing problems, seeing what is missing — does not happen efficiently under distraction. And screens are distraction delivery systems. Not because of social media or notifications (though those are real), but because the interface is designed to keep you responding to inputs rather than generating original thought.

The screen is where I record the output. It is not where the output comes from.

Understanding this changed everything about how I structure my days.

## The Three Phases

The rhythm that emerged, after months of trial and error, has three distinct phases. None of them looks like a conventional working day.

**Phase one: offline thinking.** Two hours, no screen, no inputs. For me this is a reMarkable and a pen — I find that writing by hand, on a device with no notifications, creates exactly the right conditions. But this is personal. For someone else it might be a notebook, a walk with no phone, a coffee shop before anything is opened. The specific tool matters less than the principle: no inputs, no responses, no screen demanding attention. The raw thinking happens here — problems worked through, directions decided, what needs to happen clarified. Nothing is produced for anyone. This is the substrate that everything else runs on.

**Phase two: deep co-creation.** Two hours with the agent, building or deciding something specific. Intense, absorbed, total focus. The offline thinking feeds directly into this — I arrive knowing what I want to do, which makes the co-creation faster and sharper. These sessions have a hard limit. After roughly two hours, the quality of judgment declines — the briefs get vaguer, the calls get lazier. The discipline is to stop at that point and not push through.

**Phase three: away from the phone entirely.** For me this is movement — long runs, Zone 2 training, time at altitude. That is my version. For you it might be something completely different: a drive, a swim, cooking, a long walk, an afternoon with no agenda. The point is not the activity. The point is that you are genuinely elsewhere, not half-present. The agents run in the background. The outputs accumulate. You are not managing them.

I run ultras, which means I spend a lot of time training at low intensity — what coaches call Zone 2. Heart rate controlled, legs moving, mind free. I stopped seeing this as time away from work a long time ago. It became the best thinking time available to me, because the systems were in place and acting on what I thought required only a few minutes, not a full session. But the principle — not the sport — is what matters here. Find the activity that gets you genuinely off the screen. The thinking will follow.

This creates a working rhythm that looks strange from the outside: four hours of high-intensity cognitive work in two discrete phases, then genuinely off, then a brief review in the evening of what the agents produced and what needs direction. Not eight hours of continuous presence. Intense spikes, real recovery, outputs arriving in between.

## The Bridge

The reMarkable is not a productivity tool. It is a thinking tool. The distinction matters.

For me, the bridge is the reMarkable. Two hours every morning, offline, writing by hand. The thinking happens there — rough, unfiltered, not shaped for anyone else. When I am ready to act, I record a voice note and send it to the agent: what I worked out, what needs to happen. Raw thinking goes in. Structured output comes back. The agent turns what I said — sometimes haltingly, sometimes badly articulated — into a brief, a document, a plan, a draft. Not because it understood my thinking better than I did, but because it handles the structuring and formatting that would otherwise pull me out of thinking mode and into execution mode.

Your bridge will look different. It might be a voice note recorded on a walk. A written note in a regular notebook, photographed and sent. A quick brain dump into a chat window after your morning coffee. What matters is that you have a consistent way to take the thinking that happened offline and hand it to the agent — so the gap between insight and action stays small.

I did not plan this routine. It emerged from noticing that my best ideas arrived on runs and hikes, not at a desk. The desk is where I execute. The movement is where I think. The bridge is how the two connect. Find your version of all three.

## What Translation Is For

Consider what this means at scale.

In all developed economies, more than fifty percent of work is knowledge work. Knowledge work, when you strip it to its core, is thinking. The output of knowledge work is thought, applied to decisions and problems.

And yet, for most knowledge workers, the working day is structured around mandatory presence at a screen, in an office, for fixed hours. We have taken the one resource we need most — high-quality human thinking — and built an entire set of institutional arrangements designed to prevent it from functioning at its best.

This is one of the shifts the agentic era will force. When agents handle the execution, what remains for humans is thinking and judgment. And thinking happens best when you are not managing a screen.

Translation is the practice of creating the conditions for your best thinking — and then feeding that thinking back into the system through the dictation bridge. Not working less. Working differently. Fewer hours in execution mode. More hours in the conditions where the thinking that actually matters happens.

The system runs. Your job is to think well enough to direct it.

## Your Sixth Step

Design your Translation rhythm. Not the ideal version — the version you can start this week.

Open an AI conversation and type this:

> *"I want to design a working rhythm that creates more space for my best thinking. I want to reduce time responding to inputs and increase time generating original thinking. Based on what you know about cognitive performance and focus, help me design a three-phase daily structure that works with my actual life. Here is what my current day looks like: [describe it honestly]. I am not looking for a perfect system — I want a starting point I can test this week."*

Let it ask you questions. Answer honestly. What emerges will not be my three-phase rhythm — it will be a version calibrated to your life, your constraints, your work.

One specific thing to try, regardless of what the conversation produces: identify one recurring time in your week when you could be genuinely offline for ninety minutes. Not "do less on your phone" — genuinely offline. No screen, no inputs. What you do with that time is less important than starting the practice of protecting it.

→ *If you are within a job:* Translation inside an organisation looks different. The two-hour offline morning is probably not an option on most workdays. But the principle applies: find the time in your week that is genuinely yours, protect it from inputs, and use it to think about what you are going to brief the agent to do next. Even thirty minutes of deliberate thinking, before the day starts, changes the quality of what you hand to the agent — and the quality of what comes back.
