# Closing: What I Found

I want to start with a story that is not about work.

June 24, 2026. An experiment was running in the background. The agent was handling what it handled. I was thinking about something else entirely.

I was thinking about two people — my housemates in Buenos Aires — who were about to host me for the last time before I left for Europe. I wanted to do something for them. Not a gift exactly. Something with a puzzle in it. Something that only made sense if you knew the word.

The word was "Infinito."

I built a private webpage, accessible only by entering that word. Wrong answers returned error messages in Argentine slang. The correct answer opened a Vienna itinerary — a trip back to the Austrian house in Zell am See where the Agentic Living story began.

The idea was mine. The execution was the agent's. In under an hour.

Agentic Living, it turns out, is not confined to professional life. Once you have crossed, it comes with you into everything. The gifts you give. The experiences you make possible for the people you love. The things you create not for any strategic reason but because the system is now good enough that you can.

That is the thing I found on the other side of the walk. Not just more capacity. More of myself, applied to more of my life.

## The Honest Numbers

140 days. Here is what actually happened.

March: 853 messages sent. 63 sessions — 46 interactive, 17 autonomous. Median gap between messages: 3.3 minutes. Someone sitting at a keyboard, watching, correcting, redirecting in near-real-time. This was not autonomy. This was scaffolding.

By July: 1,171 autonomous sessions in a single month. The first four months combined had produced 33 autonomous sessions total.

The number surprises people. They expect the autonomy curve to mean the human does less. The opposite happened. As autonomous activity grew, my message count grew too. More autonomy did not mean less involvement. It meant higher total throughput. More ambitious work required more active direction. More background systems meant more things occasionally needing attention.

This is the honest version of Level 1. Not a transfer of work from human to machine. An expansion of total capacity — the human does more, the machine does more, and the output is larger than either could produce alone.

The tiredness at the end of a good agentic day is different from the tiredness at the end of a conventional one.

Conventional tiredness: a diffuse, heavy feeling of having been present for a long time. The sense of time spent rather than things made.

Agentic tiredness: a specific, earned exhaustion. The feeling of having done the hardest thinking available, and then having actually rested instead of grinding through low-value tasks for the remaining hours.

The second kind of tiredness is better. It is the tiredness that comes with the sense of having been the right person, doing the right thing, at the right level of engagement.

## What the Attention Actually Went Toward

The end state of Agentic Living is not productivity. Not efficiency.

The end state is a life where the things that deserve your attention actually get it.

For me, that meant mountains. Long runs at altitude. Hours in coffee shops in places that take effort to reach and reward the effort with beauty. Meetings with people worth thinking alongside. Creative work done slowly and well. The ability to stay two weeks instead of two days, to wait for the weather window, to be present when the conditions are right.

In El Chaltén — a small town at the base of Cerro Fitz Roy in Argentine Patagonia, glaciers on three sides — I stayed for two weeks. Not because the work had slowed down. Because the architecture of the work no longer required me to be anywhere specific. And I chose to be there.

Most visitors stay two or three days. The logistics of getting there determine how long you can afford to be present. I stayed until I had seen everything I came to see — the Laguna Torre, the Fitz Roy viewpoint, one afternoon watching a glacier calve into a lake from thirty metres away.

Those are not experiences you can compress into a two-day visit. They require patience, flexibility, and the willingness to wait for the conditions to be right.

Both — the working mornings and the mountain afternoons — were possible because of the same decision made before I arrived. Build the system. Let it travel with you.

The record of all of it — the trails, the routes, the places this attention made possible — lives at [runs.trillion-initiative.com](https://runs.trillion-initiative.com) and [rides.trillion-initiative.com](https://rides.trillion-initiative.com). Trek and Sea: mountain trails and coastal rides through some of the most extraordinary terrain on Earth.

For every trail on runs.trillion-initiative.com, there is a short documentary. Built by my agent from the raw footage of my Meta AI glasses and my drone — no editor, no production team, no script. Just the footage, the data, and an agent that knew what to make of it. Each one is a record of a place I got to be in fully, because the work was handled and I was not managing a screen.

This is what the attention goes toward. Not productivity metrics. Life.

## The Question Underneath the Question

The task list is infinite. Attention is finite.

That sentence is the most important thing in this book. Not a productivity insight. A philosophical position about how to live.

The conventional approach to this problem is prioritisation — deciding which items on the infinite list to address first. But prioritisation still treats the infinite list as the primary reality. You are always managing the backlog. The backlog is always winning.

Agentic Living changes the frame. The system handles the infinite list. The question becomes: what do I do with the protected hours?

That question has no universal answer. It has your answer. And finding your answer — what it is, what it actually looks like, what you would do if the execution overhead were gone — is, in some sense, the whole point of this book.

The three questions I want you to sit with before you close this:

**One.** What would tell you that your life has actually expanded — not that you're busier or more productive, but genuinely different? What would be specifically, visibly true?

**Two.** If you recovered five hours a week, what would you actually do with them — and what does your honest answer tell you about your priorities?

**Three.** Which tasks do you consciously not want to delegate — and is that a values decision or a control habit?

The third question is the hardest. It is the one people want to skip. It is also the one that, when answered honestly, tells you the most about what Level 1 actually means for you.

One thing to do this week, regardless of where you are in the process: write down three things you would do if you had no execution overhead this month. If you cannot name them in three minutes, the system question is not the most important one yet. The more important one is: what do I actually want?

The system is only as good as the life it serves.

## What Comes After This Book

The individual story ends here. The larger one does not.

The people reading this book are almost certainly working in elastic sectors — software, research, creative work, education, consulting. When the cost of production drops in these fields, total demand expands. The opportunity grows faster than any individual workforce is displaced.

That creates an obligation. Not just a personal one.

If elastic sector workers use AI to produce the same output with less effort — if they treat agents as a productivity convenience — the economic transition gap widens. Displaced workers from inelastic sectors have nowhere to go. The social contract frays.

If elastic sector workers multiply their output genuinely and structurally — not as an initiative but as a fundamental change in how work is done — something different becomes possible. Hermann Arnold, a Swiss entrepreneur who engaged with an early version of this work, framed it precisely: the bar is not one hundred times. A two times increase in the elastic half mathematically compensates for the full displacement of the inelastic half. Most people who complete the workshop are already running at five to ten times their previous output within months. That is enough — if the distribution is right.

Going agentic is not just about your own attention. It is the appropriate response to the moment we are in.

The full argument is in a companion essay: *The Transition Paradox*. The short version: go agentic. And demand the institutions that distribute what that produces.

## The Walk

I want to end where I began.

Zell am See. January 2025. Minus fifteen degrees. The research paper. The shaking.

I did not know, that night, what I had just understood. I did not have a framework for it yet, or a name for the crossing, or a hundred and forty days of data. I had a feeling I could not sit still with, and a walk in the cold.

Less than a year later, in Tulum City, I had a different version of the same sensation. Not the shock of understanding what was now possible — I already knew that. Something else: the recognition that it was spreading. People I knew were crossing. People I met were crossing. The acceleration that had felt theoretical in January 2025 was visible and measurable by late 2025.

By February 2026, many more had already made it across. The question had shifted from whether this was possible to how many more people could be brought there.

That is why I wrote this book.

What I found, on the other side of that walk, is what this book describes.

The experiments. The failures. The sessions that ran while I slept. The morning in El Chaltén when I realised I could stay another week. The afternoon watching a glacier calve. The surprise built for two people with a single word and an hour of co-creation. The proposals that came back better than anything the full version of me with the full version of my time could have produced alone.

The walk is yours now.

You have the framework. You have the exercises. You have the proof that the crossing is learnable — 100% of the people who have walked through this in three days have made it across.

The only thing left is to take the first step.

And the only limit, on the other side, is your imagination.
