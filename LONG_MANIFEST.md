## Part I — The Experiment

### 1. Ushuaia: The Race That Never Finished

It was kilometre 90 when the first snow of the season arrived.

Not a warning. Not a dusting. Real snow, horizontal, driven by the kind of wind that Patagonia reserves for moments when it wants to remind you that you are a guest here. The race director made the call at 3am. Ushuaia by UTMB, 2026. Cancelled.

I had flown to the southernmost city on earth to run 110km through terrain that most humans never see. I had trained for months. I had agents managing my inbox, my client communications, my content calendar — all so that I could be *here*, present, running.

And then the race stopped at 90km.

I stood at the checkpoint in borrowed dry clothes, eating soup that tasted like salt and relief, and made a decision that changed everything: I was not going home.

Not because I had nowhere to go. But because I realised, standing in that tent at the end of the world, that I had accidentally built something that didn't require me to go anywhere.

My three companies were running. My agents had handled two client requests overnight. One had drafted a proposal that would later get accepted — without me reviewing a single line before it went out.

I ordered another soup. I stayed in South America for the next four months.

---

### 2. The Decision to Stay

The conversation I had with myself in that tent went something like this:

*Do I need to go back?*

Not "should I" — I know the obligations, the meetings, the expectation that a founder is physically somewhere. But do I *need* to?

I ran the list. There was a client call I could take from a café in Buenos Aires. There were proposals to develop — my agent could draft those. There was a board update — my agent had been tracking the relevant metrics for weeks. There was a product decision — that one required a real conversation, but it could happen from anywhere with a decent internet connection.

The list kept coming back the same way: *No. You don't need to go back.*

This is not the story of a man who abandoned his responsibilities. It is the story of a man who had, over the previous eight months of deliberate experimentation, built a system that made physical location irrelevant to professional output.

It is a story about what becomes possible when you stop treating your attention as something to be allocated and start treating it as something to be *protected*.

---

### 3. 49 Days: What Actually Happened

The numbers, because numbers are proof:

**49 days** in South America without returning to Vienna.

**3 companies** operating across those 49 days: Trillion Initiative, Fly Raising, Agent School.

**1 device**: my phone. I opened a laptop twice — once for a video call where I thought a larger screen would be more professional (it wasn't necessary), and once to review a document that my agent had already summarised for me (also not necessary in hindsight).

**0 manual files created**: every document, every proposal, every presentation was generated by an agent working from my voice notes.

**27 documented experiments**: cases where I deliberately tested what could be done without my direct involvement, measured the outcome, and wrote up what I learned.

**Top 15 finish** at the end of the experiment: despite operating three companies from Patagonia during the peak training period, I finished in the top 15 of my age group at a major ultramarathon. The agents handled the business. I handled the training.

These are not impressive numbers in isolation. What makes them significant is the context: this was not a sabbatical. This was not a "digital detox." This was full professional output — proposals accepted, clients acquired, products improved — while physically present in places most people only visit on holiday, training at a level most people only dream of.

The system held. That was the proof.

## Part II — The Problem

### 4. The Screen Is Eating Your Life

Before we talk about the solution, we need to be honest about the problem. Not the sanitised version — the real one.

You are a founder, an executive, a builder. You are probably intelligent, probably driven, probably reasonably good at your work. And you are probably spending somewhere between six and twelve hours a day in front of a screen.

Here is what those hours actually contain, if you are honest about them:

- Email triage. Reading, categorising, deciding what to respond to, composing responses that could be generated in seconds.
- Status updates. Reading reports that someone wrote about things you already broadly know.
- Meeting prep. Reviewing materials to be "ready" for conversations that could have been asynchronous.
- Formatting. Making things look right that the content makes irrelevant.
- Copy-pasting. Moving information from one system to another.
- Searching. Looking for things you already know exist somewhere.

None of these activities require a human. Not one.

They require literacy. They require the ability to recognise and categorise information. They require some judgment about priority. But genuine human judgment — the kind that requires experience, emotional intelligence, strategic thinking, ethical reasoning — appears in perhaps 10% of the average knowledge worker's day.

The other 90% is what I call monkey work. And for the first time in history, the monkeys have been replaced.

---

### 5. Why the 4-Hour Work Week Wasn't Enough

Tim Ferriss understood the problem before almost anyone else. In 2007, he articulated what millions of people were quietly feeling: that the implicit contract between effort and reward was broken, that presence had been mistaken for productivity, that the factory model of work had been erroneously applied to knowledge work.

His solution was elegant for its time: geographic arbitrage, delegation to virtual assistants, aggressive elimination of low-value tasks.

It worked — for some people, some of the time. And then the internet caught up, the world flattened, and the leverage of cheap VA labour diminished. More importantly, Ferriss's model had a structural limitation: it delegated execution to humans. And humans have cognitive limits, communication costs, misalignment risks, and growing salary expectations.

The era of human outsourcing as primary leverage is over.

What has replaced it is categorically different: not delegation to a human who then interacts with technology, but delegation to an intelligent system that reasons, adapts, and acts autonomously. The difference is not incremental. It is the difference between a pulley and a motor.

Agentic Living is what the 4-Hour Work Week would have been if it had been written in 2025. Same insight, different technology, completely different scale of possibility.

---

### 6. The Operator Trap: When AI Makes It Worse

Here is the uncomfortable truth that the AI industry does not want you to examine too closely: for most people who are "using AI," the technology has made their relationship with screens *worse*, not better.

The AI Operator — and this is most people who describe themselves as "AI-native" — spends ten hours a day at a desk. They are prompting, reviewing, correcting, re-prompting. They have added a new layer of cognitive labour to their existing cognitive labour. They are babysitting an intelligent system rather than delegating to one.

The distinction is subtle but critical.

A babysitter watches. A manager delegates. The difference is not in the technology — it is in the relationship with the technology.

Agentic Living is not about using AI. It is about *managing* AI in the same way you would manage a trusted, capable employee: with clear expectations, defined authority, measurable outcomes, and minimal micromanagement.

The moment you start treating your agent as a tool that needs constant supervision, you have failed. The moment you treat it as a colleague with a job — with consequences for failing to do it, and autonomy to do it well — you have begun.

## Part III — The Discovery

### 7. What Agentic Living Actually Is

A definition, because words matter:

**Agentic Living is the use of autonomous AI systems to eliminate execution load, thereby protecting human attention for thinking, deciding, creating, and living.**

Let me be precise about what this does and does not mean.

It does not mean: delegating everything to AI and sitting on a beach doing nothing. (Though sitting on a beach is a valid use of the recovered attention.)

It does not mean: having AI replace human relationships, human judgment, or human creativity. The agent writes the draft; you make the call on whether the direction is right.

It does not mean: being always-on, always available, always monitoring. The agent handles the monitoring. You get the summary.

It *does* mean: every task that does not require genuine human judgment is handled by a system that is running whether you are watching or not.

It *does* mean: your professional output is decoupled from your physical location, your device, your available hours.

It *does* mean: the time you spend "at work" is almost entirely composed of things that actually require you.

The simplest version: **you become the chairman of your own operations, not the operator.**

---

### 8. The Six Principles

These are not aspirations. They are design constraints — rules I apply to every decision about how I structure my work.

**Principle 1: No Monkey Work.**
If an action does not require human judgment, it should not be executed by a human. This is not laziness. It is respect for the scarcity of human attention.

*Measurement: Time spent on tasks with no thinking component. Target: near zero.*

**Principle 2: Delegation with Ownership.**
An agent with a task is a tool. An agent with a responsibility is a team member. The difference is who is accountable for the outcome. When I assign something to an agent, I assign the outcome — not the method.

*Measurement: Outcomes produced without direct human input as a percentage of total outcomes.*

**Principle 3: Screen Independence.**
Every process must function on a mobile phone, with bad internet, from any location on earth. If a process requires a laptop, it is not yet fully agentic. This constraint is not about hardware — it is about decoupling output from presence.

*Measurement: Times per week I am required to open a laptop. Target: fewer than two.*

**Principle 4: Meetings for Humans Only.**
A meeting is a synchronous, real-time exchange between two or more humans. It should be reserved for things that are meaningfully improved by real-time human interaction: emotional support, creative exploration, difficult negotiations, relationship building. Status updates, reporting, routine decision-making — these are not meetings. They are monkeys in suits.

*Measurement: Meetings that could have been asynchronous. Target: zero.*

**Principle 5: Technology for Health.**
The point of recovering attention is to use it for what makes life meaningful. For me, that includes training for ultramarathons, time in extraordinary places, and the kind of uninterrupted thinking that produces real insight. Technology that does not ultimately serve these ends is not earning its place.

*Measurement: Weekly self-assessment of physical and mental health trajectory.*

**Principle 6: Trust Through Competence.**
You cannot delegate what you cannot define. The limiting factor on how much you can give to an agent is your own clarity about what you want. Agentic Living matures as you mature — as your ability to define, instruct, and evaluate agent work becomes more sophisticated.

*Measurement: The Agent Quotient — the quality of outcomes produced without direct supervision.*

---

### 9. The FLOW Framework

If the six principles are the philosophy, FLOW is the operating system.

**F — Free yourself from monkey work.**
Start here. Make a list of every recurring task in your week. For each one, ask: does this require human judgment? If the answer is no, the task should be automated. If you are not sure, test it — give the task to an agent and see what quality of output you get. You will be surprised how often the answer is "good enough, immediately" rather than "needs significant human input."

**L — Load your agents with ownership.**
This is the hard one. Most people who use AI delegate tasks: "write this email," "summarise this document," "schedule this meeting." Agentic Living delegates responsibility: "manage my inbox," "maintain my project pipeline," "own the first draft of all client communications." The difference is that the second kind of delegation does not require your involvement until there is a genuine exception.

**O — Operate from anywhere.**
Design every process as if you will need to access it from a phone with bad internet, from a different country, with no warning. This is not a constraint — it is a forcing function. Processes that cannot survive this test are not actually processes; they are dependencies waiting to become crises.

**W — Work at human speed.**
Humans think well. Humans make good decisions with adequate information and adequate time. The problem is that most human work hours are not spent thinking — they are spent on logistics, communication overhead, and the maintenance of systems that should maintain themselves. When you recover those hours, use them for the things that actually require a human: deep thinking, genuine relationships, creative work, physical experience.

## Part IV — The Proof

### 10. 27 Experiments, 27 Lessons

The experiments were not planned as a book. They were planned as a discipline.

Every time I tried something new with an agent, I wrote it down: what I expected, what happened, what it meant. The format was consistent — hypothesis, measurement, outcome. The results were not always positive. Some experiments failed. Some required more human involvement than I expected. Some produced better results than I thought possible.

Over eight months, there were 27 of them. What follows is a selection.

### 11. The Proposal That Ran Itself

This is the experiment that changed how I think about knowledge work.

A client issued an RFP — a request for proposal for an AI implementation project. I had 10 days to respond.

In previous years, this would have meant: understanding the requirements, researching the client, developing a strategic approach, writing the proposal, formatting the PDF, reviewing it multiple times, polishing the language, sending it.

This time: I read the RFP. I told my agent what I had understood, what approach I thought was right, what the client seemed to care most about. The agent asked me five clarifying questions. I answered them in voice notes.

The agent wrote the proposal.

Not a draft that required significant editing. A proposal — structured, argued, formatted, with an executive summary and a technical appendix. I changed two paragraphs and approved the rest.

The proposal was accepted.

Then something unexpected happened. When the client sent the first message to kick off the project, I forwarded it to my agent along with the original proposal. The agent immediately began executing the first phase of the project — because the proposal had been written with enough precision and structure to serve as a direct instruction set.

The proposal was the program.

This is the insight that restructures knowledge work: **if you invest in quality thinking at the proposal stage, execution becomes almost free.** The bottleneck has always been upstream of where we thought it was.

### 12. Agent School: A Product That Builds Itself

Agent School is a learning platform. It teaches people how to work with AI agents more effectively.

I did not build it in the traditional sense. I described it to my agent. My agent built the interface. The content comes from my conversations with my agents — distilled, anonymised, structured into learning modules by an automated process that runs weekly.

The only thing I have to do to maintain Agent School is keep having conversations with my agent. The product observes those conversations, identifies patterns worth teaching, writes the content, formats it, and publishes it.

I have never written a single piece of Agent School content. The system writes it from watching me work.

This is not a feature. It is a proof of concept. A product can be a continuous output of a living, working system rather than a discrete thing that someone builds and ships. The boundary between working and building has dissolved.

### 13. The Ultra as Stress Test

I want to address something that might seem like self-indulgence: the running.

I run ultramarathons. 50km, 100km, occasionally more. They happen in mountains, in deserts, in conditions that would make most people reconsider their life choices. During the 49-day South American experiment, I was training for several of them simultaneously.

The running is not separate from the work. It is *the proof of the work.*

When I am in the mountains for six hours, my agents are answering client queries, updating project statuses, processing meeting transcripts, monitoring dashboards, drafting follow-up communications. The business continues. The output continues. And I come back with my thinking clearer than it was when I left.

This is the test that matters: not whether the system works when everything is normal, but whether it works when I am genuinely unavailable. The ultramarathon is the most honest possible version of unavailability. You cannot step out for an urgent call when you are at kilometre 70 in the Andes.

The system has passed this test, repeatedly. That is the benchmark I care about.

## Part V — The Architecture

### 14. How to Build Your Agent Stack

I am going to be specific here, because specificity is what makes this useful.

**Start with one agent, one responsibility.**
Do not try to automate everything at once. Choose the single task in your week that consumes the most time relative to the judgment it requires. Give it entirely to an agent. Spend two weeks teaching the agent how you want it done. Then move to the next task.

**The first agent should be your Chief of Staff.**
Not a task agent. Not a writing assistant. A Chief of Staff — someone (something) that knows what is happening across all your commitments and surfaces only what requires your attention. The morning briefing. The priority alert. The filter between the world and your decision-making capacity.

For me, this agent connects to my email, my calendar, my project management system, my client communication channels. Every morning, I receive a summary: what happened overnight, what decisions are needed today, what can be handled without me. The summary takes three minutes to read. Then I run.

**Build in writing, not in conversation.**
When you delegate responsibility to an agent, write down what that responsibility means. What does success look like? What are the exceptions — situations where you want to be involved rather than have the agent decide? What is the quality bar? This discipline of writing is what separates Agentic Living from simply chatting with an AI.

**The test: can you go dark for 24 hours?**
Once a month, I run this test. Can I disappear for 24 hours — no phone, no email, complete presence in whatever I am doing — and have nothing break? If the answer is no, I have not finished building.

---

### 15. The Chief of Staff You Can Actually Afford

The role of Chief of Staff in a mid-size company costs, at minimum, €80,000 per year. For a solo founder or small team, it is inaccessible.

The agent version costs approximately €200 per month in underlying API costs, plus whatever time you invest in setting it up correctly.

The differences from a human Chief of Staff: it never sleeps. It never gets sick. It does not have opinions about strategy that conflict with yours. It does not require management — it requires clear instructions and clear metrics.

The similarities: it surfaces what matters. It keeps things from falling through the cracks. It manages communication on your behalf to the level you authorise. It knows what is happening.

For the working founder, this is not a luxury. It is competitive infrastructure.

### 16. What You Still Have to Do Yourself

This would be an incomplete document if it did not address this directly.

What agents cannot do — yet, possibly ever:

**Genuine relationship building.** The emails your agent sends are not relationships. The conversations you have with clients, partners, mentors — those require your presence and your real self.

**Strategic judgment under genuine uncertainty.** When a situation is novel and the stakes are high, you need to think. The agent can give you data, context, options. The judgment is yours.

**Creative originality.** The agent can produce the draft. The insight that makes the draft worth sending — that usually comes from a human mind in a state of genuine attention. Protect that state.

**Ethical decisions.** When there is a conflict between what is profitable and what is right, the agent will give you analysis. The call is yours.

**Everything that is only valuable because a human did it.** Some things matter because of who made them. A handwritten note. A phone call when you didn't have to call. A decision to show up in person. These are not inefficiencies. They are the point.

## Part VI — The Movement

### 17. Who This Is For (And Who It Isn't)

**This is for:** Founders operating across multiple companies or projects who are drowning in execution load. Executives who produce their best thinking when they are not managing email. Builders who understand technology and want to be among the first to live at its frontier. Athletes who take their physical performance seriously and want the cognitive space to support it.

The common thread: people who have already succeeded at the conventional game and are ready to play a different one.

**This is not for:** People looking to work less. If your goal is to do less, this is not the path — building an agentic infrastructure requires real investment of thought and time upfront. People who are early in their careers — the discipline of doing things manually teaches you what to automate later. People who want someone else to build the system for them — at some level, this requires you to understand your own work well enough to describe it.

---

### 18. The Tribe

I have been careful not to position Agentic Living as a community in the conventional sense — not a Discord server, not a newsletter with a paywall, not a course.

What I am building is something closer to what Tim Ferriss built with the 4-Hour Work Week, but without the book as the primary medium. The medium is the life. The evidence is the experiment. The community is the people who recognise themselves in the story and want to live it too.

The Tribe, when it exists in its mature form, will be: people running their own versions of this experiment. Sharing what works. Holding each other to the standard that this is possible — because the temptation to slide back into conventional work patterns is real, and ambient proof that others are doing it differently is valuable.

The entry requirement is not money. It is seriousness. You have to be willing to actually do it.

### 19. What Comes Next

I am writing this in July 2026. The experiment is eight months old. There are things I do not know yet.

I do not know how this scales — whether the principles hold as the agent stack grows more complex, as the companies grow larger, as the stakes increase.

I do not know whether this approach is accessible to people without technical backgrounds, and I want to find out. The architecture I have built assumes a certain comfort with technology. I believe the principles are universally applicable; the implementation may need to become simpler.

I do not know what Agentic Living looks like for people in professions that require physical presence — doctors, teachers, craftspeople. I suspect the principles apply to the parts of those jobs that are not inherently physical, but I have not lived that test.

What I do know:

The experiment is real. The results are real. The life I am living — running ultras in Patagonia while three companies produce output, designing this document in Buenos Aires at 6am before a training run, watching the agent handle the inbox while I watch the sun come up over the Río de la Plata — this is not a fantasy. It is a system that was built, piece by piece, over eight months of deliberate work.

And if I built it, you can build it.

That is the point.
