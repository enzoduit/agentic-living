# Chapter 5: On Output — What You Do With What You've Reclaimed

---

Let me tell you what Level 1 is not.

It is not lying on a beach while your AI works and money falls from the sky. That version exists as a fantasy — on LinkedIn, in the promotional material of people selling courses, in the part of your own imagination that wants the crossing to mean you are done. It does not.

Level 1 is a different relationship with work. Not less work. Different work. The execution is handled. What remains is yours: the thinking, the judgment, the decisions that actually require a human. That is not a smaller amount of something. It is a different quality of something. And it takes time to learn what to do with it.

This chapter is about that.

---

## What the Experiment Actually Looked Like

March 2026. Patagonia. Ushuaia.

I was at the southern tip of Argentina to run 130 kilometres through terrain that most humans never see. I had trained for months. I had agents managing my inbox, my client communications, my content calendar — all so that I could be here, present, running.

The race stopped at kilometre 90. The first snow of the season arrived, horizontal, driven by wind that Patagonia reserves for moments when it wants to remind you that you are a guest here.

I stood at the checkpoint eating soup that tasted like salt and relief, and made the decision I will describe for the rest of my life: I was not going home.

I stayed in El Chaltén for two weeks instead of two days. I ran the mountains every morning. I fixed a server crash from my phone, standing in a hostel courtyard at altitude, in under an hour, while the agent continued everything else without being asked. I had coffee in Tilcara in the Argentine highlands while three companies kept running. The professional output did not shrink. The life expanded.

That is what Level 1 looked like for me. Not a demo. Not a controlled experiment. Real work, real mountains, real consequences — and a system that held the execution while I was elsewhere.

## What the Data Actually Shows

I want to give you numbers, because numbers are harder to dismiss than stories.

For the first four months of the experiment, the ratio of autonomous background tasks — cron jobs, scheduled checks, automated reports — to interactive sessions with me stayed roughly flat. Between five and thirty percent of the total. No clear direction.

Then July: fifty-seven percent autonomous.

In a single month, the agent ran 728 autonomous sessions alongside 542 interactive ones. For context: in the entire first four months combined, there had been 33 autonomous sessions total. The crossing did not happen gradually. It happened, and then it exploded.

What changed was not the technology. The technology had been available from the beginning. What changed was the stack — the accumulation of experiments, each one adding a layer of autonomous operation, each one extending the scope of what the system could hold without me. The first experiment was one task for one week. Four months later, the system had a daily work schedule and ran whether I showed up or not.

This is the trajectory of Level 1. Not a switch, but a gradient. You build the first thing. It works. You build the next thing. That works. The stack grows. The ratio shifts. One day you look at the numbers and the autonomous sessions outnumber the interactive ones, and you realise that something structural has changed.

## The Reinvestment Problem

Here is the thing nobody prepared me for: reclaiming attention is the easy part. Deciding what to do with it is harder.

When the execution is handled — when the reports write themselves, the emails draft themselves, the monitoring happens without you — what arrives in its place is not automatically used well. Most people, in my experience, fill it immediately with more execution. More meetings. More checking. More of exactly what they were trying to free themselves from. Not because they are lazy or unambitious. Because the alternative — the genuine use of reclaimed attention for the things that actually require you — is less comfortable than it sounds.

Genuine thinking is uncomfortable. Real judgment takes energy. The work that only you can do is often the work that requires you to sit with difficulty and not move until you have found something true.

Level 1 does not automatically produce a better life. It produces more capacity. What you build with that capacity is still entirely up to you.

## The Three Practices of Level 1

In my experience, people operating well at Level 1 do three things consistently. I call them Nomination, Transformation, and Translation.

**Nomination** is the ongoing practice of identifying what to hand off next. Not a one-time audit of your working week, but a continuous habit of looking at your own work and asking: does this require me? If the answer is no, or not always, or not in this form — it is a candidate for nomination. The first experiment was about the one obvious thing. Nomination is about everything after that. Your stack grows through nomination.

**Transformation** is the practice of redesigning existing processes to be agent-ready. Most of what we do was designed for humans — with all the inefficiency, ambiguity, and informal judgment that entails. An agent can execute a process, but not an informal one. Transformation means making the implicit explicit: documenting the steps, defining the criteria, specifying what success looks like. This is the same articulation work described in Chapter 4. The difference is that here, you are doing it systematically, across your whole working life, not just for one experiment.

**Translation** is the practice of encoding your own expertise into instructions that an agent can follow. This is the deepest and most valuable of the three. Your years of experience, your professional judgment, your knowledge of your field — none of that is available to an agent unless you translate it into explicit form. The agent can execute. You have to provide the intelligence to execute toward. Translation is how you put your expertise into the system.

These three practices are what makes Level 1 a practice rather than an event. The crossing is not the end. It is the beginning.

## The Honest Version

I want to be clear about one more thing, because I have watched people arrive at Level 1 expecting something different.

It is not passive. The beach moment in Altafulla, Catalonia — the one I mentioned in the introduction — was not me relaxing while the system worked. A server crashed. I spent an hour debugging it from my phone, in the sun, barefoot, with sand between my toes. I was not watching. I was working. Intensely.

What was different was not the absence of work. What was different was that I could work from there — that the work was mobile, that the system had enough resilience to survive a crash long enough for me to fix it, that the hour I spent debugging was followed by another hour of genuine creative work in the same spot. That was worth the walk in the Austrian mountains. Not the fantasy. The reality.

The reality is: Level 1 is a better arrangement. It is not an absence of effort. It is the right effort, applied to the right things, from the right places.

## Your Fifth Step

Look at your working week — the version you ran last week, not the ideal version.

Write down every task you did. Not the categories. The actual tasks.

Now mark each one:
- **Mine** — this required my judgment and only I could have done it
- **Not mine** — this required time and execution but not necessarily me
- **Partly mine** — this required me for one piece of it

Count the "not mine" and "partly mine" tasks. The total is your nomination list.

Pick the top three from that list — the ones that take the most time and require the least of you specifically. Write them down.

Then open an AI conversation and type this:

> *"I have three tasks I want to explore delegating to an AI agent. I'll describe each one. For each, I want you to help me understand: what would the agent need to know to do this, what access would it need, and what would a first test look like? I am not a developer and I don't want to write code. I want to understand what's possible in plain language. Here are the three tasks: [your three tasks]."*

The conversation that follows is the beginning of your stack.

→ *If you are within a job:* your nomination list comes from your professional role. Pick the three tasks in your working week that a capable colleague could handle with the right briefing — the tasks that require competence but not your specific judgment. Those are your first nominations. The same conversation works exactly the same way.
