# The Transition Paradox

*by Enzo Duit*

---

## On the Gap Nobody Is Talking About

There is a version of the AI story that goes like this: the robots arrive, everything becomes free, and governments hand out checks to make sure nobody starves. Elon Musk has described this future in reasonable detail. And at the theoretical endpoint, it is actually coherent.

Here is the underlying logic. Economics has a fundamental identity: Money Supply × Velocity = Price Level × Output. In plain terms: the total amount of money moving through an economy equals the total value of goods and services produced. If output — the goods and services side — explodes due to AI and automation, while the money supply stays fixed, prices have to fall. Dramatically. That is deflation. And deflation is dangerous — it causes people to defer spending, companies to cut investment, and economies to contract into themselves.

So if AI genuinely drives the cost of most goods and services toward zero, the only way to prevent catastrophic deflation is to expand the money supply to match. In Musk's model, this is not reckless money printing. It is a mathematical necessity. You are not inflating the currency — you are keeping the ratio stable as the denominator (goods and services) grows without limit. In the post-scarcity endgame, governments issue checks not to stimulate demand but to prevent the economy from imploding under the weight of its own abundance.

On paper, this is elegant. In practice, it runs directly into a wall.

The problem is not the destination. The problem is the gap between here and there.

Software displaces labour in months. A team of lawyers can be replaced by an AI system in a single deployment cycle. A customer support department of four hundred becomes a chatbot and two engineers in under a year. The digital economy moves at the speed of a software update.

Physical infrastructure does not.

Building the energy grids, housing stock, and raw material supply chains that a post-scarcity world requires takes decades. Nuclear plants require ten to fifteen years from planning to power. Mining operations for the critical minerals AI infrastructure depends on — lithium, copper, cobalt, rare earths — take similar timelines. Continental-scale grid upgrades are generational projects. You cannot deploy those with a GitHub push.

So here is the scenario nobody wants to say out loud: AI displaces millions of workers in Year 3. The physical abundance those workers were promised does not arrive until Year 15. In that twelve-year window, the Musk model says: print money and distribute checks. But the checks get absorbed by rising rents, energy bills, and the cost of things that cannot be duplicated by an algorithm. The goods and services that AI has not yet made free — housing, land, physical infrastructure — are still scarce. And they are owned by a shrinking number of private actors who are buying more of them with the proceeds of the AI transition.

The people who own the physical assets get richer faster than anyone in history. The people receiving the checks find that those checks buy less every quarter.

This is not a dystopia. It is just what happens when you apply 18th-century monetary mechanics to a structural problem that money cannot solve.

The economist Richard Cantillon described the underlying mechanism in 1730. Freshly created money does not arrive uniformly across an economy. It enters at specific nodes — central banks, financial institutions, sovereign treasuries, tech monopolies — and travels outward from there. Those closest to the source spend it before market prices adjust, acquiring real assets at yesterday's prices. By the time it reaches ordinary people via a state-issued check, asset prices have already expanded to absorb it. The check arrives. The landlord raises the rent.

This is the Transition Paradox: the tool designed to bridge the gap — cash — actively widens it. Not because the theory is wrong. Because the timing is.

## On Two Kinds of Work, and Why They Are Not Equal

Not all jobs face the same risk. This matters enormously, and the distinction is almost never made clearly.

Think about what happens when the cost of something drops dramatically. Two very different things can occur.

If the cost of bread drops, people do not eat ten times more bread. Demand is fixed by biology. The market shrinks. Workers who made expensive bread lose their jobs. The efficiency gain does not create new demand — it just eliminates labour.

If the cost of software development drops, something completely different happens. Suddenly projects that were previously impossible — too expensive, too slow, too complex — become viable. A team that used to build one product now builds ten. The demand for software was never limited by appetite. It was limited by cost. Remove the cost barrier and the appetite expands without a ceiling.

This is the difference between inelastic and elastic demand. And it is the single most important frame for understanding who is safe in this transition and who is not.

Inelastic sectors — basic housing, food, energy, primary transport — absorb roughly forty to fifty percent of developed economies. When AI automates these sectors, demand does not grow to compensate. Jobs are simply eliminated. Companies cut headcount, capture the efficiency gain as margin, and move on.

Elastic sectors — software, science, creative work, research, education, media, space, longevity — absorb the other fifty to sixty percent. Here the paradox runs in reverse. When the cost of producing something drops, total consumption of that thing expands dramatically. This is Jevons Paradox: efficiency increases demand instead of killing it. Make software development ten times cheaper and the world does not need ten times fewer developers. It needs ten times more software.

This is where the obligation enters the picture.

## On the Obligation of the Elastic Worker

If you work in an elastic sector — and if you are reading this, you almost certainly do — you are sitting on a different kind of responsibility than you probably realise.

Here is the logic:

The transition gap is dangerous precisely because displaced workers outnumber expanding markets. Inelastic sector workers lose jobs faster than new elastic sector work can absorb them. The gap yawns. Governments print money. The Cantillon Effect kicks in. Wealth concentrates. The social contract frays.

There is exactly one force that can counteract this: elastic sector workers multiplying their output.

Not working harder. Not working more hours. Multiplying. Taking the tools now available — AI agents, autonomous systems, agentic infrastructure — and doing ten times, fifty times, a hundred times more valuable work with the same human input. Anthropic's Dario Amodei calls this effectiveness rather than efficiency. Not doing the same output with fewer people. Doing massively more with the same people.

A team of ten software engineers equipped with agents does not become one engineer. It becomes a team that builds what previously required a thousand engineers. The ten stay employed. The output expands. The market grows to meet demand that previously could not be served because the cost was too high. New jobs emerge in the expanded market.

This is not idealism. It is arithmetic — and the arithmetic is more forgiving than it first appears.

A developed economy is split roughly in half. Approximately half is inelastic: housing, food, energy, primary transport. When AI automates these sectors, the jobs disappear but demand does not expand to replace them. The efficiency gain goes to the owners as margin; it does not create new work for the displaced.

The other half is elastic. And here is the crucial question: how much does the elastic half need to multiply its output to compensate for what happens to the inelastic half?

The answer is: two times.

Think it through. If fifty percent of the economy multiplies its output by two — total economic output stays flat, even if the other fifty percent is fully displaced by automation. The elastic half grows to fill the gap left by the inelastic half.

Hermann Arnold, a Swiss entrepreneur who reviewed an early draft of this essay, made this point directly: *"Wenn 50% der Wirtschaft Servicewirtschaft ist und die auch nur einen x2 Increase hat, dann haben wir die Wertschöpfung der gesamten Wirtschaft kompensiert, auch wenn die zweite Hälfte komplett wegfällt."*

He is right. The bar is not 100 times. It is two times. That is achievable today, with current tools, by anyone willing to actually change how they work rather than add AI as a supplementary layer on top of existing processes.

Hermann also said something honest about where he personally stands: approximately five to ten times his previous output. His teams, he estimates, are at two to five times. He described this as sufficient for commercial success — but not for societal obligation.

He is right about both. But here is the harder truth: even five times output from every elastic sector worker would not by itself solve the problem. Because the problem is not only the size of the economic pie — it is the shape of the distribution.

Productivity gains in elastic sectors flow primarily to the companies and their owners, not automatically to the workers displaced from inelastic sectors. The United States ran a decade of strong productivity growth from 2010 to 2020. Total output was up. Median real wages stagnated. The aggregate improved. The distribution did not follow.

This is the second layer of the obligation. The first layer is multiplication — produce more, use the tools, stop treating AI as a convenience and start treating it as the infrastructure of your work. The second layer is ensuring that what is produced by that multiplication reaches the people who need it — which requires not individual good intentions but institutional design.

The individual obligation and the structural obligation are not substitutes. They are complements.

You cannot build public capacity — sovereign energy, public compute, social housing — on a tax base that has collapsed because elastic sector workers did not multiply their output. The revenue has to come from somewhere.

And you cannot rely on elastic sector workers to solve the distribution problem on their own. No individual's decision to go agentic will redirect the gains toward displaced workers by itself. That requires policy: sovereign equity stakes in AI companies, public compute infrastructure, housing capacity that removes the cost of living from private speculation.

The conclusion is the same — go agentic — but the framing is more honest. The multiplication is necessary. It is not sufficient. The structural work has to happen alongside it, not instead of it.

*Go agentic. And demand the institutions that distribute what that produces.*

## On What Cash Cannot Buy

Assume the transition gap argument is wrong. Assume governments handle the displacement well, print the right amount of money, and distribute it sensibly. The checks arrive. People have purchasing power.

What do they spend it on?

Housing. Energy. Land. The physical goods that are finite, cannot be duplicated by an algorithm, and are owned by a shrinking number of private actors. The checks get absorbed. The owners of physical assets capture the value. The Cantillon Effect runs its course.

So the first instinct — print and distribute — does not work. What are the alternatives?

**Option one: tax the winners.** If AI concentrates wealth in the hands of those who own the models, the infrastructure, and the data, tax them heavily and redistribute. The political economy of this is obvious — the people being taxed are also the people with the most influence over the rules of taxation. More importantly, taxing corporate profits after the fact does not solve the speed problem. Workers are displaced now. Tax revenues from AI companies arrive on a different timeline, in a different form, and under constant pressure to be reduced. It buys time. It does not bridge the gap.

**Option two: regulate the speed of displacement.** Slow the adoption down. Give workers time to retrain. This is the instinct of every trade union and most European governments. The problem is that slowing adoption in one country does not slow it globally. Capital flows to wherever the transition is moving fastest. The country that delays falls behind in the elastic sector expansion, loses tax base, and ends up with the same displaced workers and less revenue to help them.

**Option three: build sovereign capacity before the gap opens.** This is what Norway did — not with AI, but with oil. In 1990, Norway began systematically converting finite natural resource revenues into permanent financial capital. The Government Pension Fund Global now holds $1.8 trillion in assets, equivalent to roughly $330,000 per Norwegian citizen. The rules are precise: only three percent can be withdrawn per year, preserving the principal permanently. And critically — the fund invests exclusively abroad. Not in Norwegian assets. The reason is elegant: injecting oil revenues directly into the Norwegian economy would inflate Norwegian asset prices, distorting the market for everyone. By investing abroad, Norway participates in global growth without cannibalising its own domestic economy.

The AI equivalent of this logic is striking. The companies capturing AI-driven productivity gains — the elastic sector winners, the model providers, the infrastructure owners — are also inflating the assets ordinary people depend on. A state that takes a sovereign equity stake in those companies, invests the returns externally, and then uses the accumulated capital to build domestic physical capacity is doing something fundamentally different from a state that taxes and redistributes. It is not extracting value. It is owning a share of creation.

Singapore did something adjacent with Temasek and its Housing Development Board — not by owning external equities but by building public physical assets so thoroughly that eighty percent of Singaporeans live in state-built housing, removing the floor of the cost of living from the private market entirely. Vienna did the same with social housing at a different scale, for a different history. The result in both cases: citizens whose basic survival costs are decoupled from market speculation.

The contrast is Great Britain after the industrial revolution. Productive assets privatised during the transition. Displaced workers distributed into poverty rather than capacity. A century of structural catch-up, still incomplete. The lesson was available. It was not applied. It is available again now.

This is why the policy answer cannot be Universal Basic Income alone. Cash distributed into a market where core physical assets are scarce and privately owned simply accelerates concentration. It is not a bridge. It is a transfer mechanism from the state to whoever owns the things people need to survive.

The alternative is Universal Basic Services. Not cash, but capacity. Abundant clean energy built and owned publicly, so the cost of power trends toward zero. Municipal housing that removes land speculation from the baseline cost of life. Public compute infrastructure — the AI equivalent of roads and schools — ensuring that the tools of the elastic economy are not gated behind private subscription fees for the people who need them most.

The state that navigates the transition well will not be the state that distributes the most cash. It will be the state that, in the window before the gap opens fully, builds enough sovereign capacity that its citizens' survival costs approach zero — and that participates in the global AI value creation through sovereign investment, the way Norway participated in global markets through its oil fund.

*An individual does not need a large check if their rent, energy, and compute access are essentially free. And a state does not need to tax its way to that outcome if it builds the capacity early enough.*

## On Whether AI Companies Will Have Enough to Share

Hermann Arnold, a Swiss entrepreneur who read an early draft of this essay, raised a challenge worth taking seriously: *"Du gehst davon aus, dass die grossen AI-Modelle viel Geld verdienen werden, soviel wie der norwegische Staatsfonds. Davon gehe ich heute nicht mehr aus."*

He is right to push on this. The sovereign participation argument — the idea that states should take equity stakes in AI companies and use the returns to build public capacity — depends on there being significant profits to participate in. What if the AI transition looks more like a race to the bottom?

The case for race to the bottom is not unreasonable. AI models are becoming more efficient, requiring less compute per inference. Edge AI is improving rapidly — models that run on mobile devices rather than data centres. As the major cloud providers have built out infrastructure aggressively, overbuilding creates the conditions for price wars. Technology has commoditised before.

But technology has also produced durable, extraordinarily profitable businesses that were theoretically supposed to commoditise — and did not. The mechanism is always the same: the underlying resource commoditises, but the product layer above it does not.

**AWS** is the clearest example. Compute was supposed to become a utility — undifferentiated, low-margin, priced like electricity. Instead, AWS generates over $100 billion in revenue with margins above thirty percent. The raw hardware commoditised. But the APIs, the managed services, the integrations, the ecosystem of tools, the institutional knowledge accumulated by millions of developers who built their infrastructure on AWS — none of that commoditised. The switching costs are not in the hardware. They are in everything built around the hardware.

**Uber** faced the same theoretical race to the bottom — a transport service where any driver with a car should be able to compete on price until margins disappear. Except the network effects — driver density, demand prediction, route optimisation, trust infrastructure — created a product layer that proved genuinely hard to replicate. The race stalled before the bottom.

Foundation AI models are playing out the same dynamic. The underlying weights of a model might eventually be replicated by open source equivalents. But a frontier AI product — with its hundreds of millions of users, the interaction data accumulated from those sessions, the workflows businesses have built around it, the memory and context layers on top — is not replicated by better weights. The product moat compounds over time precisely because it is built from use. More use creates better product creates more use.

This suggests three plausible scenarios for where AI profits end up:

**Scenario A — Race to the Bottom.** Edge AI plus efficient models plus overbuilt data centres → compute becomes utility-priced. Margins collapse across the industry. There is nothing left to tax, nothing to co-invest in. The sovereign participation strategy fails before it begins.

**Scenario B — Product Layer Moat (the AWS model).** Raw compute commoditises, but the handful of companies with dominant product positions — trained on proprietary interaction data, embedded in enterprise workflows, with compounding network effects — maintain durable high margins. The wealth concentrates in fewer hands than the optimists expect, but it is real wealth.

**Scenario C — Hybrid, with public infrastructure layer.** Both happen simultaneously in different layers. Commodity compute at the infrastructure level. Oligopolistic product positions at the application layer. Public compute infrastructure becomes viable and necessary — governments build the roads; private companies build the cars on top of them.

Scenario A is historically unusual. The technology transitions that looked most like commodity races — cloud computing, mobile, the internet itself — produced some of the most profitable companies in history, not because the underlying resource stayed expensive but because the product built on top of it created durable value. Scenarios B and C are more consistent with the pattern.

Crucially: both Scenarios B and C still produce the wealth concentration problem. The profits exist. They flow to a small number of private actors. Which is precisely why the sovereign participation argument matters more, not less, under those scenarios — not as a mechanism to tax AI companies after the fact, but as a strategy to take equity positions early, before the product moats are fully established and valuations price in expected dominance.

Norway did not wait until oil was expensive to start building the fund. It started when oil was cheap and the infrastructure was still being built. The window for sovereign AI participation is not unlimited. It is now — not because AI companies are guaranteed to remain hugely profitable, but because the option value of an early equity stake is highest before the outcome is certain.

## On What This Has to Do With You

I spent 140 days running an experiment in agentic living. The details are in the first essay. The short version: one person, phone only, multiple companies running, two ultramarathons, four countries, no limitations found.

I wrote that essay because the individual story matters. Because the person who sees what is possible is more likely to do something with it.

But the individual story is only half the picture.

The other half is this: the reason the individual story matters is not personal. It is structural. Every person working in an elastic sector who chooses to multiply their output instead of simply protecting their current position is making a decision that has consequences beyond their own career.

This is not a comfortable framing. It is easier to treat AI agents as a productivity tool and move on. It is easier to ignore the macro picture and focus on the next quarter. It is easier to wait and see.

But the gap does not wait. It grows in the meantime, every month that elastic sector capacity goes unmultiplied.

Seneca wrote: *"It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a great deal of it."* He was talking about individual attention. The same principle applies at scale. The transition window is not short. But we are wasting a great deal of it.

The experiment continues. The gap does not close itself.

*Which side of the gap are you building toward?*
