# Chapter 11: Reinvesting Attention

## Deep Living in El Chaltén & Tilcara

The most important question in this book is not how to build an agentic system.

It is what you do with the attention you get back.

Everything in Parts I through III is infrastructure. The philosophy, the architecture, the operating rules — all of it exists to create one thing: time and attention that belong to you instead of to your task list. The question of what to do with that is the harder one. And it is the one almost nobody asks before they start building.

I did not have a clear answer before the experiment. I do now.

---

## El Chaltén

El Chaltén is a small town in the Argentine Patagonia, at the base of Cerro Fitz Roy. It has a handful of hostels, a few restaurants, one main street, and some of the most dramatic trail running in the world. Most visitors stay two or three days. The logistics of getting there — the flights, the buses, the weather windows — usually determine how long you can afford to be there.

I stayed for two weeks.

The reason was not that I had nothing to do. The reason was that I had set up a system that did not require me to be anywhere specific, and I chose to be there.

In the mornings, when the weather was grey, I worked from coffee shops. Dictating briefings. Reviewing output. Directing the agent through voice notes. Productive hours, but light-touch — the kind of work that takes ninety minutes instead of four hours because the preparation has been handled by the system.

When the weather opened, I was out. Full days in the mountains. Runs to the Laguna Torre. Hikes to the Fitz Roy viewpoint. One afternoon watching a glacier calve into a lake. These are not things you can fit into a two-day visit. They require patience, flexibility, and the ability to wait for the right conditions.

Both of those weeks — the working mornings and the mountain afternoons — were possible because the same decision. The decision to build a system that travels.

## What Deep Living Actually Means

There is a version of "work from anywhere" that is not what this chapter is describing. The person who brings their laptop to a café in Bali and works twelve-hour days with a better view. The freelancer who checks email on the summit. The founder who is technically in Patagonia but is mentally in their inbox at all times.

That is location independence without presence. It is not the goal.

Deep living is different. It is the ability to be somewhere fully — to have the mountains take up the entire space of your attention when you are in them, because the work is not competing for that space. The agent is holding the threads. The decisions that require you are queued and waiting. The things that do not require you are handled.

This requires trust in the system. Not blind faith — trust earned through the failures and the refinements of the preceding months. Trust that when you come back from a six-hour hike, the right things will have happened and the right things will be waiting for your attention.

That trust was not there on day one. It accumulated. The fake NGO quote, the Netlify billing surprise, the Krisp chaos day, the Agent School loops that failed silently — each of those failures produced a guardrail. Each guardrail made the system more reliable. By El Chaltén, the reliability was high enough to walk away from the phone for an afternoon without anxiety.

## Tilcara

Tilcara is a small town in the Puna region of northwestern Argentina, at 2,500 metres above sea level. It is where I spent several weeks training for the Val d'Aran 110km ultramarathon in July.

The altitude training required four to five hours of running per day, mostly at low intensity — Zone 2, the aerobic base that endurance sports depend on. That is a lot of time with nothing to do but move and think.

In the past, that time would have felt like time away from work. A cost to be minimised, balanced against the training benefit.

During the experiment, it felt different. Four hours of running is four hours of cognitive availability. Questions arrive and settle. Problems untangle themselves. The decisions that had been accumulating in the queue got worked through during the run — not in front of a screen, but in motion, in fresh air, at altitude.

By the time I returned, I usually had a clearer sense of what mattered, what didn't, and what needed to happen next. The agent had been working during those hours. I had been thinking. The combination was more productive than either alone would have been.

## The Data

The experiment ran 140 days. Here is the honest summary of what the numbers show.

In March — the first month — I sent 31 messages total and the system ran 10 autonomous background tasks. Most of those tasks were tiny: SSL checks, early cron jobs. The work was overwhelmingly human-directed. I was the engine.

In July — the fifth month — I sent 568 messages and the system ran 728 autonomous background sessions in a single month. The agent had a daily schedule. It monitored, checked, summarised, and reported without being prompted. 57% of all activity that month required no human trigger.

The number that captures the shift most clearly: in July alone, the agent ran more autonomous tasks than it had in the entire first four months of the experiment combined.

This did not mean I was less involved. The message count went up, not down — because more ambitious builds require more active direction, and I was building more ambitious things. But the ratio of human effort to total output shifted dramatically. The same human input was producing more. Not because I was working harder. Because the system had matured.

That maturity is what made El Chaltén and Tilcara possible in their fullest sense. Not the early weeks — those required too much active supervision for real presence. But by the time I was in the Puna doing altitude work, the background was running. The threads were held. The attention was mine to reinvest.

## What the Attention Goes Toward

The end state of Agentic Living is not productivity. It is not efficiency. Those are intermediate conditions.

The end state is a life where the things that deserve your attention actually get it.

For me, in 2026, that meant mountains. Long runs at altitude. Hours in coffee shops in places that take effort to reach and reward the effort with beauty. Meetings that actually mattered — with clients, with team members, with people worth thinking alongside. Creative work done slowly and well instead of quickly and adequately.

None of that is possible when the task list is making all the decisions about where the attention goes. The task list is infinite. Attention is finite. The only way to live deliberately is to protect the finite thing from the infinite one.

Agentic infrastructure is the protection mechanism. The agent handles the infinite list. The human decides what to do with the protected hours.

That decision is yours. This book cannot make it for you. But it can make sure the infrastructure exists for you to make it.

## Where the Experiment Ends

The experiment has no endpoint.

July 21, 2026 — the day this chapter was first written — the Garmin numbers were pulled from the API as a way of marking the moment: 4,280 activities logged since I started wearing the watch. 51,468 kilometres moved — 1.28 times the circumference of the Earth. 606,218 metres of elevation gained — 68.5 times the height of Everest.

Those numbers were not assembled for this book. They were already there, logged automatically, available on request. This is the experiment summarised in one action: the record of a life, already kept, retrieved in seconds.

The experiment continues tomorrow, as it continued yesterday. The questions get harder. The builds get more ambitious. The failures produce better guardrails. The autonomy increases — not in a straight line, not without setbacks, but in the general direction of a system that holds more responsibility and requires less supervision each month.

Seneca wrote: *"Dum differtur vita transcurrit."* While we delay, life passes.

The experiment is the attempt to not delay. To be where the mountains are when the mountains are visible. To do the work that matters with the attention it deserves. To build a system that carries the rest.

That is what Agentic Living is for.
