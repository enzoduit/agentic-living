# Chapter 5: Stripping Zero-Judgment Tasks
## The End of Formatting & Menus

Before building anything, you have to clear the space.

This is the step most people skip. They discover AI agents, get excited about automation, and start building workflows — around a working week that still contains forty percent waste. The result is an agent that handles the waste faster. Slightly more efficient clutter.

Eliminate first. Automate second. Always.

---

## The Audit

For five consecutive working days, track everything you do in fifteen-minute intervals. At the end of each day, go through the list and ask one question about each item:

*Did this require genuine human judgment — reasoning under uncertainty, a call that only I could make, thinking the task actually deserved?*

If yes: this is where your attention belongs.

If no: it is a candidate for elimination, delegation, or automation.

| STEP | WHAT TO DO |
|---|---|
| **1 — Log** | Track every activity for 5 days in 15-minute intervals |
| **2 — Judgment Test** | For each item: did this require genuine human judgment? |
| **3 — Categorise** | Label every No: formatting, filing, scheduling, navigation, summaries |
| **4 — Prune** | Remove from your personal stack — assign to agent or eliminate entirely |

What you will find is roughly what I found: somewhere between forty and sixty percent of the week consists of tasks that require time but not genuine thought.

The categories that appear most consistently: formatting documents and slides, navigating software interfaces, writing status updates, scheduling and rescheduling, producing first drafts that will be revised anyway. None of these require you. All of them consume you.

If the five-day log feels too abstract to start, two alternative entry points:

**For anyone who works with KPIs:** Take the two or three metrics you are personally responsible for moving. Map every human action that currently goes into moving them. For each step, ask: does this require my judgment, or does it just require execution? The execution steps are candidates for the agent. Your judgment is the only thing that should remain.

**For executives:** Think about the last three decisions you took. What was required for you to take them confidently — what data, what preparation, what context? What would have made those decisions even better that was not done? The preparation gap is usually where the most delegation opportunity lives. An agent that shows up with everything already prepared — the relevant data pulled, the context assembled, the options framed — changes the quality of the decision, not just the speed of reaching it.

## Three Rules

After running the audit, three structural changes produce the most consistent results. These are not one-time fixes. They are permanent shifts.

**Rule 1 — No manual files.**

The moment you create a file yourself — a spreadsheet, a presentation, a document — you build a door back to the old pattern. Every file created by hand invites your hands back. You format it. You align it. You make decisions about presentation that do not matter. All of it consumes you.

At some point around month four of the experiment, I noticed I had barely opened Google Drive in weeks. Not because I had decided to stop using it. Because I had stopped creating the kind of files that needed to live there. The agent generates the outputs. They are stored where the agent can work with them. The Drive had been my filing system. The agent became the filing system. The Drive became irrelevant.

When agents create files, they create them in formats they work with well: Markdown, structured data, databases. You cannot easily open these and start editing them the way you would edit a Word document. That friction is intentional. It keeps you in the role of thinker. It keeps the agent in the executor role.

*Exception:* when a rough calculation in a spreadsheet makes your own thinking faster — use it. The line is whether you are enhancing your thought process or producing something for others. The first is yours. The second is the agent's job.

**Rule 2 — Outcomes, not steps.**

"Write a summary of this meeting" is a step. An outcome looks different: *You are responsible for making sure everything discussed in my meetings gets acted on. Do whatever you can do without me — draft the follow-up emails, create the tasks, prepare the materials. For anything that requires my approval before it goes out, surface it to me with a draft already done. I should never have to think about meeting follow-up. It should just happen.*

The difference is not just specificity. It is the shift from describing what the agent should do to describing what should exist when it is done — and making the agent responsible for the gap between the two. A step describes a process. An outcome describes a world in which the problem no longer exists.

The requirements engineering skill — knowing what success looks like precisely enough that a system can pursue it without hand-holding — is the most valuable capability in the agentic era. Not prompt writing. Not model selection. Knowing what you want before you build toward it.

**Rule 3 — Raw output first. Formatted output never.**

Stop formatting things yourself. The thinking is yours. The presentation layer is the agent's job.

My mornings are built around this. More than two hours, every day, completely offline. reMarkable tablet, handwriting, no screen. The deep thinking happens there — without the pull to polish, structure, or format as I go. Ideas arrive rough. That is the point.

When I am ready to act on something, I dictate. Thirty seconds of voice note describing what I worked through and what needs to happen next. The agent takes the raw thinking, converts it into whatever form the output requires, and handles the rest.

I never see the formatting step. I move from thinking to result.

The discipline is to resist tidying your own output before handing it over. The mess is information. The agent can work with mess. It cannot work with the version you have already pre-edited to the point of ambiguity.

## What Remains

After running the audit and applying the three rules, what is left on your list?

The work that is genuinely yours. Strategic decisions. Creative direction. Relationship-building. The judgment calls that require your specific experience, your values, your understanding of what matters in this particular situation.

This is not a small category. It is the most demanding one — the work that is hardest, most draining, and most rewarding. The work that makes the difference between an ordinary outcome and one that could not have happened without you.

The problem was never that this work was insufficient. The problem was that it was surrounded by everything else — the formatting, the filing, the status updates — and the everything else was consuming the attention the real work deserved.

Remove the everything else. The real work expands to fill the space.

*The audit tells you what you have been giving your attention to. The rules tell you how to stop. What comes next is what to build instead.*
