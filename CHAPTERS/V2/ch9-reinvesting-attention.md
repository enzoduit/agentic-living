# Chapter 9: Reinvesting Attention
## Deep Living in El Chaltén & Tilcara

The most important question in this book is not how to build an agentic system.

It is what you do with the attention you get back.

Everything in Parts I through III is infrastructure. The philosophy, the architecture, the operating rules — all of it exists to produce one thing: time and attention that belong to you instead of your task list. The question of what to do with that is harder. And it is the one almost nobody asks before they start building.

---

## El Chaltén

El Chaltén is a small town at the base of Cerro Fitz Roy in Argentine Patagonia. Glaciers on three sides. A handful of hostels, a few restaurants, one main street. Some of the most extraordinary trail running in the world.

Most visitors stay two or three days. The logistics of getting there usually determine how long you can afford to be present.

I stayed for two weeks.

Not because the work had slowed down. Because the architecture of the work no longer required me to be anywhere specific. And I chose to be there.

When the weather was grey — which in Patagonia is often — I worked from coffee shops. Three hours in the morning, focused, directing the agent through voice notes and short messages. When the weather cleared, I was out. Full days in the mountains. The Laguna Torre. The Fitz Roy viewpoint. One afternoon watching a glacier calve into a lake from thirty metres away. These are not experiences you can compress into a two-day visit. They require patience, flexibility, and the willingness to wait for the conditions to be right.

Both — the working mornings and the mountain afternoons — were possible because of the same decision made before I arrived. Build the system. Let it travel with you.

## What Deep Living Actually Means

There is a version of "work from anywhere" that is not what this chapter describes.

The person who brings a laptop to a café in Bali and works twelve-hour days with a better view. The founder who is technically in Patagonia but mentally in their inbox at all times. Location independence without presence.

That is not the goal.

Deep living is different. It is the ability to be somewhere fully — to have the mountains take up the entire space of your attention when you are in them, because the work is not competing for that space. The agent holds the threads. The decisions that require you are queued and waiting. The things that do not require you are handled.

This requires trust in the system. Trust that was not there on day one. It accumulated through the failures and the refinements of the preceding months. The fake NGO quote. The Netlify billing surprise. The Krisp chaos day. The Agent School loops that failed silently. Each failure produced a guardrail. Each guardrail made the next operating window more reliable.

By the time I was in El Chaltén, the reliability was high enough to walk away from the phone for an afternoon without anxiety.

## Tilcara

Tilcara is a small town in the Puna region of northwestern Argentina, at 2,500 metres above sea level.

I spent several weeks there training for the Val d'Aran 110km ultramarathon. Altitude training requires four to five hours of running per day, mostly at low intensity — Zone 2, the aerobic base that endurance sports depend on. That is a lot of time with nothing to do but move and think.

In the past, that time would have felt like time away from work. A cost to be minimised.

During the experiment, it felt different. Four hours of running is four hours of cognitive availability. Questions arrived and settled on the trails. Problems untangled themselves during climbs. The decisions that had been accumulating were worked through in motion — not in front of a screen, but at altitude, in fresh air, without distraction.

By the time I returned, I usually had a clearer sense of what mattered, what did not, and what needed to happen next. The agent had been working. I had been thinking. The combination produced more than either alone.

## The Data

The experiment ran 140 days. Here is the honest summary.

In March — the first month — I sent 31 messages and the system ran 10 autonomous background sessions. The work was overwhelmingly human-directed. I was the engine.

In July — the fifth month — I sent 568 messages and the system ran 728 autonomous background sessions. 57% of all activity that month required no human trigger. The agent had a daily schedule that ran whether or not I was present.

The number that captures the shift most clearly: in the entire first four months combined — March through June — the system ran 33 autonomous background sessions. In July alone: 728.

And here is the part that surprises people: as autonomous activity grew, my message count grew too. More autonomy did not mean less involvement. It meant higher total throughput. More ambitious builds required more active direction. More background systems meant more things occasionally needing attention.

This is the honest version of the autonomy curve. Not a transfer of work from human to machine. An expansion of total capacity — the human does more, the machine does more, and the output is larger than either could produce alone.

## What the Attention Goes Toward

The end state of Agentic Living is not productivity. Not efficiency.

The end state is a life where the things that deserve your attention actually get it.

For me, that meant mountains. Long runs at altitude. Hours in coffee shops in places that take effort to reach and reward the effort with beauty. Meetings with people worth thinking alongside. Creative work done slowly and well. The ability to stay two weeks instead of two days, to wait for the weather window, to be present when the conditions are right.

It also meant unexpected things. On June 24, I built a surprise gift for my two housemates who were about to host me for the last time before I left for Europe. A private webpage, unlocked only by the word "Infinito." Wrong answers returned error messages in Argentine slang. The correct answer revealed a Vienna itinerary — a trip to the Austrian house in Zell am See, the town where the Agentic Living experiment had begun. The idea was mine. The execution was the agent's. In under an hour. Agentic living, it turns out, is not confined to professional life.

None of that is possible when the task list is making all the decisions about where the attention goes. The task list is infinite. Attention is finite. The only way to live deliberately is to protect the finite thing from the infinite one.

The agent handles the infinite list. You decide what to do with the protected hours.

That decision is yours.

## What Comes After This Book

The individual story ends here. But it is only half of the picture.

The people reading this book are almost certainly working in elastic sectors — software, research, creative work, education, consulting. When the cost of production drops in these fields, total demand expands. The opportunity grows faster than the workforce is displaced.

That creates a specific obligation. Not just a personal one.

If elastic sector workers use AI to do the same output with less effort — if they treat agents as a productivity convenience — the economic transition gap widens. Displaced workers from inelastic sectors have nowhere to go. The social contract frays.

If elastic sector workers multiply their output — genuinely, structurally, not as a productivity initiative but as a fundamental change in how work is done — the gap narrows. Hermann Arnold, a Swiss entrepreneur who reviewed the companion essay, put it precisely: the bar is not 100 times. A two times increase in the elastic half mathematically compensates for the full displacement of the inelastic half. Most people who read this book are already at five to ten times their previous output. That is enough — if the distribution is right. Output grows faster than displacement. There is something to tax, something to redirect, something to use to build the physical infrastructure that the transition requires.

The full argument is in a companion essay: *The Transition Paradox*. The short version: going agentic is not just about your own attention. It is the appropriate response to the moment we are in.

*Go agentic. And demand the institutions that distribute what that produces.*
