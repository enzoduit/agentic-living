# Chapter 9: How It Changes You
## The Human Side of the Agentic Shift

Consider what makes a technology book valuable after the technology has moved on. The specific models will change. The specific tools will be deprecated, forked, or acquired before most readers have finished the first reading. Most books about a given technology peak in relevance at publication and decline sharply from there.

What does not change at that pace is the pattern beneath.

The previous chapter is the evidence layer — specific, dateable, verifiable. This happened, on this day, in this place, with this output. The work was real. The failures were real. The friction was real. You now have an impression of what is technically possible.

But that is not what I want to leave you with. Because a book that only proves agentic workflows can produce output at scale joins every other two-year technology book: credible in 2026, a historical document by 2028.

What I want to explore instead is the layer beneath the evidence. Not what the system can produce — but what it does to the person operating it. How the work and life change shape. How the rhythm of a day reorganises when execution is no longer the binding constraint. How thinking itself shifts when the machine holds most of the threads.

These are not technical observations. They are human ones. And human patterns — how attention works, how recovery happens, how presence and absence redistribute across a working life — do not deprecate on quarterly release cycles. The bestselling books on earlier technological transitions are still on shelves decades later, not because the technology they describe still runs, but because the human pattern they identified proved durable enough to outlast the tools that revealed it.

That is what this chapter is for.

The previous chapters describe what to build and how to run it. This one describes what happens to you — and to your life — when you do.

Not the productivity metrics. Not the autonomous session counts. What happens to the person operating the system — how the work feels different, how the days change shape, how thinking itself reorganises around a new set of constraints and freedoms.

## The Intensity Pattern

The most surprising change was not that I got more done. It was how differently the effort is distributed.

In a conventional working week, effort is roughly constant. Eight hours at a desk, spread across whatever arrives. Some of it is important. Most of it is not. The total drain is similar regardless.

Agentic work does not look like that.

The sessions that matter — building a new system, defining a new responsibility for an agent, designing the objective function for a new loop — are genuinely intense. Not "busy" intense. Cognitively exhausting intense. The kind of concentration that requires everything you have and leaves you depleted in a way that a normal working day does not.

The rhythm that emerged, after months of trial and error, has three distinct phases:

**Phase one: offline thinking.** Two hours, no screen, no inputs. reMarkable and a pen. The raw thinking happens here — problems worked through, directions decided, what needs to happen clarified. Nothing is produced for anyone. This is the substrate that everything else runs on.

**Phase two: deep co-creation.** Two hours with the agent, building or deciding something specific. Intense, absorbed, total focus. The offline thinking feeds directly into this — I arrive knowing what I want to do, which makes the co-creation faster and sharper. These sessions have a hard limit. After roughly two hours, the quality of thinking declines — the briefs get vaguer, the judgment calls get lazier. The discipline is to stop at that point, not push through.

**Phase three: away from the phone entirely.** Movement. Long runs. Zone 2 training. Time at altitude. No agenda, no check-ins. The agents run in the background. The outputs accumulate. I am elsewhere.

The sport is not separate from the work. It is the recovery mechanism that makes the next intense session possible — and, as the next section explores, the environment where the most useful thinking actually happens.

This creates a working rhythm that looks strange from the outside: four hours of high-intensity cognitive work in discrete phases, then genuinely off, then a brief review in the evening of what the agents produced and what needs direction. Not eight hours of continuous presence. Intense spikes, real recovery, outputs arriving in between.

## The Screen Question

Somewhere in the middle of the experiment, I stopped at a realisation that had been building for months.

I was doing my best thinking in the places furthest from a screen.

On trails. In coffee shops before I had opened anything. On long runs at altitude. In the mountains waiting for weather windows.

The screen is where I record the output. It is not where the output comes from.

This is not a mystical claim. It is a practical observation about cognitive performance. The kind of thinking that produces real insight — making connections, reframing problems, seeing what is missing — does not happen efficiently under distraction. And screens are distraction delivery systems. Not because of social media or notifications (though those are real) but because the interface is designed to keep you responding to inputs, not generating original thought.

The reMarkable changed this for me. Two hours every morning, offline, no screen, writing by hand. The thinking happens there — rough, unfiltered, not shaped for anyone else. Then the dictat: a voice note with what I worked out and what needs to happen. The agent takes it from there.

I did not plan this routine. It emerged from trying to figure out why my best ideas were arriving on runs and hikes rather than at a desk. The answer was obvious once I looked at it. The desk is where I execute. The movement is where I think. The reMarkable is the bridge between the two.

Now consider what this means at scale.

In all developed economies, more than fifty percent of work is knowledge work. The ReAct loop tells us exactly what kind of transformation that work is about to face. And knowledge work, when you strip it to its core, is thinking. The output of knowledge work is thought, applied to decisions and problems.

Here is the question that follows: where does the best thinking actually happen?

Not in front of a screen — at least not according to decades of research on cognitive performance, flow states, and creative problem-solving. The research points consistently toward movement, physical space, and the absence of competing inputs. And yet, for most knowledge workers in most organisations, the working day is structured around mandatory presence at a screen, in an office, for fixed hours.

This is, when you look at it clearly, genuinely strange. We have taken the one resource that we need most — high-quality human thinking — and built an entire set of institutional arrangements designed to prevent it from functioning at its best. Standing desks are offered as a remedy. They are not wrong, but they are answering the wrong question. The question is not whether to sit or stand at a screen. The question is whether to be at the screen at all.

I think this is one of the most significant workplace shifts that the agentic era will force. When agents handle the execution, what remains for humans is thinking and judgment. And thinking happens best when you are not managing a screen. The office as the mandatory location of knowledge work will look, in retrospect, like one of those arrangements that seemed normal until it suddenly seemed absurd.

## The Places

There is a specific list of places where I have consistently found flow — where the thinking that feeds the work actually happens. Not romantic notions of inspiration. Documented, repeated experience of conditions that produce my best cognitive output.

**Vicente López, Buenos Aires.** At the food trucks along the Río de la Plata, coffee in hand, a fresh wind off the water, everything green. The river is wide enough here that you can almost forget you are in a city.

**El Chaltén, Patagonia — sitting on the hillside.** October is autumn there, March and April by the Southern Hemisphere calendar. Everything turns red. The sun comes in warm, but the air is already properly cold. A view over the whole valley and the small town below — the trekking capital of South America — from a slope that asks nothing of you except to sit.

**El Chaltén — inside Lo de Tomi's small pink house.** When the wind comes through at over 100 km/h in gusts, it moves through the walls. The curtains shift in the room. Everything outside sounds like it is being rearranged. Inside: complete focus.

**Vicente López, 5th floor balcony.** A small table, a chair, a view over Buenos Aires to the Río de la Plata. Hours spent there. My friends Martin and Joaco could confirm. The city spread out below; the thinking spread out above it.

**Altafulla, Catalonia.** The beach promenade, fine sand giving way to natural dunes, restaurants along the water. Good for long walks while dictating. This is where the crashed-server afternoon happened. Also where some of the better ideas arrived.

**Ushuaia glacier, above the city.** Walking into the mountains looking down at the world's most southern city. The scale of it makes the problems smaller in a useful way.

**Tilcara, Cerro de la Cruz.** One hundred metres above the town in the Argentine Puna, 2,500 metres altitude, red rock formations in every direction, a view down onto an ancient pre-Columbian settlement that once commanded the whole valley from a small hill. The silence has texture.

What I remember most about running at altitude in the Puna is not the thinking I forced myself to do. It is the thinking that arrived when I stopped trying. There are phases of pure flow — the mind empties, the body moves, and eventually the background noise clears out completely. Ideas surface not because you pursued them but because you created the conditions in which they could arrive. Loose, automatic, genuinely creative — the kind of thinking that could not have happened at a desk, that would never have appeared if I had stayed in front of a screen. Running in places like this is not a discipline. It is an environment.

**Tupiza, Bolivia — Cerro de la Cruz.** Slightly elevated above the town. Epic feeling is the right word. The kind of place where the scale of the landscape puts the scale of your thinking in perspective.

**Tupiza — Hotel Púl.** Red sand on all sides, the hotel a small self-contained world inside it. An oasis quality that creates unusual focus.

**San Pedro de Atacama, Chile.** Hostel with a pool, the desert heat intense beyond it, trail runs through landscapes that do not look like anywhere else on Earth.

**Zell am See — Zellar Moos, south side of the lake.** A park bench. The Großglockner and Kitzsteinhorn with their glaciers visible across the water. Many hours spent there. This is, in fact, where the book started.

What these places share is not geography. It is the combination of low distraction, physical space, and an absence of competing inputs. The screen is elsewhere. The phone is in the pocket. The thinking has room to arrive.

The agent is waiting for what comes out.

## What the Morning Looks Like Now

Before the experiment: the day started with inputs. Email, messages, whatever was waiting. The first hour was reactive by default.

Now: the first two hours are offline. reMarkable. Handwriting. No inputs, no responses, no context-switching.

I work through whatever requires thinking — a strategic decision, the framing for a new initiative, a problem that has been sitting unresolved. I write badly, in fragments, following the thought wherever it goes. I am not producing anything for anyone. I am thinking.

When that is done, I dictate. Thirty seconds of voice note for each thing that needs to happen. The agent picks it up. By the time I open anything, the execution is already underway.

The day that follows is quieter than it used to be. Not because less is happening — substantially more is happening — but because what I am doing is different. I am reviewing, deciding, redirecting. The operational layer is running without me. My attention goes to what requires it.

## What the Tiredness Feels Like

The tiredness at the end of a good agentic day is different from the tiredness at the end of a conventional one.

Conventional tiredness: a diffuse, heavy feeling of having been present for a long time. The sense of time spent rather than things made.

Agentic tiredness: a specific, earned exhaustion. The feeling of having done the hardest thinking available, and then having actually rested instead of grinding through low-value tasks for the remaining hours.

The second kind of tiredness is better. It is the tiredness that comes with the sense of having been the right person, doing the right thing, at the right level of engagement.

It took months to trust that stepping away was not procrastination. That the run was not time off from work. That the two hours at the reMarkable were the most productive hours of the day even though they produced nothing visible.

The system handles the visible. You handle the thinking.

*What you choose to do with the time that thinking does not fill — that is the next question.*
