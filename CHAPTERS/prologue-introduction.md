## Reclaiming Attention, Multiplying Output, and Escaping the Transition Paradox

*by Enzo Duit*

## Prologue: The Sub-Zero Epiphany

On a freezing night in January 2025 in Zell am See, Austria, I sat reading a research paper on my reMarkable tablet. Outside, the mountain air was fifteen degrees below zero. Inside, I was reading about the ReAct framework — a simple architecture describing how an AI system could reason through a problem, take an action, observe the result, and loop autonomously.

Looking down at my Garmin, my heart rate was elevated as if I were standing on the starting line of a race. I stepped outside into the cold just to hear myself think out loud.

Knowledge work — the backbone of developed economies — is fundamentally an **Observe → Reason → Act** loop. If that loop could be automated on cloud infrastructure, what was left for human effort?

Within a year, that theoretical question became my daily reality. I wasn't coding foundation models; I was orchestrating them from my phone while traveling through Patagonia, running ultramarathons, and operating three companies without opening a laptop.

This book is the field manual for that shift.

## Introduction: The A.G.E.N.T. Framework

*"While we delay, life passes."*
— Seneca, Letters to Lucilius

Almost all non-physical human suffering stems from a single source: the misallocation of attention. We spend hours formatting slide decks, navigating menu interfaces, filing emails, and attending meetings that produce no decisions. We confuse the sensation of busyness with the substance of progress.

When Tim Ferriss wrote *The 4-Hour Workweek*, he leveraged the early web, offshore delegation, and automated arbitrage to decouple time from income. Today, autonomous AI agents represent the next evolution of that pattern — but the underlying truth is older than both of us.

Seneca wrote to his friend Lucilius: *"It matters not how many books you have, but how good they are."* The same principle applies here: it matters not how many AI tools you adopt, but how many of them give you back something that was always yours — your attention.

**Agentic Living** is the practice of using technology to improve human thinking and living by stripping away every task that does not require genuine human judgment.

This book is structured around five principles that together form the complete practice:

| | PRINCIPLE | WHAT IT MEANS |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | **Awareness** | Recognise that knowledge work is an Observe → Reason → Act loop — and that loop can be automated |
| **G** | **Grounding** | Design systems that survive the harshest constraints: cloud-first, phone-only, low bandwidth |
| **E** | **Elimination** | Strip away every task that does not require genuine human judgment |
| **N** | **Networked Autopilot** | Deploy continuous background agents that run systems without waiting for chat prompts |
| **T** | **Tethering** | Reinvest freed attention into physical reality — movement, presence, deep living |

Each part of this book corresponds to one of these five principles. The first three parts are the philosophy, the architecture, and the operating system. The fourth part is 140 days of stress-testing it in the real world — honest about what worked, what failed, and what surprised me.

One note before we begin: going agentic is not just a personal productivity decision. The elastic sector workers who read this book — software, science, research, creative work, education — have a structural obligation to multiply their output, not just reclaim their time. The full argument for why is developed in a companion essay, *The Transition Paradox*. The short version: if knowledge workers use AI to do the same output with less effort, the economic gap widens. If they use it to do fifty times more with the same input, the gap narrows. The individual incentive and the collective obligation point in exactly the same direction.
