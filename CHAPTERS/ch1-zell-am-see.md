# Chapter 1: The Night in Zell am See

## Deconstructing Knowledge Work

There are moments when you understand something so completely that your body reacts before your mind has time to process it.

January 2025. I was at one of my favourite places in the Austrian mountains — Zell am See. Sitting inside, reading a research paper on my reMarkable. The ReAct framework. Reasoning and Action. A paper describing how an AI system could reason through a problem, execute an action, observe the outcome, and reason again — in a loop, autonomously, indefinitely.

My heart rate spiked. I track my biometrics continuously with a Garmin, and the number looked like the start of a marathon. I needed to move. The thoughts in my head were too fast for a sitting body.

It was a moonlit night but freezing cold — fifteen degrees below zero. I didn't notice the temperature. I walked through the snow, talking out loud to hear myself think.

Here is what the paper described:

**Observe → Reason → Act → Observe again.**

That loop is the foundational engine of almost everything we call knowledge work. A consultant reads a brief, analyses it, writes a proposal, reads the client's response, adjusts. A developer reads a bug report, locates the issue, writes a fix, tests it, reads the result. A marketer reads campaign data, interprets the trends, writes new copy, reads the performance. Every role, every day, in every office in every developed country. The same loop, thousands of times, across millions of people.

If that loop could run on a server — without a human in it — what exactly is left for human effort?

I had no answer. I walked until the cold finally cut through. Then I went inside. I didn't sleep well.

---

Within twelve months, the answer was no longer theoretical.

Everything I had imagined on that walk was real. Not in a research paper — on live infrastructure, producing commercial results, while I managed it from my phone in the mountains of Patagonia.

I am not a developer. I cannot write production code from scratch. Everything described in this book is accessible to anyone with basic technical understanding. Which makes the implications far more radical than most people realise — and far more urgent than most people have begun to act on.

## What Knowledge Work Actually Is

Before we can understand what changes, we need to be precise about what knowledge work is.

The term gets used loosely. What it actually describes is a specific kind of labour: work where the primary input is human cognition rather than physical effort, and where the primary output is information, decisions, or creative artefacts rather than physical objects.

By this definition, knowledge work is not a niche category. It is the dominant form of employment in developed economies — software engineers, consultants, marketers, lawyers, researchers, analysts, writers, educators, managers, financial advisors. The majority of economic value in wealthy countries flows through this category.

And the core of all of it — beneath the industry-specific jargon and the different tools and the different contexts — is the same loop. Observe. Reason. Act.

A lawyer observes a contract, reasons about its implications, acts by writing a clause.
A consultant observes a problem, reasons about its causes, acts by writing a recommendation.
A software engineer observes a requirement, reasons about an implementation, acts by writing code.

The loop is not the interesting part. The interesting part is what the ReAct framework revealed: the loop does not inherently require a human.

It requires:
1. Access to the relevant information
2. A reasoning system capable of drawing meaningful conclusions
3. The ability to take action based on those conclusions
4. The ability to observe the results of that action

For decades, items 1, 3, and 4 were manageable — computers could store information, execute actions, and record results. Item 2 was the blocker. Reasoning — the genuine ability to understand context, draw conclusions, and make judgments — was what kept the human in the loop.

That blocker is gone.

Not partially gone. Not gone for some subset of tasks. Gone as a categorical constraint. The reasoning systems available today can handle the middle step of the loop across most of the domains that constitute knowledge work.

What this means is not that humans are obsolete. It means that the human's role in the loop has fundamentally changed. The human is no longer the only one who can reason. The human is now the one who decides what the loop is trying to achieve, and evaluates whether it has succeeded.

That is a profound shift — and it is almost entirely unaccounted for in how most organisations operate today.

## The Night's Real Question

Walking through the snow in Zell am See, I was not thinking about productivity. I was not thinking about efficiency. I was asking a much more fundamental question:

*If the loop can run without me — what should I be doing instead?*

That question does not have a single answer. The answer depends on who you are, what you care about, and what kind of life you want to live.

But the question itself is unavoidable. And the people who answer it deliberately — who design their lives around it, rather than stumbling into whatever default remains after the loop has been automated — will live very differently from those who don't.

This book is the account of one answer, lived across 140 days, in four countries, across two ultramarathons and three companies and one very useful security incident.

It is not the only answer. But it is a real one.
