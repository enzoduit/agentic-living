# Chapter 6: Stripping Zero-Judgment Tasks

## The End of Formatting & Menus

Before you build anything, you have to clear the space.

This is the step most people skip. They discover AI agents, they get excited about automation, they start building workflows — and they build them around a working week that still contains forty percent waste. The result is an agent that handles the waste faster. Slightly more efficient clutter.

Elimination first. Automation second. Always.

---

## The Audit

The Attention Audit is simple enough to explain in a paragraph and uncomfortable enough to actually do honestly.

For five consecutive working days, track every activity you do in fifteen-minute intervals. At the end of each day, go through the list and ask one question about each item:

*Did this require unique human judgment — genuine reasoning under uncertainty, a call that only I could make, creative or strategic thinking that the task actually deserved?*

If yes: keep it. This is where your attention belongs.

If no: it is a candidate for elimination, delegation, or automation.

| STEP | WHAT TO DO |
|---|---|
| **1 — Log** | Track every activity for 5 days in 15-minute intervals |
| **2 — Judgment Test** | For each item: did this require unique human judgment? |
| **3 — Categorise** | Label every No: formatting, filing, scheduling, navigation, summaries, copy-pasting |
| **4 — Prune** | Remove from human stack. Assign to agent or eliminate entirely |

What you will find is roughly what I found: somewhere between forty and sixty percent of the working week consists of tasks that require time and attention but not genuine thought. Not edge cases — the normal texture of professional life.

The categories that appear most consistently:

**Formatting.** Slides, documents, reports. The visual presentation of information that has already been created. Choosing fonts, aligning boxes, making things look consistent. None of this requires judgment. All of it requires time.

**Navigation.** Moving through software interfaces to get to the thing you actually need. The first twenty minutes of any session with a tool not built for your specific situation. Clicking through menus that were designed by someone who was not thinking about your workflow.

**Status updates.** Writing to tell people what is happening when you could be making things happen. Summarising meetings that should have been shorter. Reporting progress to people who could read the output directly.

**First drafts.** The initial version of anything that will be revised anyway. Emails, briefs, proposals. The agent does not produce a finished product here — but it produces a starting point that takes ten minutes to finalise instead of forty minutes to originate.

**Scheduling.** Finding times, sending invites, confirming attendance, rescheduling when things change. Pure logistics. Zero judgment involved.

Run the audit. Be honest. The number will be higher than you expect.

## The Three Rules

After running the audit, three structural changes produce the most consistent results. These are not one-time actions — they are permanent shifts in how you operate.

**Rule 1 — No manual files.**

The moment you create a file yourself — a spreadsheet, a presentation, a document — you build a door back to the old pattern. Files created by human hands invite human hands back to them. You format it. You align it. You decide whether the table header should be bold. None of these decisions matter. All of them consume you.

When the agent creates files, it creates them in formats that agents work with well: Markdown, structured data, databases. You cannot easily open these and start editing the way you would edit a Word document. That friction is intentional. It keeps you in the role of thinker and the agent in the role of executor.

I realised how far this had gone when I noticed, somewhere around month four, that I had barely opened Google Drive in weeks. Not because I had decided to stop using it — but because I had stopped creating the kind of files that needed to live there. The agent was generating the outputs. They were stored in the workspace, accessible to the agent, retrievable when needed. The Drive had been my filing system. The agent became the filing system. The Drive became irrelevant.

The exception: when a rough calculation in a spreadsheet makes your own thinking faster — use it. The line is whether you are enhancing your thought process or producing something for others. The first is yours. The second belongs to the agent.

**Rule 2 — Meetings require a decision output.**

A meeting without a decision is a status update that should have been a message. Before accepting any meeting invitation, ask: what decision will this produce? If the answer is unclear, the meeting should not happen. Replace it with an agent-generated summary of the relevant status, delivered to everyone who would have been in the room.

This is not about avoiding collaboration. Real collaboration — working through a hard problem together, making a call that requires multiple perspectives — is valuable and irreplaceable. What this rule eliminates is the meeting that exists because someone was not confident an email would be read. That meeting is a symptom of unclear ownership. Fix the ownership. Eliminate the meeting.

**Rule 3 — Raw output first. Formatted output never.**

Stop formatting things yourself. The thinking is yours. The presentation is the agent's job.

My morning routine is built around this. More than two hours, every morning, completely offline. reMarkable tablet, handwriting, no screen. The deep thinking happens there — without the temptation to polish, structure, or format as I go. The ideas arrive rough. That is the point.

When I am ready to move from thinking to action, I dictate. A voice note describing what I worked out and what needs to happen next. The agent takes the raw thinking, converts it into whatever output is required — a brief, a document, a set of instructions — and handles the presentation. I never see the formatting step. I skip from thinking to result.

This applies to everything. Instructions to the agent: voice notes or rough text, never polished. Briefings for clients: dictated first, formatted by the agent. Content: ideas spoken aloud, structure built by the system. The discipline is to resist the urge to tidy up your own output before handing it over. The mess is information. The agent can work with mess. It cannot work with the version you have already pre-edited to death.

## What Remains

After running the audit honestly and applying the three rules, what is left on your list?

The work that is genuinely yours.

Strategic decisions. Creative direction. Relationship-building. The judgment calls that require your specific experience, your values, your understanding of what matters in this particular situation. The thinking that cannot be described precisely enough in advance for a system to pursue it without you.

This is not a small category. It is, in fact, the most demanding category — the work that is hardest, most draining, and most rewarding. The work that makes the difference between an ordinary outcome and an extraordinary one.

The problem was never that this work was insufficient. The problem was that it was surrounded by everything else — the formatting, the filing, the status updates — and the everything else was consuming the attention that the real work deserved.

Remove the everything else. The real work expands to fill the space.
