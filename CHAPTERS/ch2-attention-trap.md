# Chapter 2: The Attention Trap

## Why Tech Books Expire & Seneca Was Right

Most writing about technology expires within two years.

Not because the facts become wrong — often they remain accurate. But because technology writing is almost always too close to the surface. It describes what is happening without asking what it means. It names the tool without examining the pattern the tool is exposing. And when the tool changes — which it always does — the writing has nothing left to say.

There is a different kind of writing that does not expire. It asks what is always true, and uses the current moment as evidence.

Seneca wrote to his friend Lucilius: *"It matters not how many books you have, but how good they are."* He was addressing a library. But the principle has a broader application: it matters not how many things happen, but how many of them reveal something that was already true before they arrived.

The question worth asking about AI agents is not: what can they do? That changes every six months. The question worth asking is: what does it reveal about us — about how we spend our time, where our attention goes, and what we have been tolerating as normal?

The answer to that question has not changed in two thousand years.

## The Misallocation

Seneca wrote in the first century. He observed something that is still true today, in every open-plan office, on every Zoom call, in every project management tool with notifications turned on:

*"It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a great deal of it."*

He was not describing distraction in the social media sense. He was describing a more fundamental problem: the systematic application of human attention to things that do not deserve it. The performance of productivity instead of its substance. The activity that fills the hours without filling the life.

Early in my career as a technology consultant, a significant part of every week went to creating presentations. Not thinking about what the slides should say — that part I enjoyed. The other part: formatting them. Choosing fonts. Aligning boxes. Making things look consistent across fifty pages. None of that required judgment. All of it required time.

Or consider the hours spent navigating software not built for your specific situation — spending the first twenty minutes of every session figuring out how to make it work this particular way, for this particular need. Or the meetings that exist because someone was uncertain whether an email would be read, so they called a meeting instead. Or the status update that gets written because someone needs to demonstrate that work is happening, not because the information has anywhere useful to go.

This is not a description of bad workers. This is a description of how work is structured. And the structure has been in place for so long that most people no longer see it as a choice — they see it as the texture of professional life.

The texture is what AI agents are replacing. And what is being returned in its place is something rarer: time that belongs to you.

## Where Judgment Actually Lives

Here is the useful question: of everything you did last week, how much of it genuinely required your specific judgment?

Not your presence. Not your time. Your judgment — the ability to assess a situation that has not been seen before, make a call under uncertainty, connect something from one domain to something in another, decide what matters and what doesn't.

I have run this exercise with many people. The honest answer is almost always the same: somewhere between five and twenty percent of the week. The rest — the formatting, the filing, the summarising, the navigating, the scheduling, the first draft that will be revised anyway — required time and attention, but not judgment.

**Agentic Living** is the deliberate practice of protecting that five to twenty percent.

Not by working less. By ensuring that the things that consume your time without requiring your judgment are handled by systems that do not have judgment to offer — and don't need it.

The goal is not efficiency. Not productivity. Not even freedom — though freedom tends to follow. The goal is: more of your actual self, applied to the things that actually require you.

## The Obligation

One more thing before the field manual begins — and it is not about personal productivity.

If you work in software, research, media, education, creative work, or any field where the output is primarily digital — you are in what economists call an elastic sector. These sectors have a specific property: when the cost of producing something drops, total demand expands rather than shrinks. Make software development ten times cheaper and the world does not need ten times fewer developers. It needs ten times more software. The appetite for these things has no natural ceiling.

This is different from sectors where demand is fixed by physical reality. You cannot eat ten times more bread just because bread becomes cheaper. When AI automates baking, jobs disappear and demand does not fill the gap. Those workers face a harder transition.

The full argument for why this creates a structural economic gap — and why elastic sector workers have an obligation to multiply output rather than just save time — is developed in a companion essay called *The Transition Paradox*. The short version: the gap between how fast digital displacement happens and how slowly physical infrastructure gets built creates a dangerous window. The only force that closes it is elastic sector workers doing fifty times more with the same input.

The individual incentive and the collective obligation point in exactly the same direction. This book is the practical guide to acting on both.
